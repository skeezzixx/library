    Editing dialogue¶

    Part II · Editing the scene — chapter 1 of 1 · ↑ Film editing · Deep dive: the generated dialogue scene →

    A dialogue scene is the editor's most common job and the least forgiving, because nothing in it hides the cut. Action covers a cut with motion; a montage announces its cuts as the point. Two people talking on a floor offer neither. Every cut in a dialogue scene is a decision about attention that the audience feels without seeing, and the craft comes down to one reframing: the words are already recorded — what the edit decides is whose face we are looking at while they are said.

    The chapter builds that craft in the order an editor meets it: what the scene is really about, what footage the editor is handed, the shape of a scene over its length, where inside a line the cut lands, how a performance is assembled from takes, rhythm, scenes with more than two people, the sound that makes it all read — and then one short exchange cut three ways, so the decisions can be seen side by side. Rules — things editors commonly do, at least within one tradition — are marked § throughout and gathered at the end.

    The scene is the listening, not the lines¶

    Screenwriters give a dialogue scene its words; the editor gives it its witness. A line spoken on screen has two available images — the face producing it and the face receiving it — and the cut chooses between them, line by line, sometimes word by word. The choice looks trivial and is the whole scene. Watch the speaker and the line is information. Watch the listener and the line is an event: we see it land, or fail to, and the audience reads the relationship off the reaction instead of being told it.

    This is why the reaction shot, not the speaking shot, is the modern editor's default currency. Cut to the listener when the reaction is the story. It is the oldest piece of cutting-room wisdom, stated in some form in nearly every editor's memoir, because it inverts the novice's instinct — which is to cut to whoever is talking, as a television line-cut does. The corollary matters as much: Stay on the speaker when the line changes the speaker. A confession, a lie being constructed as it's spoken, a line that costs something to say — those belong on the face that pays. The reaction shot is a tool, not a policy.

    Two consequences follow for how you watch dailies. First, you are hunting listening as much as speaking: the moment an actor hears the line and something moves behind the eyes is footage, and in a good take it often happens off the line, in the seconds before or after, where a lazy assembly would have trimmed. Second, the edit tells the audience whom to believe. Cut to A's reaction on B's claim and the claim is now in doubt; hold on B and it stands. Nothing in the script says which. The cut does.

    Take the scene this chapter ends with. Two survivors on the floor of a looted store, and a Twinkie between them; the first line is "It's still soft." Whom do we watch — the woman saying it, reverent, or the one who has been watching the door and now, for the first time, looks down? Everything about the scene's tone is decided in that choice, before a single word has changed.

    Coverage: what the editor is given¶

    Editors don't shoot; they inherit. What they inherit is coverage — the set of angles a director shot so that the scene can be assembled in more than one way — and the vocabulary of coverage is the vocabulary of the dialogue cut:

    • The master (for two people, usually a two-shot): both characters in frame, the whole scene played through once. It establishes the geography and is the shot you can always return to.
    • The over-the-shoulder pair (OTS): each character framed past the near shoulder of the other. The standard way to cover two people facing each other; it keeps both bodies in every shot.
    • Singles: one character alone. A clean single has nothing of the other person in frame; a dirty single lets an edge of shoulder or hair intrude, which keeps the scene's geometry alive at the cost of a slightly less pure face.
    • Inserts (a detail — hands, an object) and cutaways (something in the scene but outside the exchange — a clock, a door, a third person).

    All of it obeys one geometric contract, the axis of action: an imaginary line drawn between the two characters. Cameras stay on one side of it, so that A always looks screen-right and B always looks screen-left, and the two singles cut together as a conversation instead of as two people addressing the same wall. Keep the line: screen-left looks right, screen-right looks left, in every shot of the exchange (continuity editing). Cross it and the characters appear to swap places; there are legal ways across — a neutral shot on the line, a move shown on screen, a cutaway — and the grammar chapter in Part I takes them up. Directors who plan coverage well tend to think in Daniel Arijon's triangle: three camera positions on one side of the line — the two singles and the master's apex — from which any dialogue scene can be built.1

    Two things the editor is owed and doesn't always get. Overlap: each angle should carry the whole exchange, or at least the lines on either side of any planned cut, because a cut needs a choice of frames on both sides. And the tail: the seconds after "cut" is called, when actors drop the pose and breathe — surprisingly often the best reaction in the scene lives there. Roll early and hold late: the frames before and after the line are where the cuts live. A director who calls cut on the last syllable has thrown away the editor's material. And the master exists so that the singles can be understood: Establish the geography before you break it. A scene that opens in tight singles and never shows the room is asking the audience to build a map they were never given.

    The scene at the end of this chapter is staged frontally — both women on the floor facing the camera, not each other — and that staging deletes half the vocabulary above. There is no over-the-shoulder, because there is no shoulder between them and the lens. Coverage collapses to the two-shot, two frontal singles, and inserts, and the eyelines run down to the object on the floor between them and back to the door behind. Which is a choice, not a limitation: the flat, symmetrical two-shot is the native frame of deadpan comedy — Ozu's tatami-level frontality and Wes Anderson's centred symmetries are its two famous poles — and it pushes the interesting decision, two-shot or single, to the front.

    The shape of a scene: the funnel¶

    Scenes have a shape over their length, and the commonest good shape is a funnel. Open wide; tighten as the stakes rise; the closest framing arrives at the scene's turning point; and if the scene releases, widen again as it does. The reason is economic, not aesthetic: a close-up is the strongest thing a cut can offer, and its strength comes from contrast with what preceded it. Never go tighter than the moment has earned. A scene that opens in close-ups has nowhere to go — a mistake so common in first films that editors have a phrase for it: you spent the close-up.

    Width, conversely, is where you go to release — and to re-anchor. Go wide on a physical event, a power shift, or a new presence. Someone stands; the balance of the argument tips; a third person enters; the audience needs the room again, and a cut to the master gives it while also marking the beat. The cut is the exclamation mark.

    Then there is the option the funnel hides: not cutting. A held two-shot in a confrontation puts two performances in one frame and makes the audience do the cutting with their eyes — which is more work and more involvement. Richard Linklater's Before Sunset runs its walking conversations in takes of minutes; Noah Baumbach's Marriage Story holds its central argument in long two-shots and only fragments into singles as the argument does, so the cutting rate tracks the collapse.2 The inverse deliberate choice is Michael Mann's diner scene in Heat: two men who are the film's whole subject, covered in singles and over-the-shoulders, so that they share a table and never share a frontal frame — the separation is the meaning.3 Both choices are about currency. The longer you don't cut, the more the first cut is worth. Withheld cuts accumulate.

    Where in the line to cut¶

    Novices cut on the line — the picture changes when the speaker changes — and the result is a tennis match. The craft cuts on the thought. Cut on the thought, not the line. The unit is not the sentence but the change: the instant the listener understands, the instant the speaker decides, the instant a lie becomes visible. That instant is rarely at a line boundary. It is often a beat before the answer (we go to B as she decides to speak, so we watch the decision and then hear it) or in the middle of a sentence (the picture leaves the speaker when the words stop being what matters).

    The tool for this is the split edit, in which sound and picture cut at different moments. In a J-cut, the next shot's sound arrives before its picture: we hear B begin to answer while still on A's face, and the picture follows. In an L-cut, the current shot's sound continues under the next picture: B keeps talking while we have already gone to A's reaction. The letters are the shapes the clips make on a timeline. Lead the sound when the listener matters; trail it when the speaker does. Split edits are why cut dialogue feels continuous: the ear is never handed a hard edge at the same instant as the eye, so neither notices the other's seam.

    Walter Murch adds a physiological cue. People blink at the ends of thoughts, and he found that an audience's attention resets at the same moments; a cut placed on the listener's or the speaker's blink lands in the gap and disappears.4 When in doubt, cut on the blink. The breath is the same signal from the other end of the line: the inhale before speech is where a J-cut wants to start.

    Then there is air — the pauses. Every first assembly is too long, and mostly because of air: the seconds of recording between lines that are simply the set waiting. First cuts are too long — tighten the air before you tighten the shots. But tightening isn't a policy either, because in a good scene one pause is the scene: the silence before "…Yes." Keep the pause that is the scene; cut the pauses that are only recording. Telling them apart is the fine-cut skill, and the honest test is whether something is happening on someone's face during the pause. If it is, the pause is a line.

    Overlapping dialogue is the other extreme. Howard Hawks had the actors of His Girl Friday start lines on each other's cues so the exchange runs faster than a person can speak; Robert Altman miked whole rooms and let conversations collide. Both are shooting decisions the editor then has to honour — overlaps can't be cut apart cleanly, so the coverage has to hold them in two-shots. For the frame-level decision Dmytryk's advice still holds after forty years: when undecided about the exact frame, cut long rather than short, because a few extra frames can be trimmed on the next pass and a cut that's too tight reads as a stumble.5

    Building the performance from takes¶

    The performance the audience sees rarely existed on set. It is assembled: line 3 from take 6 because the reading was better, the reaction to line 4 from take 2 because that was the one where the actor actually heard it, the sound of line 5 from take 9 laid under the picture of take 6 because the picture was right and the reading wasn't. Steal the performance where it lives — sound from one take, the face from another. This is legitimate, universal, and invisible if the split edits are done well; it is also why editors get called the film's last writer.

    The composite performance forces a hierarchy of continuities. A glass moves between takes; a hand is up in one angle and down in the other; the cigarette is a different length. The audience notices those less than editors fear, and far less than a dead reaction. Continuity of emotion beats continuity of props. Dmytryk's version — cut for proper values rather than proper matches — is the sixth of his seven rules. When a mismatch is bad enough to notice, the fix is usually a cutaway or an insert, which resets the audience's memory of the position.

    Take selection has its own trap. The best line reading is not always the best line for the scene: a perfect, complete performance of line 5 may leave nothing for the reaction on line 6, or be pitched for a different scene than the one the cut is building. Choose the take for the scene, not the take for the line. David Fincher's method is the extreme statement of this. For the opening of The Social Network — a two-person argument in a bar, nine pages of Sorkin — he shot ninety-nine takes, not because the actors kept failing but so that his editors, Kirk Baxter and Angus Wall, could build the scene's rhythm from a database of near-identical readings, choosing per line for the pace of the whole.6 The scene cuts fast, often on every line and sometimes inside one, and never reads as tennis, because every cut is a change in who is winning.

    Rhythm in dialogue¶

    Dialogue editing has a rhythm problem that action editing doesn't: the material is nearly metrical. Two people trading lines of similar length in similar singles produce a beat, and a beat in the cutting reads as mechanical. Don't metronome — vary shot length; ABAB reads as tennis. The remedies are all asymmetries: hold one single across two of the other person's lines; go to the two-shot for a stretch; land an insert; let one reaction run long and cut the next one short.

    Cutting out of a shot is as expressive as cutting into it. Leave a reaction before it finishes when the next line is the answer. The audience's pull forward comes from being denied the end of something, and a scene in which every reaction is allowed to complete has no forward pull at all. Conversely, the held single — staying on a face after the line has landed, into the silence — is the most powerful single instrument in the dialogue cut, and it works in exact proportion to how rarely it's used.

    For a musician the analogy is exact enough to be useful and wrong enough to be a trap. Shot durations are note values; the cut is the attack; the held shot is a sustain; the two-shot is a pedal under the singles. But the scene's meter is not a grid. The rhythm Karen Pearlman describes — physical (movement in the frame), emotional (the arc of feeling), and event rhythm (the story's beats) — is pulsed but not metric, and dialogue cut on a grid is the equivalent of quantising a rubato.7 Part IV makes the point again about score: even when there is a beat in the room, you cut around it, not on it.

    Two live-action extremes bracket the question. The Sorkin–Schlamme walk-and-talk of The West Wing is dialogue with no cuts: a Steadicam follows two people through corridors and the rhythm comes entirely from their walking and from who joins and leaves — the camera does the editor's work. The four-camera sitcom is its opposite: a line-cut switched during the performance, on every line, which is the metronome adopted as a house style and is why sitcoms feel like sitcoms.

    Three or more¶

    Add a third person and coverage stops being a pair of singles with a line between them; it becomes a network of eyelines, and the audience must always know who is looking at whom. The master becomes more important, not less. The master is home — when the audience is lost, go back to the wide. And every change of arrangement — someone stands, moves, leaves the table — needs a wide shot soon after it, or the singles that follow will float. Re-establish whenever the geography changes.

    Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men is the classic on shaping a group scene across a whole film. Lumet describes moving over the film's length from wider lenses shot at eye level and above to longer lenses shot from below — so that the room appears to shrink, the ceiling comes into frame, and the faces crowd — a funnel applied at feature length, with twelve eyelines to keep straight throughout.8 The contemporary example is Succession, whose handheld, multi-camera coverage — with the snap zooms left in — was made precisely so that group scenes could be cut on any face at any moment: the editors are handed every reaction to every line, and the show's nervous rhythm comes from using them.

    Sound in the dialogue cut¶

    The dialogue track is the spine of the picture cut, and much of what reads as picture craft is really sound craft. Two matters belong here; the rest is Part IV's.

    Room tone — the recorded silence of the space — is what fills the gaps between spliced lines so that the cuts in the audio don't read as drop-outs; without it, the composite performance sounds composite. And ADR — dialogue re-recorded in a studio and laid under the picture — is placed where the mouth can't contradict it: on the listener's reaction, on the back of a head, in the wide. The animation deep dive that follows makes the same move from the other direction — staging dialogue so the mouth needn't be seen at all — and it is the same trick.

    One more thing decided in the picture cut: Music enters on the turn, never under the setup. A cue that begins under a scene's opening lines tells the audience how to feel before anything has happened; a cue that arrives on the line where the scene changes confirms what they already felt. In the example below, if there is music at all, it starts on the last word.

    Worked example: one exchange, three cuts¶

    The scene: a zombie apocalypse. Two women have just found shelter in an abandoned convenience store, and — on the floor between them, in its wrapper, perfectly preserved — a Twinkie. The tone is dark comedy. A (screen left: shaved head, tank top, dirt to the elbows) is carefree and apparently oblivious to what's outside; B (screen right: bicycle helmet, shoulder pads over a pink hoodie) is practical and reactive. The master is a flat frontal two-shot — both on the tile with their backs to the shelving, the glass door behind and between them, the dead drifting past in daylight. The names are placeholders; so are the lines, which are scratch dialogue written to give the cuts something to cut:

    1. A: It's still soft.
    2. B: Don't touch it.
    3. A: They said these things last forever. I never believed them.
    4. B: Nobody said that. That was a joke people made.
    5. A: Well. Look who's laughing.
    6. B: There are eleven of them outside.
    7. A: Twelve. (beat) Do you want half?
    8. B: (the door; the Twinkie) …Yes.

    Eight lines, one master, two frontal singles, one insert, and the same recorded performances every time. Only the cutting changes.

    Cut A — by the book. Coverage cut the way it was shot: master to establish, singles on the speaker, an insert on the reach, back to the master to close.

    CUT A · by the book                                     ~38 s · 9 cuts
 0:00  MASTER (two-shot)   A  It's still soft.                     4 s
 0:04  SINGLE B            B  Don't touch it.                      3 s
 0:07  SINGLE A            A  They said these things last…         5 s
 0:12  SINGLE B            B  Nobody said that. That was…          4 s
 0:16  SINGLE A            A  Well. Look who's laughing.           3 s
 0:19  INSERT (Twinkie)    —  A's hand enters frame                2 s
 0:21  SINGLE B            B  There are eleven of them outside.    3 s
 0:24  SINGLE A            A  Twelve. (beat) Do you want half?     5 s
 0:29  SINGLE B            B  door · Twinkie · …Yes.               5 s
 0:34  MASTER              —  she takes it                         4 s

    It works. Every line is clear, the geography is never in doubt, the joke lands. It is also the tennis match: nine cuts on nine speaker changes, and the audience is told, each time, where to look — which means they never choose to look, and choosing is where comedy lives. The door, the scene's third performer, exists only in the master, so the threat is absent for thirty seconds in the middle. This is the version a line-cut would produce, and the version most first assemblies are.

    Cut B — listener-led. The cut goes to whoever is changed by the line, and three lines play off screen.

    CUT B · listener-led                                    ~38 s · 6 cuts
 0:00  MASTER              A  It's still soft.   (B is watching the door)      5 s
 0:05  SINGLE A            B (off)  Don't touch it.   — A doesn't look up —
                           A  They said these things last forever…            8 s
 0:13  SINGLE B   J-cut    on "believed them" we are already on B, who
                           decides to answer.   B  Nobody said that…
                           — her eyes go to the door mid-line —                5 s
 0:18  SINGLE A            A  Well. Look who's laughing.   (reaching)          3 s
 0:21  INSERT (Twinkie)    the hand closes on it.
                           B (off)  There are eleven of them outside.          4 s
 0:25  SINGLE B            A (off)  Twelve.   — B turns her head: the first
                           time in the scene she looks at A, not the door.
                           A (off)  Do you want half?
                           B  door · Twinkie · …Yes.                           9 s
 0:34  MASTER              the wrapper opens                                   4 s

    Six cuts instead of nine, and the scene is now about something: B's discipline breaking. "Don't touch it" is heard, not seen, and what we watch is that it has no effect; "eleven" is spoken over the hand taking the Twinkie anyway; "Twelve" is the punchline and we don't see A say it — we see B hear it, and turn, and for the first time look at the person she has been sharing a floor with. The nine-second hold at 0:25 is the scene's only long shot and it carries the three glances that are the joke. A is a force of nature seen through B's eyes, which is what the premise asked for.

    Cut C — the withheld two-shot. No cut at all until the last word.

    CUT C · the withheld two-shot                           ~38 s · 1 cut
 0:00  MASTER              lines 1–7 play in the two-shot, uncut.
                           The dead drift across the glass behind them;
                           on "eleven" one of them stops and looks in.      34 s
 0:34  SINGLE B            …Yes.                                             4 s

    The deadpan register's native cut. For thirty-four seconds the audience does the cutting themselves — eye to A, eye to B, eye to the Twinkie, eye to the door — and the frame's flat symmetry keeps all four available at once; the zombie who stops is a gag that only exists in the wide. Then the single cut of the scene spends the whole accumulated currency on B's face. This is the version in which the frame is the joke, and it is the one that most trusts the audience — and the performances, which have nowhere to hide for half a minute. It is also, as the deep dive that follows explains, the most expensive of the three to generate: thirty-four seconds of two independent performers in one frame is the hardest thing a video model is asked for, and the "cheap" Cut A is nearly free. In generated film the economics of the long take invert, and the listener-led cut is the natural one.

    Which cut is right depends on what the scene is for in its film — a question the edit can ask but the story has to answer. What the three show together is that the same eight lines and the same four angles produce three different scenes, and the difference is entirely in whose face the audience is looking at, and for how long.

    For the synthetic cut¶

    Everything above assumed a camera. The deep dive that follows takes the same craft into the children's cartoon, where the shots are generated five seconds at a time and only one thing in the frame can move — and finds that a budget-constrained tradition, television animation, already wrote the same rules for the same reasons: the generated dialogue scene.

    The rules¶

    • Cut to the listener when the reaction is the story.
    • Stay on the speaker when the line changes the speaker.
    • Keep the line: screen-left looks right, screen-right looks left, in every shot of the exchange (continuity editing).
    • Roll early and hold late: the frames before and after the line are where the cuts live.
    • Establish the geography before you break it.
    • Never go tighter than the moment has earned.
    • Go wide on a physical event, a power shift, or a new presence.
    • The longer you don't cut, the more the first cut is worth.
    • Cut on the thought, not the line.
    • Lead the sound when the listener matters; trail it when the speaker does.
    • When in doubt, cut on the blink.
    • First cuts are too long — tighten the air before you tighten the shots.
    • Keep the pause that is the scene; cut the pauses that are only recording.
    • Steal the performance where it lives — sound from one take, the face from another.
    • Continuity of emotion beats continuity of props.
    • Choose the take for the scene, not the take for the line.
    • Don't metronome — vary shot length; ABAB reads as tennis.
    • Leave a reaction before it finishes when the next line is the answer.
    • The master is home — when the audience is lost, go back to the wide.
    • Re-establish whenever the geography changes.
    • Music enters on the turn, never under the setup.
