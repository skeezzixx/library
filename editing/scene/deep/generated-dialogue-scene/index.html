    The generated dialogue scene¶

    Part II · Editing the scene — deep dive · ← Editing dialogue · The rulebook →

    The dialogue chapter assumed a camera, actors, and coverage. The children's cartoon has none of them. Its shots are generated one at a time — character sheets and per-character LoRAs from Krea 2, a pair of keyframes per shot, and WAN 2.2 filling the five seconds between them — under three constraints live action never meets: a generation stays coherent when one region of the frame moves, the model is unreliable at mouths, and every shot costs a generation plus its re-rolls. That sounds like a different craft. It isn't. Television animation worked under the same three constraints for sixty years — for money rather than for models — and the grammar it settled on is the dialogue chapter's grammar with the budget made explicit. This page walks the chapter's sections again with the constraints switched on, and ends by running the chapter's three cuts through them.

    Voice first¶

    The order of operations changes before the first shot exists. Live action records dialogue while shooting the picture; animation records it first and animates to it, and the industry regards the reverse — building motion and post-syncing a voice onto it — as almost always a mistake, partly because delivery legitimately changes what the shot should be: where the actor breathes, pauses, and speeds up decides which expression the keyframes need.1 Record the voice first; the waveform is the shot's duration (animation). In a five-second-clip pipeline the rule is sharper than in a studio: a line's exact in and out timecode, read off the waveform in the animatic, is the clip's length. Guess it instead and the generation cuts the line off (regenerate) or runs long (compute wasted, an awkward trim). The animatic — a timed cut of stills against the recorded voice — is therefore the first edit and the budget: every locked cut in it becomes a shot row, a keyframe spec, and a duration. The chapter's "cut on the thought" survives intact; it just gets decided with a scratch track and stills, weeks before anything moves.

    The coverage grammar under one moving region¶

    The chapter's coverage — master, singles, inserts, cutaways — is television animation's coverage too, with a difference in why. Budget animation settled on a two-shot to establish (both characters, waist up, minimal individual animation), shot/reverse-shot singles to carry the exchange (each side animated independently, held, and reused), and the reaction cutaway — the listener's response without re-animating the speaker at all. Limited animation pushed it further: mouths over static bodies, or, at the Evangelion extreme, characters framed from behind or in silhouette while talking so that no mouth is on camera.2 Two-shot establishes, singles carry, reaction cutaways punctuate (TV animation).

    The AI-video constraint that makes this mandatory rather than merely thrifty: a five-second generation holds together far more often when only one region of the frame is in motion. Two characters moving independently is where drift is worst, and it is also where two character LoRAs interfere with each other. Never require two independently moving characters in one generation (AI video). So the dialogue scene is planned as (1) an establishing two-shot at idle-level motion — one held generation, or a still extended in the edit; (2) a single per line, each its own low-motion generation with one face and torso working; (3) reaction cutaways on near-static listening poses — the cheapest, most coherent shot type available, and the free fix when a main-action generation doesn't land.

    Read the chapter's three cuts through this. Cut A (by the book) is nine generations of one moving thing each — cheap and safe. Cut B (listener-led) is six, three of them listening shots: the cheapest shots in the pipeline carrying the scene's meaning, and the version the grammar actively prefers. Cut C (the withheld two-shot) is thirty-four seconds of two independent performers in one frame: seven chained clips, drift compounding from each to the next, two LoRAs fighting, and no cut to reset any of it. In live action it is the cheapest to shoot; here it is the one shot the pipeline is worst at. The funnel becomes a budget curve, and the long take goes from the trusting choice to the extravagant one.

    Staging around the mouth¶

    The chapter mentioned ADR placed where the mouth can't contradict it. Animation built a whole toolkit around the same avoidance, because mouths were expensive: (1) voice-off — the speaker is in the geography but out of frame; (2) the long shot — at distance only gross body motion must read; (3) the reaction shot — the listener's expression while the line plays; (4) ¾-away, profile, behind-the-shoulder, or obscured-mouth framing — a hand near the face, a silhouette; the classic anime dialogue staging; (5) a still under a camera pan. Dialogue-heavy limited animation backed the sparseness with strong sound design, so economy never read as cheap.

    In this pipeline the five aren't fallbacks; they are the primary strategy, for two reasons that both have to do with the model. First, AI lip-sync on a subdued, naturalistic stylised face is unproven: the mid-2026 options — WAN 2.2's own S2V and Animate modes, Hedra, LatentSync, the old Wav2Lip baseline — have no benchmark on this register, and marketing claims of "cartoon support" mean nothing until a trial pass runs in the project's actual character style.3 Second, and worse, WAN 2.2 generates unwanted mouth movement in image-to-video even when the prompt says "no talking, mouth closed" — an open issue with no fix.4 Default every line to an off-mouth staging; the front-facing, lip-synced close-up is the rationed exception (limited animation, AI video). And its mirror: Review every silent listening shot for mouth motion — a moving mouth is not evidence of sync (WAN 2.2). Each of the five stagings is a free decision at the animatic stage and a costly regeneration if discovered in review — so it is made in the animatic.

    The chapter's listener-led cut turns out to be the native cut here for a second reason: in Cut B, three of the eight lines play off screen, over the face of someone not speaking — and a listening face is exactly the shot this pipeline is best at.

    How long a shot lives, and how a hold stays alive¶

    The chapter said first cuts are too long and dialogue must not metronome. For ages five to eight the cutting rate has a floor as well, and it is a measured one. Lillard and Peterson randomised four-year-olds to nine minutes of a fast-paced cartoon (scene changes about every eleven seconds), a slow one (about every thirty-four), or drawing; the fast-paced group scored significantly worse immediately afterwards on an executive-function battery, controlling for baseline attention and television exposure, and a matched follow-up replicated it.5 Anderson and colleagues found that three and a half minutes of fast- versus slow-edited film measurably changed continuous-attention performance in four-to-seven-year-olds.6 Pacing effects appear after one brief exposure. For scale: Hollywood's average shot length fell from eight-to-ten seconds in the 1960s to three-to-four by 2005; the shows in this project's register sit at roughly seven to fifteen seconds a shot, and the over-stimulation anti-pattern lives at one to three.7 Hold shots for 8–15 seconds; never live under 4 (children's TV, ages 5–8). This is the rare case where developmental evidence and production economics point the same way: a ten-second shot is two chained generations or one generation plus a held frame, while rapid intercutting multiplies the generation count and costs the audience.

    A long hold on a generated face raises the chapter's "held single" in a new form: how does a held pose stay alive? Animation's answer is that a hold is never static — the moving hold — in one of two shapes: an overshoot (past the target pose, then a recovery into it) or a long ease-in (a slow continuous settle toward the key), with hair, cloth, and above all eyes and blinks keeping it breathing. A duplicated frame reads as dead, not restrained. Traditional limited animation modulated this with the frame schedule — twos and threes, breaking to ones for emphasis. WAN interpolates continuously, so the effect has to come from the keyframe pair and the prompt, and the pipeline rule is blunt: A held pose is a moving hold, never a freeze — never give the model two identical frames (animation, AI video). Zero pose delta gives the model no motion to find; you get a literal freeze or undirected noise. Make the last frame a small settle past the first, describe the settle in the motion prompt, and treat blinks and eye-darts as their own short beats — sub-second eye business is the first thing lost when a shot is labelled "static" by both the planner and the model, and it is most of what makes a subdued face feel alive. Cut B's nine-second hold on B is, in this pipeline, two chained clips whose keyframes carry three eyelines — door, Twinkie, A — with a settle between each.

    Cut, don't move; and where the camera sits¶

    The chapter's funnel assumed the freedom to reframe. Storyboarding for budget television gives two clean triggers and no others: cut when the new framing is dramatically closer or farther (a same-size re-angle reads as a jump cut — go bigger, smaller, or don't cut), and move the camera only to preserve continuous action or a spatial relationship on screen — following a character, or re-crossing the line safely by showing the reposition happen. Every shot is labelled full-, minimal-, or no-movement at the boarding stage; small connective moves are boarded as one panel framed at the move's end, because the move is connective tissue, not an event.8 Cut when the framing size changes; move the camera only to keep a spatial relationship on screen (TV storyboarding). The pipeline doubles the justification: camera motion is where generation is least reliable — geometry warps and backgrounds drift in ways a locked shot never shows — and a camera move plus character motion in one clip splits the model's motion budget. A move, when the story requires one, is one simple short move, flagged as a high-risk row with extra candidates budgeted. Cuts, meanwhile, are free drift-resets that chaining is not — the chapter's "the cut is the seam" in its most literal form.

    Where the camera sits is a rule too. Eye level is cinema's neutral; for a child protagonist, neutral is the child's eye line, not an adult observer's, and that baseline is what makes scenes belong to the child instead of being watched from above. The two departures carry meaning and are spent deliberately — low angle for agency, high for smallness or awe — plus the child's-POV shot looking up at adults who loom and crop at the top of the frame.9 Neutral is the child's eye height; low and high angles are spent as beats (children's TV). Each character's standing and seated eye height goes in the continuity bible so that every shot prompt inherits it.

    Where the generations go¶

    The chapter's economy of attention — the close-up as currency — becomes a ledger. Hilda's animators sync micro-expression to the vocal delivery rather than to a frame quota, and its director describes the medium as wholly deliberate: "there are no found moments, only intentional actions." Song of the Sea underplays most of its cast to spend performance on a few; Ronja covers rig limits with pacing and shot choice.10 Naturalistic children's television puts its craft on the face because that is where the viewer's gaze concentrates, and the port to generation is direct: Spend the generations where the face is (naturalistic kids' TV). Face-on-camera dialogue shots get the careful keyframes, the extra candidates, and the pickiest review — which is also exactly where lip-sync risk lives; establishing shots, travel, and environment beats run with restrained motion and fewer retries, and mostly need to not-fail.

    Two register facts round out the budget. Dialogue is sparser than adult television — one heuristic puts an eleven-minute episode at seventy to ninety lines, from a single unverified source — with the simplest adequate words, consistent within an episode, key information deliberately repeated, and each character's lines recognisably theirs; the sparseness is a gift, since beats carried physically need no mouth at all.11 And music is punctuation: the documented contrast is constant-loudness scoring with no dynamics against Bluey's sparse, intermittent cues in short sharp bursts on specific beats, played on a deliberately familiar hand-played palette. The bed is silence and ambience. Music is punctuation, not wallpaper (children's TV). The chapter's "music enters on the turn" is the same rule at scene scale.

    The Twinkie scene, generated¶

    The convenience-store scene from the dialogue chapter isn't the cartoon — it is photoreal and adult — but it is generated, and the three constraints are the same family. Run it through this page. Voice first, so the eight lines' timecodes set eight clip lengths. The establishing two-shot as one held generation at idle motion, with the dead drifting past the glass as the one moving region and the women still. Singles as low-motion clips with one face working; the reaction cutaways doing the comedy; and the frontal staging conveniently putting both faces at a three-quarter-down eyeline toward the Twinkie for most of the scene, which is off-mouth staging pattern four without trying. Cut B is the version that costs least and says most. Cut C — the deadpan long take — becomes the one shot to plan for if the frame is the joke: a single generation can't hold it, so it would be chained from a locked plate with the two performers pinned and only the background walkers moving, cut to a single on the last word. The mechanics of that plate live in the dance subject's rendering and pipeline chapters; the editing of it is Part VII of this one.

    The rules¶

    • Record the voice first; the waveform is the shot's duration (animation).
    • Two-shot establishes, singles carry, reaction cutaways punctuate (TV animation).
    • Never require two independently moving characters in one generation (AI video).
    • Default every line to an off-mouth staging; the front-facing, lip-synced close-up is the rationed exception (limited animation, AI video).
    • Review every silent listening shot for mouth motion — a moving mouth is not evidence of sync (WAN 2.2).
    • Hold shots for 8–15 seconds; never live under 4 (children's TV, ages 5–8).
    • A held pose is a moving hold, never a freeze — never give the model two identical frames (animation, AI video).
    • Cut when the framing size changes; move the camera only to keep a spatial relationship on screen (TV storyboarding).
    • Neutral is the child's eye height; low and high angles are spent as beats (children's TV).
    • Spend the generations where the face is (naturalistic kids' TV).
    • Music is punctuation, not wallpaper (children's TV).
