    Locomotion without a choreographer¶

    Every chapter in this part has had an author of motion somewhere in the loop. Cunningham's coins sequenced units he had made; Brown's rules ordered gestures she had chosen; the learned archives of the computational-choreography chapter proposed from a corpus a human had danced. This chapter drops the author. Its subject is motion that nobody writes — that falls out of a body, a physics, an objective, a rule — and the question of what such motion is good for when the body in question is a humanoid, and when it's something stranger.

    Three reasons a dance library cares. Synthetic dancers will increasingly move by these methods, because capture and keyframing don't scale and priors only reproduce what was recorded. Non-human performers are already on stage — a quadruped robot with its own choreography tool, an industrial arm in a duet, wind-driven linkages on a beach. And it is the purest case of the commons' deepest motif: emergence, watched in a body.

    Seven places motion comes from¶

    Each source below has a canonical exemplar and a lesson that travels. They aren't exclusive; modern systems stack them.

    • Mechanics alone. Tad McGeer's passive dynamic walkers (1990): an unpowered pair of legs, set on a gentle slope, walks down it with an eerily human gait — no motor, no controller, no program. The gait is a property of leg length, mass distribution, and gravity. Theo Jansen's Strandbeests are the art-world twin: twelve-bar linkages whose proportions — Jansen's "eleven holy numbers," found by evolutionary search on a home computer — turn wind into a walking foot-curve.1 The lesson has a name, morphological computation: put the motion in the body and the controller can shrink toward nothing.
    • Oscillators. Animals walk on central pattern generators — networks of coupled oscillators in the spinal cord that produce rhythmic motion without brain-level commands; the lamprey is the textbook case. Auke Ijspeert's salamander robot (2007) reproduced the trick: one drive signal, and the body switches from swimming to walking as the oscillator network changes mode.2 A gait, in this view, is a limit cycle, and its parameters are frequency, phase relationships, and amplitude — precisely the LFO-and-phase logic of a modular patch, applied to legs. Oscillator-plus-learning hybrids are current practice.
    • Evolution. Karl Sims's Evolved Virtual Creatures (1994) remains the founding image: block-bodied creatures whose morphology and neural controllers were co-evolved to swim, walk, jump, and follow — and which famously exploited bugs in the physics simulator when that was cheaper than walking.3 The line runs to Evolution Gym (2021), a benchmark for co-designing voxel soft robots in a mass-spring simulator fast enough to train in minutes, and on to 2026 work driving co-design with graph neural networks and continuous material stiffness (stiffer bodies evolve better land gaits; softer ones do better in water).4 But the sobering result is from 2025: brain-body co-optimization consistently fails to select for morphological potential — bodies lock in before their controllers can reveal what they could have done, and the search converges prematurely; novelty search yields far more diverse morphologies than fitness does.5 Read that as a rule: for emergence, diversity pressure beats fitness pressure.
    • Optimization. Igor Mordatch's contact-invariant optimization (2012): specify a goal and the physics, and a trajectory optimizer discovers gaits, climbing, getting up off the floor — with no motion data at all.6 The motion is the solution; the author wrote only the problem.
    • Objectives (reinforcement learning). DeepMind's 2017 parkour humanoids — flailing, knees-high, unmistakably emergent — showed locomotion arising from rich terrain and a forward-progress reward alone.7 The legged-robot era made it precise and, surprisingly, biological: minimizing energy consumption makes natural gaits emerge; so do barrier-shaped "style" rewards with no motion capture whatsoever (bounding appears on its own); quadrupeds trained without references discover pace and gallop as speed rises; humanoid controllers now walk, hop, and stand on one leg from a single policy.8 The lever here is the environment: when a behavior won't emerge, change the world before tuning the reward — the self-play and affordance-ladder lesson — and when you want a range of behaviors, co-evolve terrains with the walker, admitting only terrains that are neither trivial nor impossible (POET's minimal-criterion gate, which the commons found to be the load-bearing part).
    • Rules. Craig Reynolds's boids (1987): three local rules — separation, alignment, cohesion — and a flock appears that no one choreographed.9 Drone-swarm shows and crowd simulation are boids made commercial: collective locomotion defined entirely by rule.
    • Repertoires. Quality-diversity turns all of the above from "find a gait" into "find every gait." The canonical demonstration is Cully et al. (2015): MAP-Elites fills a behavior grid with some thirteen thousand distinct hexapod gaits, and when the robot is damaged it searches that map for a compensating gait in minutes.10 That is a machine-made gamut of locomotion — a finite, indexable vocabulary of ways to move — which is where this chapter rejoins the rest of the part.

    Humanoid versus not¶

    The same emergent gait means two different things depending on the body that performs it, and the difference is perceptual, not technical.

    On a humanoid, emergence reads as error. The architecture chapter carried a finding from motion-graph research that bites here: everyone is an expert on walking, so tolerance for wrongness in human gait is near zero. The 2017 parkour humanoid is funny because it is a person-shape doing it wrong; a physically plausible but uncanny walk is worse than stylized animation. Which is why humanoid locomotion defined by objective gets paired with priors or physics cleanup before anyone sees it.

    On a non-humanoid body, emergence reads as character. No one knows how a six-legged voxel blob should walk; there is no ground-truth gait to be wrong against, so whatever the system discovers is, by default, how that creature moves — which is why Sims's creatures have been loved for thirty years, why a dancing quadruped robot charms instead of unsettling, and why a Strandbeest on a beach reads as an animal rather than a mechanism. The design rule falls straight out: use emergent definition most freely where nobody knows what correct looks like. Novel morphologies are not a complication for these methods — they are their natural home.

    Non-human bodies on stage¶

    Which brings the chapter to the stage, where non-human performers already work, and where how their motion gets defined turns out to recapitulate this entire part.

    • Spot, and the Choreographer. Boston Dynamics ships a public tool for dancing their quadruped: a drag-and-drop timeline of moves — a built-in library of pre-validated primitives (steps, body poses, turns, kneels, arm gestures), each with parameters and a beat-length — laid against music and, recently, synced to SMPTE timecode so the robot cues like a lighting rig.11 Their stated goal is that a user works "like a choreographer instructing a dancer rather than an engineer programming a robot," and the famous "Do You Love Me" routine was in fact made by one — Monica Thomas, a human dance-maker.12 Look at the structure: an engineer-authored gamut, a human sequencer — the chance chapter's two layers verbatim — with one genuinely new feature: transition legality lives in the firmware. Every move is something the balance controller can always execute and chain, so the choreographer never meets an illegal join. The legality graph, solved once, shipped in the body.
    • KUKA, and Huang Yi. The Taiwanese choreographer has danced duets with an industrial robot arm since the early 2010s, programming every trajectory himself — roughly ten to twenty hours of programming per minute of choreography.13 No gamut, no sequencer: hand-keyed motion, and the human is the adaptive layer, fitting his body and timing to a machine that cannot improvise. It is the human gate run in reverse, and the tenderness of the piece comes from exactly that asymmetry.
    • Strandbeests perform wherever they are set down; the choreography is the choice of beach and wind.
    • Drone swarms are choreographed at the level of formations — the authored layer is trajectories for the group; local rules keep the thousand agents from colliding.
    • Pilobolus, Momix, and the puppeteers go the other direction: non-human forms made of human bodies or operated objects — several performers sharing one creature — where the thing that reads as alive (breath, weight, hesitation) is precisely what no single operator controls. War Horse's three-person horse is the textbook case.

    What it means for the synthetic dancer¶

    The synthetic dancer of Part VII does not have to move by capture. Its motion can be defined by a body and an objective and allowed to emerge — and for a humanoid that is a risky choice requiring priors and physics to stay presentable, while for any stranger form it is the best choice available. The human gate returns, as it always does, one level up: not "is this step legal" but "which of the thirteen thousand gaits reads as dance." And the most interesting creative territory this chapter opens may be the least explored: choreographing for bodies that have never existed, whose way of moving is discovered rather than known — the one place where the generator genuinely gets to invent material, because there was no tradition to author it.
