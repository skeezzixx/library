    Chance and rule-based choreography¶

    In the early 1950s, Merce Cunningham began deciding parts of his dances by tossing coins. It remains one of the most misunderstood acts in twentieth-century art — misread then as abdication, misread now as a quaint precursor to "generative art." What Cunningham actually built, and what Trisha Brown, Lucinda Childs, Anna Halprin, and the Fluxus artists built after him, was a body of working generative systems with documented mechanisms, documented sizes, and documented failure modes — a seventy-year experimental record on exactly the question every generative practitioner faces: which decisions do you keep, and which do you hand to the system?

    The record has a shape. Learn the shape and you can skip a decade of rediscovering it.

    Cunningham: chance decides continuity, never material¶

    Cunningham's procedure, developed with John Cage and stabilized by Suite by Chance (1953), ran on charts. Before any randomness entered, he composed charts of movement material by hand — one chart of phrases, and separate charts for the questions he was willing to let chance answer: sequence, duration, direction, which dancer, how many. Then coin tosses (later hexagrams from the I Ching, later dice and card draws) indexed into the charts.1

    The division of labor is the whole point, and Cunningham stated both halves in the same breath. The famous axiom — "it is possible for anything to follow anything else" — is remembered; the constraint that made it work is not: the charts, he was explicit, were not made by chance. The movement in each cell was chosen. Chance was permitted to decide continuity — order, duration, spacing, assignment — and was never once, in the entire half-century record, permitted to invent material.

    The mature works show the system at scale. Torse (1976) draws its title from its method: sixty-four phrases — the I Ching's number, which became his default gamut size — expanded from five torso positions, with hexagram draws determining sequence and spatial distribution. Canfield (1969) dealt its structure from the card game of the same name, fifty-two motions for fifty-two cards. The numbers matter, and here is the first structural regularity of the whole tradition: every gamut in the record is counted in dozens. Three phrases and one operator in Brown's Solo Olos. Twenty-seven points in Locus. Thirty movements in Primary Accumulation. Sixty-four in Torse. Hundreds is not a bigger version of this — it is a different and worse thing. When Forsythe once built a 135-item alphabet for a single solo, keeping it usable required the kinesphere itself as a memory palace.

    The systems generation: Brown, Childs, Halprin, Fluxus¶

    The choreographers who came out of Judson Dance Theater in the 1960s kept Cunningham's architecture and swapped the stochastic layer for deterministic rules — and in doing so demonstrated that the architecture never depended on randomness at all.

    Trisha Brown made the rule itself the dance. Accumulation (1971): perform gesture 1; then 1, 2; then 1, 2, 3 — an additive list executed with the patience of a proof. Locus (1975) encoded a paragraph of autobiography as numbers (A=1 … Z=26) mapped onto twenty-seven points of an imaginary cube around her body; the text drove which point she gestured toward. Same two layers as Cunningham — a small authored gamut, a mechanical sequencer — but the sequencer is a readable algorithm rather than a coin.2

    Lucinda Childs fixed nearly everything and permuted the little that remained. Calico Mingling (1973): forty six-count phrases over a vocabulary of essentially one step, walked through path permutations across four dancers — canon, phase, interference, the Steve Reich move performed by bodies (and a decade before she and Reich shared a stage with Einstein on the Beach's creative orbit).

    Anna Halprin, on the West Coast and a generation earlier than Judson (she taught several of its founders on her outdoor deck), contributed the meta-system. The RSVP Cycles, formalized with her husband, landscape architect Lawrence Halprin: Resources (the material available), Scores (instructions of deliberately chosen openness), Valuaction (a scheduled stage for judging what the score produced), Performance — then cycle.3 Two ideas here outlived the acronym: that a score's openness is a dial — Halprin placed every score explicitly on an open↔closed continuum — and that evaluation is a named, scheduled stage of the system rather than something that happens ambiently. The next chapter builds its human gate directly on that second idea.

    Fluxus supplied the limit case of openness. George Brecht's event scores — cards from the Water Yam box, often one line ("Drip Music: a source of dripping water and an empty vessel are arranged so that the water falls into the vessel") — are scores pared to almost pure frame, to be realized "publicly, privately, or negatively." Movement-prompt decks in use today (fifty-six to seventy cards is the standard range) are this idea, commercialized.

    The architecture all of it shares¶

    Line the systems up and one architecture appears in every single one:

    A gamut — a finite, named, human-authored set of units — plus a sequencer that decides only continuity: order, duration, tempo, placement, facing, assignment, density, entrances and exits.

    Three design decisions, made separately, define every system in the record:

    1. The unit list, by hand. Crisp boundaries, each unit masterable in isolation, none requiring a particular predecessor. (How to build that list well is the gamut chapter.)

    2. Exactly which dimensions the sequencer owns — enumerated in advance. This is the deepest lesson, and the least obvious. Cunningham pre-wrote, per work, the list of questions chance would be asked; the list varied, and the variance was the composition. And every strong system randomizes few dimensions and fixes the rest: Cunningham fixed the units, freed continuity; Brown fixed the sequence rule, freed body-part choice; Childs fixed everything but the path permutation; Halprin fixed the frame, freed everything inside it. Each dimension handed over multiplies unfamiliarity — when Cunningham stacked independent draws per body part, the resulting material was, in his own account, nearly impossible, and cost months of rehearsal to weld. Free every dimension and you haven't built a more generative system — you've built improvisation. That isn't a sneer: the Grand Union (the improvisational collective that emerged when Judson veterans loosened every constraint at once) is the documented instance of the transition, and the point at which the practice stops being composition in any repeatable sense.

    3. When the draw happens. At authoring time, frozen thereafter — Cunningham's default: chance composed the piece, then the piece was rehearsed and toured as fixed repertory. Or live at showtime — his Field Dances gave dancers in-performance choices, and that is a different genre with different failure modes, not a braver version of the same one.

    The claim underneath all three, and the tradition's central finding: a generator's leverage comes from the separation, not from the randomness. Chance, rule, algorithm, model — the sequencer technology changed four times across the record, and it never much mattered. What mattered was that material stayed authored, small, and bounded while continuity stayed free.

    Can the audience read the rule?¶

    Set two of these systems side by side and a second design axis appears. Watch Accumulation for sixty seconds and you get it — the additive rule becomes legible, and from then on you're watching two things at once: the movement, and a composition materializing in real time (plus a third: the performer's visible labor of memory). Watch a Cunningham chance sequence and no amount of attention reveals a rule, because the generating structure is statistical — what observers report instead is strangeness: sequences that feel, in a way audiences struggled to articulate, inevitable rather than arbitrary, freed as they are from a choreographer's habits of what should follow what. And Yvonne Rainer's Trio A (1966) is the tradition's control experiment: a dance built to refuse every compositional recursion at once — no repetition, no accumulation, no climax, deliberately uniform dynamics — and with it, the audience's perceived structure drops to zero, on purpose.4

    So: rule legibility is a per-section design decision, independent of quality. A legible rule buys a second layer of audience attention — disproportionately valuable when the surface is thin (one dancer, one voice, one instrument, one screen). An opaque rule buys inevitability and strangeness, and asks the surface to carry all the interest itself. Make the choice by default and generative work reads as arbitrary; that's usually the actual diagnosis behind the complaint.

    The legible mechanisms, usefully, are the cheap ones — additive and subtractive lists, retrograde, rotation, phase, exhaustive permutation of a tiny lexicon. And the tradition's answer to their predictability is not added noise, which destroys readability immediately, but layering a second legible system: Accumulation with Talking runs two accumulations and two spoken stories simultaneously; the group Accumulation phase-shifts one material across four bodies. Two readable rules interfere; interference is interesting; readability survives.

    Scores that are deliberately incomplete¶

    There's a third lever, subtler than the other two, that this tradition pulls constantly: emit an unfinished instruction, and let the performer finish it.

    Brecht's one-line cards are the pure case, but the move is everywhere. Laban's own Motif Notation — the stripped-down cousin of Labanotation, using Guest's Movement Alphabet — exists precisely to record only the leading intention ("travel, then turn, ending enclosed") and leave every other dimension free: a score that is literally a program with free variables. The contemporary systems make the purpose explicit. Scuddle (2011) evolves "movement catalysts" — a body position, a height level, effort qualities, and deliberately no path between them — scored by Laban-derived heuristics, with the stated goal of breaking the choreographer's habits. Kate Sicchio's Terpsicode projects keyframes and leaves the dancer to tween them.

    Why this is the cheapest robust pattern in the whole toolkit: the three hardest problems in recombining movement — legal transitions, stylistic coherence, physical feasibility — are delegated in a single move to the one person who was going to execute the result anyway. Nothing else solves three problems with one decision.

    But the record is equally clear about when it pays, and the conditions bite:

    • The axes you do specify must collide with habit. Under-specify along dimensions the performer is fluent in and they hand you their defaults back — novelty pressure has to land where the habits live, which is Scuddle's entire design.
    • Openness is a dial, set per section — Halprin's continuum, or Nancy Stark Smith's Underscore (the contact-improvisation frame), which sets the dial differently in each of its twenty-plus named phases.
    • It trades machine control for human competence. The pattern needs a performer whose completion beats your rule. With no performer in the loop — a fully synthetic pipeline — this lever mostly isn't available, and everything it delegated comes back on the system's plate.

    Old news, in the best way¶

    One closing reframe. This chapter's architecture — a fixed, checkable frame; a free, skilled realization inside it — is not a 1950s invention. It is how much of the world's music has always specified itself: a jazz standard's changes against the solo, a raga's grammar against the alap, flamenco's compás against the cante, a dub riddim against its versions. Traditions discovered again and again that you don't specify the piece — you specify a space of performances, as a constraint system plus a convention for filling it. Cunningham's charts and Halprin's scores are dance finding the same split, and stating it unusually plainly.

    Which is encouraging, if you're building such a system: you are not imitating a mid-century avant-garde experiment. You are implementing one of the oldest architectures in performance — with, for the first time, a sequencer that can check its own work. How it checks is the next chapter.
