    Choreographing in latent spaces¶

    Part V · Chance, systems, algorithms — chapter 4 of 5 · ← Computational choreography: a history · Locomotion without a choreographer →

    Everything in this part so far has been discrete: named units, finite gamuts, legality graphs with countable edges. The obvious question — obvious now, anyway — is whether any of it survives when movement is represented the way modern ML actually represents it: as a point moving through a continuous learned space, where between any two movements there are infinitely many others, and "vocabulary" seems to dissolve entirely.

    People have choreographed this way. Some of them used autoencoders. Some of them, remarkably, did it decades before the mathematics existed — and the pre-mathematical versions turn out to illuminate the learned ones. This chapter takes the continuous position seriously, traces both lineages, and then asks the structural question: how much of the gamut+rules architecture survives the loss of the unit?

    The learned movement space¶

    The framing, briefly, for the one reader who hasn't built one. Train a model to compress motion data — sequences of joint rotations — through a bottleneck and reconstruct them, and the bottleneck's coordinates become a latent space: a compact space in which every point decodes to a motion, nearby points decode to similar motions, and directions acquire meaning no one programmed. Movement operations become geometry: interpolation is a line between two points, blending is averaging, "more like this" is a direction you can keep walking.

    For motion specifically, the founding demonstrations are Daniel Holden's mid-2010s graphics work — convolutional autoencoders over large mocap corpora, yielding a "motion manifold" on which synthesis, interpolation, editing, and style transfer all became latent-space operations.1 Dance arrived almost immediately. chor-rnn (2016) trained a recurrent network on five hours of one choreographer's mocap and generated novel continuations in her style — the space was implicit, but the compositional act ("start here, let the model continue, curate") was already latent-space choreography.2 Mariel Pettee — a particle physicist and dancer — built the explicit version: Beyond Imitation (2019) trained variational autoencoders on her own improvisation, then used sampling and interpolation as an improvisation partner, generating phrases she then learned back into her body.3

    The flagship remains Wayne McGregor's Living Archive (2019, with Google Arts & Culture): a model trained on twenty-five years of McGregor's company archive, its movement space visualized as a navigable map, generating continuations from a dancer's live pose. You met its gate in the architecture chapter — thirty proposals per pose, dancers selecting. Note what the dancers were navigating: not "dance" in general but a learned space of McGregor — his archive, his style, made spatial.4

    Where the manifold bites¶

    The seduction of the continuous picture is interpolation: infinite movement between any two movements, no transition problem, no legality graph. The trap is one of the deepest recurring findings in the commons, and it has a dance-specific bite.

    Valid movement is a thin manifold in a vast space. The space of all joint-angle sequences is astronomically larger than the space of possible human movement — almost every point in it is a body doing something no body can do. Learned motion spaces are useful precisely because training bends them toward the valid manifold; they are dangerous because nothing guarantees it. Walk a straight line between two legal points and the midpoint can be quietly illegal — which is what foot-sliding, floating pivots, and impossible weight shifts in generated motion are: the visible symptom of a path that left the manifold and came back.

    The audio world learned the design lesson in a directly transplantable form: naive interpolation between two synth presets in raw parameter space sounds terrible, because a straight line through parameters is not a straight line through timbre — and the fix is to regularize the latent during training so that its geometry matches perceptual distance, after which interpolation stays musical. The motion translation: a movement latent is choreographically useful in proportion to how much the geometry you navigate matches the space that matters — physical validity, and beyond it style. Distance in a good movement latent should mean "a body can get there from here credibly," which is, of course, exactly what the transition legality graph computed discretely. Same requirement, new substrate: the legality graph becomes the metric.

    The pre-mathematical lineage¶

    Now the part that should permanently change how you see the effort chapter. Choreographers built navigable movement spaces by hand, long before anyone could learn one — and the constructions are good enough to be read, without squinting, as designed latent spaces.

    Laban's choreutics is the strongest case, and one of the least-told stories in this subject. The same Laban who factored effort spent decades on what he called space harmony: a geometry of the kinesphere — the sphere of reach around a standing body — structured by inscribed polyhedra, most famously the icosahedron, whose vertices define standard directional points. Movement, in this system, is a trajectory through the structured space, and Laban composed scales — the A scale, the B scale, prescribed circuits visiting the icosahedron's points in order — explicitly analogized to musical scales, with sequences of directions treated as consonant or strained the way intervals are.5 Sit with what that is: a hand-built coordinate system for movement, with named positions, canonical paths, a notion of distance, and a notion of harmonic relation between regions. Dancers trained the scales daily, which is to say: they practiced navigating the space. It lacks only the word "latent."

    The effort cube is the quality-space version — three bipolar factors as axes, eight named corners, adjacency as similarity, diagonals as contrast. And it was made continuous the moment EMOTE implemented the factors as dials in [−1, +1]: at that point "punch" and "float" are regions, not categories, and a phrase can trace a curve through quality space. A designed, interpretable, three-dimensional latent for movement dynamics — from the 1940s.

    Forsythe's operations are directions. Extension, folding, extrusion, compression — each is a continuous transformation applicable to any material by degrees, which is precisely what a meaningful direction in a learned latent is (the "smile vector" of face models, the style axes of motion models). A dancer literate in the operations is doing vector arithmetic on their own material, kinesthetically.

    And Gaga speaks in directions, not positions. Float, quake, melt are not shapes to reach but qualities to move toward, by degrees, from wherever you are — instructions of the form "walk this way in sensation space." The anti-vocabulary of the technique chapter is, in this light, a navigation convention for a space with no landmarks: only vectors, never coordinates.

    The architecture, translated¶

    So: does the two-layer finding — authored material, sequenced continuity — survive the continuous world? Check it against the actual practice, and the answer is: almost entirely, in translation.

    • The corpus is the gamut. Living Archive learned McGregor's archive; chor-rnn learned one choreographer's five hours; Pettee's VAEs learned her own improvisation. In every documented case the material is still human-authored, bounded, and personal — chance still never invents material; it now interpolates it. The authoring moved from writing the unit list to curating the corpus, but it did not disappear, and its size discipline echoes too: a space distilled from one artist stays navigable and stylistically coherent the way a 64-phrase gamut does, where "all of mocap" is the latent version of the 135-item alphabet.
    • The walk is the sequencer. Which dimensions the system owns is still the composition: Pettee choosing where to sample, McGregor's dancers choosing when to consult; a latent walk's step size and direction policy are Cunningham's "list of questions chance will answer," re-parameterized.
    • Staying on the manifold is the legality graph — argued above.
    • The gate persists, unchanged. Thirty proposals, dancers select. Pettee learning phrases back into her body is valuaction. Nothing about continuity removed the human gate; if anything the firehose got wider, making the gate's throughput budget bind harder.

    What genuinely changes is the loss of the unit boundary, and it cuts both ways. The transition problem dissolves — there are no joins, only paths. But with it go the edit points: the held stillness, the phrase seam, the moment the gamut chapter called the most useful cut on camera. A latent walk is all middle. And rule legibility — the chance chapter's second axis — collapses toward the opaque pole: an audience can read an accumulation in sixty seconds, but no one reads a latent walk; strangeness is the only register it offers. The continuous world, in other words, buys smoothness with structure, and the price is exactly the structures the discrete tradition knew how to spend.

    Which is why the most interesting position is the hybrid one — and why it's so satisfying that Laban got there first. A discrete gamut of authored units, each placed in a continuous quality space that modifies and interpolates its rendering: that is choreutics plus the scales, it is effort dials over a movement alphabet, and it is, roughly, where the strongest current systems are converging — discrete tokens or graph nodes for material and editability, continuous latents for quality, blending, and style. The state of that art, and what it means for a working pipeline, is Part VII's business — the movement-generation chapter's research sweep will take the inventory.
