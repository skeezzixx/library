    Computational choreography: a history¶

    Part V · Chance, systems, algorithms — chapter 3 of 5 · ← The gamut+rules architecture · Choreographing in latent spaces →

    The chance chapter made a claim worth testing against the record: the sequencer technology — coin, rule, algorithm, model — changed repeatedly across seventy years and never much mattered, because the leverage was always in the separation of authored material from delegated continuity. This chapter is the test. It follows choreographers into the computer, from the first keyframed stick figure in 1989 to a public AI tool trained on one man's archive in 2025, and asks what actually changed each time the tools did.

    1989: the stick figure enters the studio¶

    The computer arrived in choreography as a drawing tool. LifeForms — developed at Simon Fraser University through the late 1980s by Tom Calvert's group with dancer-technologist Thecla Schiphorst, later commercialized (and later still renamed DanceForms) — let a user pose and keyframe an articulated figure and play the result back.1 Its famous adopter was Merce Cunningham, then seventy, who took it up in 1989 and premiered the first dance composed with it, Trackers, in 1991. He kept composing with it for the rest of his life.

    Two things about LifeForms matter more than its feature list. First, it gave Cunningham a proposal medium that suited his system exactly: a figure that could be asked to do anything, with no opinion about whether a body could. Sequences the software rendered happily turned out to be impossible on dancers and had to be re-solved in rehearsal — the architecture chapter's point that a convincing preview defers the feasibility check rather than performing it. Second, and more consequential: the software's avatar was divided into fourteen sections you keyframed independently, which made per-limb layering the cheapest operation in the tool — and critics can date Cunningham's work of that era on sight by its angular arm material running in counter-rhythm to the legs. Nobody chose that style. The tool's easy path became the house idiom over a decade of work. It is the cleanest documented case of an affordance leaking into art, and every later tool in this chapter is implicated by it.

    1999–2001: the captured body as decor¶

    The next move was capture, not composition. For Biped (1999), Cunningham had his dancers motion-captured and the data handed to Paul Kaiser and Shelley Eshkar — the digital artists who would become OpenEndedGroup — who rendered it as hand-drawn figures projected on a scrim in front of the live dancers: the captured body as a second, ghostly cast.2 Loops (2001) went further inward — motion-capturing only Cunningham's hands performing his 1971 solo of that name, then rendering the data as abstract drawn forms. The compositional system was untouched; what computation added was a new kind of performer — one made of data — and a question that hasn't closed since: what does a captured dance look like when you stop rendering it as a person?

    1994–2013: Forsythe makes the thinking visible¶

    William Forsythe's digital projects took a different road: not composition, not decor, but analysis rendered public. Improvisation Technologies began as an in-house training tool in 1994 and was published by ZKM in 1999 as a CD-ROM in which Forsythe, on video, draws his operations — lines, folds, extrusions — as white graphics over his own moving body.3 The gamut chapter treated those operations as an API; here they are an artifact — a choreographer externalizing his literacy so others can acquire it.

    Synchronous Objects (2009), made with Ohio State's Advanced Computing Center for the Arts and Design, took one dance — One Flat Thing, reproduced — and generated a suite of data-visualizations from it: cueing networks, movement density, alignment annotations, the dance's "counterpoint" drawn as a system.4 And Motion Bank (2010–2013) extended the method to other choreographers, producing online scores for Deborah Hay, Jonathan Burrows and Matteo Fargion, Bebe Miller, and Thomas Hauert.5 Behind all of it sits Forsythe's essay Choreographic Objects (2008), whose central formulation is worth carrying around: a choreographic object is "a model of potential transition from one state to another" — which is to say, a choreographic idea detached from a body and made available to other media.6 That sentence licenses this entire subject.

    2000s–2019: the machine proposes¶

    The computer started proposing movement in earnest through OpenEndedGroup's long collaboration with Wayne McGregor. The Choreographic Language Agent, developed across the 2000s with his company, generated choreographic suggestions and — the design decision that survives every model change since — rendered them as animated points, lines, and planes rather than as an anatomical avatar, because dancers imitate a figure but must interpret a sketch. Fidelity of the proposal sets how much the human contributes: low fidelity for completion, high for selection.

    The learning era followed quickly:

    • Scuddle (2011) — an evolutionary system generating deliberately pathless "movement catalysts" to break choreographers' habits (the under-specification lever of the chance chapter).
    • chor-rnn (2016) — a recurrent network trained on one choreographer's mocap, generating continuations in her style.
    • Beyond Imitation (2019) — Mariel Pettee's variational autoencoders as an improvisation partner, trained on her own movement.
    • Living Archive (2019) — McGregor with Google Arts & Culture: a model trained on twenty-five years of the company's video, visualized as a navigable map of his movement, proposing continuations from a dancer's pose; the dancers selected.7

    In parallel, an academic track built the datasets and models for music-to-dance generation — AIST++ (2021) as the benchmark corpus, Bailando (2022) with its learned choreographic memory, EDGE (2023) with diffusion — the work the discovery chapter and Part VII examine in depth. The two tracks barely cite each other and are solving the same problem: the artist's track wants a collaborator, the research track wants a benchmark score, and both keep building a codebook plus a sequencer.

    2025: the gate goes public¶

    The most recent move is the second generation of McGregor's collaboration. AISOMA (2025) rebuilt Living Archive on better capture — nearly four million poses extracted from hundreds of archive videos, now in 3D rather than 2D — and changed the interface: a user dances a short phrase at their device's camera, and the system extends it with original phrases in McGregor's vocabulary.8 McGregor used it privately first, to "expand, challenge, and interrogate" existing sequences; then it was released publicly alongside Wayne McGregor: Infinite Bodies at Somerset House (October 2025 – February 2026), so that anyone could co-create with the archive of one choreographer. Dancers who use it describe the output as stimulus to interpret, not steps to copy — the Choreographic Language Agent's lesson, now at consumer scale.

    The 2019-to-2025 delta is the telling thing. The compositional idea didn't change at all: propose from a corpus, human selects. What changed was capture quality — two dimensions to three — and who sits at the gate: a public tool makes every user their own valuaction stage.

    The five patterns¶

    Thirty-five years, a dozen systems, and the record is consistent enough to state as rules:

    • The material stayed authored. LifeForms keyframed Cunningham's choices; chor-rnn, Pettee, Living Archive, AISOMA all learned from one artist's corpus. Computation never once supplied material from nowhere; the corpus is the gamut.
    • The human gate never left. Cunningham refined LifeForms output in rehearsal; McGregor's dancers select from proposals; AISOMA's users interpret. Every system that produced kept work has a person choosing.
    • Proposal rendering is a design decision. Points and lines when you want interpretation; a realistic figure when you want selection. OpenEndedGroup understood this in the 2000s and nobody has improved on the principle.
    • Affordances leak into style. LifeForms' fourteen sections became a decade of counter-rhythm arms. Audit the easy path of any tool as an aesthetic choice.
    • The real progress has been capture. From hand-keyframed figures, to mocap as decor, to learned archives in 2D, to learned archives in 3D: each leap in what could be recorded changed what could be proposed. The compositional architecture has been stable since 1953.

    Which is exactly what the chance chapter predicted, and why the latent-spaces chapter could check that architecture against the continuous world and find it intact. The computers changed everything about the studio and nothing about the split. Part VII takes up the capture story where the progress actually is.
