    The gamut+rules architecture¶

    The previous chapter read seventy years of generative choreography as a record; this one reads it as a specification. Suppose you accept its findings — material authored and small, continuity handed to a sequencer, dimensions enumerated — and set out to build such a system today, with a typed gamut in hand. Three problems remain, and they are where the real engineering lives: what makes a chain of units legal, who judges whether a legal chain is any good, and — least obvious — what form the system's proposals should take. Each has a documented answer.

    Transitions are the hard part — and they are content¶

    Start with the pedagogy's blunt correction to how engineers instinctively frame this. Transitions are not glue between the real material. Dance teaching is explicit that a transition is material — it can be a breath, a weight shift, or an entire traveling passage whose job is repositioning — and Cunningham went further: "dynamics in movement come from the continuity." The joins are where the expressive interest concentrates, not where it's absent.

    Two practical facts ride along with this reframe. First, the expensive transitions are the ones that change level or support — floor to standing, standing to air. They dominate rehearsal time in the human case and failure rates in the synthetic one, so keep level-changing units a minority of the gamut and rate-limit them per phrase. Second, transitions start early: in an eight-count phrase the transition typically begins around count six or seven — the last beats of a unit are already preparation for the next. A system that treats units as sealed blocks meeting at instantaneous boundaries has mismodeled the material from the start.

    Structurally, the consequence is that a vocabulary should partition into content units and connector units — weight shifts, pivots, breath suspensions, rises and lowers, rolls — whose declared job is satisfying somebody else's preconditions. Hold that thought; it becomes the fix of choice below.

    Three transition policies¶

    Any system that recombines authored units makes a policy decision about joins — usually by accident. The record contains exactly three policies, and the crucial fact about them: they are per-section choices, not system-level ones.

    1. The legality graph. Annotate every unit's entry and exit state; generate only legal walks. Smooth, scalable, the animation industry's default, and the only policy that works with no human in the loop. Cost: the annotation work, plus one system-level check covered below.

    2. The neutral hub. Define a common neutral — a stand, a home position — and route every join through it. Always legal, zero annotation. This is what movement-prompt decks and accumulation practice do implicitly. Cost: every join has the same shape, the result is rhythmically flat, and the hub becomes a visible tic. (You have heard this failure: it is the generative-music equivalent of returning to the tonic between every phrase.)

    3. Illegal by design. Cunningham's position, held deliberately: anything may follow anything, and solving the impossible joins in rehearsal is the expressive content. The costs are documented with unusual precision — Changeling took months; dancers mastered units in isolation and then welded them; Carolyn Brown described the only viable coping strategy as "absolute concentration on each single moment." So is the payoff: audiences reported chance sequences over a good gamut as reading inevitable, where conventionally structured work "looks contrived." Choose this policy knowingly, and cap unit difficulty so the welds stay humanly solvable. With synthetic dancers, note, the rehearsal room where impossible joins get solved does not exist.

    Mechanics of the legality graph¶

    For policy 1, the definitive recipe comes from an unexpected direction: Motion Graphs, the SIGGRAPH 2002 paper that taught game animation how to stitch mocap clips.1 Its four mechanics transfer to any system that concatenates authored units — the paper is about pixels, but the findings are about joins.

    Compare a window, not an instant. Naive per-frame pose distance fails, because a good join must match velocities and accelerations, not just positions. The paper's fix: compare the material over a window of roughly a third of a second, which makes the derivatives implicit in the comparison. General form: define the join metric over a window of the signal and derivative continuity comes free.

    Quotient out what the domain leaves free. A motion clip is the same motion anywhere on the floor and facing any direction, so the distance is minimized over that free transform before comparing. Consequence for your gamut's type signatures: facing is stored relative, never absolute — only rotational momentum genuinely constrains a successor. Every domain has such a symmetry (in music it is transposition), and failing to quotient it out means rejecting valid joins and duplicating your library to compensate.

    Set thresholds by audience expertise, not globally. The paper's own example: walking demands exacting joins because every viewer is an expert on walking; ballet is more forgiving because they aren't. Read that twice — it means join tolerance is a perceptual parameter, per category of material. Pedestrian connectors (walk, stand, turn) need the cleanest joins in your entire set; stylized material grants slack.

    Prune to the largest strongly connected component. Pairwise legality is not enough: a graph can be locally correct and still contain sinks — states with legal entries and no legal exits — where generation stalls. Keep only the largest strongly connected component, and when it comes out too small, add connector units rather than loosening the metric. A gamut of brilliant content units with no connectors fails this check no matter how good the units are; the fix is never to redefine legality, it is to hire more of the units whose job is joining.

    One adjustment when the executor is human rather than a renderer: a dancer out-blends any interpolator, so tune the thresholds permissive and let the performer legalize marginal joins. The structure transfers; the numbers don't.

    The human gate¶

    Here is the finding the generative-art literature keeps treating as an embarrassment, restated as the load-bearing component it is: every system in the seventy-year record that produced kept work has a human accept/reject gate. Cunningham "almost always" refined what chance handed him. Halprin's RSVP Cycles schedules the gate as a named stage — valuaction — which is precisely what makes it a cycle rather than a one-shot generator. Wayne McGregor's Living Archive (the machine-learning collaboration with Google) proposes thirty continuations per pose; the dancers select. Curation is designed in. It is not a confession that the generator is weak.

    Cunningham's gate is worth studying in detail, because he stated his criteria, and they are two different kinds of check: aesthetic interest — does the sequence read as a necessary, integral form — and practicality — can human beings perform it, in this space. A candidate can be interesting-but-impossible or performable-but-dull, and knowing which gate rejected it tells you which knob to turn upstream. Keep the criteria separate in your own gate, even if one person applies both.

    The architecture that falls out is a clean two-tier split:

    • The machine tier checks whatever is stateful and enumerable. For movement: the support/level/balance/momentum/facing chain — the legality graph above. Cheap, exhaustive, and blessedly tasteless.
    • The human tier judges quality and interest — plus everything the domain defines as a performance target rather than a measurable. Effort qualities are the clean example: no checker can verify a "wringing" quality, and pretending otherwise just launders taste through a metric.

    Blurring the tiers fails in exactly two directions, both worth naming. Train a learned "is-it-good" scorer and start optimizing against it, and you get the classic Goodhart collapse — the score improves while the work degrades. Or keep tightening the mechanical checker to encode taste it cannot see, and it starts rejecting joins a performer would have absorbed without noticing. The machine tier's job is to be strong and dumb: strict about state, silent about quality.

    And a budgeting rule that sounds trivial and decides projects: a gate is only load-bearing if somebody sits at it. Cunningham filtered every draw; RSVP schedules valuaction; a proposal stream nobody selects from is a firehose, not a system. Size the generator's output to the number of candidates a human can actually judge, and let the machine tier guarantee nothing obviously invalid ever reaches them.

    The proposal is an interface¶

    The last finding is the one nobody expects, and it may be the most transferable: the representation a system proposes in carries the same design weight as the model doing the proposing.

    Exhibit one. The Choreographic Language Agent — built by Marc Downie's OpenEndedGroup for Wayne McGregor's company — renders its choreographic proposals as animated constructions of points, lines, and planes, deliberately not as an anatomical avatar. The stated reason: dancers imitate a realistic figure literally, but are forced to interpret an abstract sketch. Fidelity of rendering directly sets how much the human contributes. Want the human's job to be selection? Render high-fidelity, so they judge the real thing. Want their job to be completion — the under-specification lever — render deliberately lossy, and lossy in the dimensions you want them to own.

    Exhibit two, the cautionary one. Cunningham spent his last two decades composing with LifeForms, early choreography software whose avatar was divided into fourteen independently keyframeable sections. Critics can date works of that era on sight: angular arm material running in counter-rhythm against the legs — because independent per-limb layering was the cheap operation, and the tool's easiest path quietly became the house style. Nobody chose that. Whatever your tool makes one-click will show up in the artifacts; audit the easy path as a style decision, not a convenience.

    Corollary, for high-fidelity previews: they defer the feasibility check, they don't perform it. LifeForms happily rendered sequences that were physically impossible, and the impossibility surfaced downstream, on dancers, in rehearsal. A convincing preview moves the reckoning later — budget for it there.

    The assembled architecture¶

    The whole synthesis fits in a paragraph. A small, typed, hand-authored gamut (content units plus connectors, state-changing verbs first-class). A sequencer owning an enumerated list of continuity dimensions and nothing else. A machine tier enforcing transition legality — window metric, symmetry quotiented out, expertise-scaled thresholds, one strongly connected component. A human gate with stated criteria, interest and feasibility kept distinct, throughput budgeted. A proposal medium chosen for the role you want the human to play. Seventy years of practice, one working diagram — and nothing in it that stops applying when the dancer is made of pixels, except the rehearsal room where policy 3 got solved.
