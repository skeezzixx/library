    Wayne McGregor¶

    Born Stockport, England, 12 March 1970. Trained Bretton Hall College (dance and drama); the José Limón School, New York. Companies Random Dance (founded 1992; Studio Wayne McGregor from 2017, resident at Sadler's Wells and then at its own East London studios); Resident Choreographer of the Royal Ballet since 2006 — the first from outside the classical world. Key collaborators the scientists and technologists (Open Ended Group, the Google Arts & Culture Lab, cognitive scientists at Cambridge and UCSD), composers (Max Richter, Joby Talbot, Jlin, Jon Hopkins, Thom Yorke), designers (Lucy Carter, lighting; Gareth Pugh, Moritz Junge, costume; the artists Olafur Eliasson, Tacita Dean, Julian Opie, Edmund de Waal, Ben Cullen Williams), and his dancers, who are trained into his thinking-body method.

    Contributions¶

    The hyper-articulate body. McGregor's movement language is immediately recognizable — extreme extension, spinal undulation and hyper-flexion, speed, the limbs folding and unfolding at angles the classical line doesn't use — applied equally to his own company and to the Royal Ballet's dancers, whom he re-wired. It is a technique in the sense that it produces a distinctive body, and it is the most imitated contemporary vocabulary in ballet companies worldwide.

    Choreography as cognition. His deepest contribution is to have treated choreography as a problem in thinking — how dancers generate material, how a "creative body" can be studied, how tools might extend it. Long collaborations with cognitive scientists (the "Choreographic Thinking Tools" research with David Kirsh and others; the Mind and Movement resource for schools) produced a documented method of imagery-led generation: dancers given mental tasks (imagine the body as a drawing, move a point through space, subtract a limb) produce movement no direct instruction would — the technique chapter's operator idea from the cognitive side.

    The computational collaborations. Three projects carry the computational-choreography chapter: the Choreographic Language Agent (2004 onward, with Open Ended Group) — software that proposes abstract movement ideas in response to a dancer's; Living Archive (2019, with Google Arts & Culture) — a model trained on twenty-five years of the company's video that generates new phrases in his idiom, used live in Living Archive: An AI Performance Experiment; and AISOMA (2025) — the public version, a tool that lets anyone work with his archive the way the company does. McGregor is the field's clearest case of a choreographer treating a generative model as a collaborator whose proposals he gates — the gamut+rules architecture's human gate, with a learned sequencer. The latent-spaces chapter reads Living Archive as navigation of a learned space of his own work.

    Scale and institution. As the Royal Ballet's resident he made full-evening narrative ballets (Woolf Works, The Dante Project) that reconciled his abstraction with the house's storytelling; as a company director he built a studio that hosts research, residencies, and education; he has choreographed for film (Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire, Fantastic Beasts, Mary Queen of Scots), opera, Radiohead, and the Paralympic ceremony.

    Major works¶

    • Xeno (1994), Sulphur 16 (1998), Nemesis (2002 — the dancers with prosthetic arm extensions) — the early Random Dance works, technology already present.
    • AtaXia (2004) — from research on the neurological condition: movement built from disrupted coordination; Amu (2005) — the heart, with scientists and a cardiac surgeon.
    • Chroma (2006, Royal Ballet) — Joby Talbot and Jack White's music, John Pawson's white box; the work that got him the residency and that ballet companies worldwide now dance.
    • Entity (2008) — Random Dance, with the Choreographic Language Agent; Infra (2008, Royal Ballet) — Max Richter's score, Julian Opie's walking LED figures above the dancers.
    • Limen (2009), Live Fire Exercise (2011), Carbon Life (2012 — Mark Ronson, Gareth Pugh), Raven Girl (2013 — a narrative ballet after Audrey Niffenegger).
    • FAR (2010) — after Roy Porter's Flesh in the Age of Reason; UNDANCE (2011); Atomos (2013 — in 3-D glasses; the dancers' material generated partly from a film).
    • Woolf Works (2015, Royal Ballet) — Max Richter, three Virginia Woolf novels; Alessandra Ferri's return; the Olivier Award and the proof that his idiom could carry narrative.
    • Tree of Codes (2015) — Jamie xx, Olafur Eliasson, Jonathan Safran Foer's cut-up book: the Paris Opéra and his company together.
    • Obsidian Tear (2016), Multiverse (2016), Autobiography (2017 — twenty-three sections ordered each night by an algorithm working from his genome sequence; Jlin's score), Yugen (2018), Living Archive (2019), The Dante Project (2021, Royal Ballet — Thomas Adès, Tacita Dean; the Divine Comedy full-length), MaddAddam (2022, National Ballet of Canada — Atwood), UniVerse: A Dark Crystal Odyssey (2023), Deepstaria (2024), and the 2025 AISOMA performances.

    Where he is in this subject¶

    The computational-choreography chapter (the Agent, Living Archive, AISOMA); the latent-spaces chapter; the technique chapter; the modern revolt (the contemporary now); the lighting chapter (Lucy Carter).
