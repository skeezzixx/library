    Doris Humphrey¶

    Part VIII · The shelf — page 4 of 24 · ← Martha Graham · Katherine Dunham →

    Born Oak Park, Illinois, 17 October 1895 · died New York, 29 December 1958. Trained Denishawn (1917–28), where she also taught and made her first "music visualizations." Companies Humphrey-Weidman (with Charles Weidman, 1928–44); artistic director of the José Limón Dance Company (1946–58). Key collaborators Charles Weidman, José Limón, Pauline Lawrence (designer, manager), Wallingford Riegger and Vivian Fine (composers), and the Bennington School of the Dance, where she taught with Graham and Holm (1934–42). Students Limón, Sybil Shearer, Eleanor King, and through the Juilliard dance division (which she helped found, 1951) a generation of teachers.

    Contributions¶

    Fall and recovery. Humphrey's technique derives movement from gravity: the body's "arc between two deaths" — the stillness of standing and the stillness of lying down — with the fall (yielding to gravity), the recovery (the rebound), and the suspension at the top of the arc as the expressive moment. Breath rhythm, weight, momentum, the swing: the technique chapter sets it beside Graham's contraction as the other foundational principle of American modern dance, and the one whose physics (pendulum, momentum, the moment of suspension) maps most directly onto the plausible-body chapter's dynamics.

    The ensemble and the group form. Where Graham built on the solo heroine, Humphrey built on the group: Water Study (1928), sixteen dancers breathing and surging as one body, without music; The Shakers (1931), the religious community's ecstatic order; New Dance (1935) and the trilogy (Theatre Piece, With My Red Fires), the individual and the society danced as counterpoint. Her sense of the stage as a field of forces — the craft chapter's stage geography — came from composing for masses.

    The theorist. The Art of Making Dances (1959, finished just before her death) is modern dance's canonical text on composition — the stage map, symmetry as death, "all dances are too long," the eye faster than the ear, design the ending early — and the craft chapter is largely an exposition of it. She was the first modern choreographer to write down how.

    The Limón company. Crippled by arthritis, she stopped dancing in 1944 and became the artistic director of her protégé's company, choreographing for it (Day on Earth, 1947; Night Spell, 1951; Ruins and Visions, 1953) and shaping Limón's own masterpieces (The Moor's Pavane, 1949) — the model of the choreographer as director and outside eye.

    Major works¶

    • Air for the G String (1928 — Bach, the Denishawn visualization style perfected), Water Study (1928), Life of the Bee (1929), Drama of Motion (1930 — in silence), The Shakers (1931), Dionysiaques (1932), New Dance (1935), Theatre Piece (1936), With My Red Fires (1936), Passacaglia and Fugue in C minor (1938 — Bach; her ensemble masterpiece), Inquest (1944), Day on Earth (1947 — Copland), Night Spell (1951), Ruins and Visions (1953), Dawn in New York (1956), Brandenburg Concerto No. 4 (1959, completed by Ruth Currier).

    Where she is in this subject¶

    The technique chapter (fall and recovery); the craft chapter (The Art of Making Dances); the modern revolt; the working-with-music chapter (breath phrasing; Water Study); the choreomusicology chapter (music visualization).
