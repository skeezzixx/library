    Merce Cunningham¶

    Part VIII · The shelf — page 8 of 24 · ← Jerome Robbins · Alvin Ailey →

    Born Centralia, Washington, 16 April 1919 · died New York, 26 July 2009. Trained Cornish School, Seattle (where he met John Cage, 1938); soloist with Martha Graham, 1939–45. Company the Merce Cunningham Dance Company, founded at Black Mountain College, 1953; dissolved by his own plan in 2011, two years after his death, after a final "Legacy Tour." Key collaborators John Cage (music director and partner until Cage's death in 1992), David Tudor, Robert Rauschenberg (resident designer 1954–64), Jasper Johns (artistic adviser 1967–80), Mark Lancaster, Charles Atlas and Elliot Caplan (film), and composers from Feldman, Wolff, and Brown to Radiohead and Sigur Rós. Dancers who became choreographers include Paul Taylor (briefly), Viola Farber, Steve Paxton, Karole Armitage, Trisha Brown (a student), Lucinda Childs (influenced), and a large share of the Judson generation.

    Contributions¶

    Chance as a compositional method. From the early 1950s Cunningham used chance operations — coin tosses, dice, the I Ching — to decide the order of phrases, the number of dancers, the directions, the durations, and the timing of a work, while keeping the material (the movements themselves) entirely his own. The chance chapter makes the structural point at length: chance decided continuity, never invention; the gamut was authored and the sequencer was random. Its purpose, in his telling, was to get past his own habits — to produce transitions and juxtapositions his taste would never have chosen and his dancers' bodies then had to solve. The result was a new kind of stage: many things happening at once, no narrative, no climax, movement that began and ended without preparation, and a literalness (a leap is a leap) that made expression the viewer's business.

    The independence of the elements. Music, dance, and décor were made separately and met at the performance (the choreomusicology chapter). The dancers rehearsed in silence and timed their phrases by stopwatch; the composer was given a duration; the designer was given a stage; the first performance was often the first time any of them saw the whole. Cage's word for the relationship was coexistence, and the audience's irrepressible habit of finding connections anyway became one of the work's subjects.

    The decentralized stage. Following Einstein's remark that there are no fixed points in space, Cunningham declared every point on the stage equally important: no front, no center, no hierarchy of soloist and corps, movement facing any direction, the viewer free to look anywhere. His Events (from 1964) — ninety-minute, unbroken assemblages of excerpts from the repertory, performed in gyms, museums, and piazzas, often with dancers facing several ways — made this literal, and they are the props chapter's case of the set as co-present rather than depicting.

    A technique. The Cunningham technique — a long, erect, flexible spine (Graham's torso freed of its drama) over fast, intricate, balletic legs, with the back's own vocabulary of curves, tilts, twists, and arches — produced what the technique chapter calls the chance-ready body: one that can go anywhere from anywhere, because the sequence may demand it.

    The computer and the camera. In 1989 he began composing with LifeForms (later DanceForms), the first choreographer to make dances on software — Trackers (1991) was the first result — and the computational-choreography chapter opens with him; in his last years he used motion capture (BIPED, 1999, with Paul Kaiser and Shelley Eshkar's projected figures; Loops for the hands). With Charles Atlas he made video-dances (Channels/Inserts, 1981) that were choreographed for the camera rather than filmed.

    The company as crossroads. For half a century the Cunningham company was where the New York avant-garde met: Rauschenberg, Johns, Warhol, Stella, Morris, Nauman, Paik; Cage's circle of composers; and a roster of dancers who went on to found the next generations. Its dissolution by design — the Legacy Plan, with the works licensed through a trust and the company closed — is itself a contribution: the first choreographer to decide, in advance, how his work would outlive his company.

    Major works¶

    • Sixteen Dances for Soloist and Company of Three (1951) — the first use of chance, for the order of sections.
    • Suite by Chance (1953) — the first work fully composed by chance operations, to Christian Wolff's electronic score; the chance chapter's founding document.
    • Summerspace (1958) — to Feldman, in Rauschenberg's pointillist costumes and backdrop: dancers dissolving into a field; later staged for New York City Ballet.
    • Crises (1960), Aeon (1961), Winterbranch (1964, to La Monte Young, with Rauschenberg's harsh lighting — the work audiences walked out of).
    • Variations V (1965) — with Cage, Tudor, Paik, and Stan VanDerBeek: photocells and antennas triggered sound as the dancers moved; the first interactive dance.
    • RainForest (1968) — Warhol's helium Silver Clouds, Tudor's score; Walkaround Time (1968) — Johns's Duchamp Large Glass, Behrman's score; Scramble (1967) — Stella's banners; Canfield (1969) — Morris's light column.
    • Torse (1976) — the technique at its most rigorous, made entirely by chance from a gamut of sixty-four phrases; Sounddance (1975) — to Tudor, a fast, dense ensemble erupting from a curtain.
    • Roaratorio (1983) — to Cage's Roaratorio, with Irish jigs; Pictures (1984) — the dancers forming still pictures in Lancaster's light.
    • Points in Space (1986) — made for television with Elliot Caplan, then for the stage.
    • Trackers (1991) — the first LifeForms work; Beach Birds (1991); CRWDSPCR (1993) — the computer years.
    • Ocean (1994) — in the round, 112 musicians around the audience, Cage's posthumous plan; BIPED (1999) — Gavin Bryars's score, motion-captured projections, the late masterpiece.
    • Split Sides (2003) — with Radiohead and Sigur Rós, the order decided by dice on stage; Nearly Ninety (2009) — the last work, for his ninetieth birthday.
