    Choreomusicology: the relationship spectrum¶

    Part IV · Music and dance — chapter 1 of 3 · ← Video, digital scores, and annotation · How choreographers work with music →

    Dance and music are so often together that the relationship is easy to mistake for a single thing. It isn't. Between a tap dancer whose feet are the percussion and a Cunningham dancer who first hears the score on opening night lies a whole spectrum of ways movement and sound can be related — and the great choreographers are distinguished as much by where they stand on it as by anything in their vocabularies. The scholarly field that studies this is called choreomusicology (Paul Hodgins coined the word in the early 1990s; Stephanie Jordan's Moving Music, 2000, is the standard work), and its first finding is the one this chapter is built on: the relationship is a design variable, chosen per piece and often per phrase, and each position on the spectrum has a perceptual reason it works.1

    This chapter lays out the spectrum through its practitioners, then turns to the mechanism — what the audience's nervous system actually does with simultaneous sound and movement — because the commons holds a good deal of it, learned from audiovisual systems, and it turns out to be the same physics.

    The spectrum¶

    Four positions, from most to least bound:

    • Mickey-mousing — every accent in the sound is an accent in the movement. The term comes from cartoon scoring (the orchestra hitting each footstep) and is usually pejorative, but it is also Fred Astaire: the taps are the drum part, and the pleasure is exactly the redundancy.
    • Visualization — the movement renders the music's structure: its phrases, voices, repetitions, form. The term was Ruth St. Denis's and Doris Humphrey's for the Denishawn "music visualizations" of the 1910s and 20s, and it is what Balanchine and Mark Morris are accused of and proud of.
    • Counterpoint — movement and music share a time frame but follow independent lines that meet at designed points: Paul Taylor's pedestrian runs against Bach (Esplanade, 1975), Twyla Tharp's loose-limbed rhythms against Sinatra, De Keersmaeker's geometry against Reich.
    • Independence — music and dance are made separately and share only a duration. Cunningham and Cage's position, and the one that forced the field to think about the others.

    And beside the spectrum, silence: Humphrey's Water Study (1928) danced to breath, Jerome Robbins's Moves (1959) performed without any music — the control condition, which shows how much of what an audience "sees" in dance is heard.

    The practitioners¶

    Balanchine is the master of visualization, and the common caricature of him — see the music, hear the dance — undersells what he did. Trained as a pianist at the Petrograd Conservatory, he choreographed structure, not surface: Concerto Barocco (1941) gives Bach's two solo violins to two ballerinas and lets the corps be the ripieno; The Four Temperaments (1946) maps Hindemith's theme-and-variations onto bodies; Agon (1957) follows Stravinsky's twelve-tone construction so closely that the dance has been analyzed as a serial work. The point was never to illustrate a loud passage with a big jump. It was that the music's architecture — its voices, its recurrences, its cadences — is made visible, which is a different and much rarer thing.2

    Mark Morris is the living champion of the position and takes the criticism head-on. His company has performed to live music as a matter of policy since the 1990s; L'Allegro, il Penseroso ed il Moderato (1988, Handel) and Dido and Aeneas (1989, Purcell) visualize text, harmony, and counterpoint with a literalness that is either naïve or profound depending on the critic — and his defense is the one Balanchine would have given: the music is the score of the dance, and a choreographer who ignores its structure is not being independent, merely deaf.

    Anne Teresa De Keersmaeker made visualization into a structural method. Fase (1982) sets four movements to Steve Reich's phase pieces, and the dancers' phrase relationships drift exactly as the music's do — you watch the phase shift as you hear it. Rosas danst Rosas (1983) was composed simultaneously with its score (Thierry De Mey and Peter Vermeersch); Drumming (1998) and Rain (2001) derive their floor geometry from the scores' structure so rigorously that her books of the work are part musical analysis. This is visualization of process rather than of surface, and it is also the purest case in the canon of music and dance sharing one generative rule — the chance and systems chapter's territory, seen from the music side.

    Cunningham and Cage made independence a principle. From the early 1950s the music and the dance for a Cunningham work were composed separately, often in different cities, to an agreed length, and met at the first performance; the dancers kept time with stopwatches, and the relationship was, in Cage's word, coexistence. The audience, notoriously, made connections anyway — a cymbal crash "on" a leap, a silence "under" a stillness — and that is the interesting part, because those coincidences were real coincidences. Independence turned out to be a machine for revealing how hard the viewer works to bind the two. (Cage's contribution to how the music was built is the composing chapter's business.)

    Pina Bausch sits off the axis: music as collage — tango, blues, pop, Purcell, folk song cut together — where each fragment's relationship to the movement is chosen locally, from illustration to irony, and the collage's seams are part of the meaning.

    Music-led versus movement-led making cuts across the spectrum. Balanchine, Morris, and De Keersmaeker start from the score ("the floor," Balanchine called it). Graham commissioned scores to her scenarios and then choreographed to them. Forsythe has had Thom Willems compose in the room as the dance is made (In the Middle, Somewhat Elevated, 1987), so neither leads. Bausch built movement from questions and found music afterwards; Cunningham did neither first. Which leads is a decision about what the piece is about, and the next chapter treats the working practice in detail.

    The physics of the marriage¶

    Why does each position work when it works? The commons' answer comes from audiovisual systems — reactive visuals, sound-to-image mapping — but the perception it describes is the audience's, and the audience is the same for a dancer as for a projection.

    Sync is asymmetric. The brain's window for judging a sound and a sight "simultaneous" is not centered on zero. Light arrives before sound from any real-world event, and visual processing is slower than auditory, so the built-in prior is sight slightly before sound is normal. Measured: a visual that leads its sound is forgiven for a long time — broadcast standards put acceptability at up to ~125–185 ms of audio lag — while a sound that arrives before its visual event reads as wrong within roughly 15–45 ms. The shape of that asymmetry is a robust, replicated finding across psychophysics and broadcast-engineering panels; the exact figures are stimulus-specific.3 Every dancer knows the consequence without the numbers: land on or fractionally before the beat, never after. A hit that arrives after the drum reads as late almost immediately; one that arrives a hair early reads as on. The professional norm of dancing "on top of" the music — Balanchine's insistence on it is famous — is the binding window's asymmetry, embodied.

    Binding is constructive. The sound-sight relationship is not a passive coincidence detector. In the stream/bounce illusion, two discs crossing on a screen are seen to bounce if a click sounds at or just before their contact, and to pass through if it doesn't; the same click 150 ms late has far less effect. A sound that lands in a causally plausible window is bound into the visual event and changes what is seen.4 This is why mickey-mousing is powerful, not merely crude — the accent in the music literally sharpens the accent in the body — and it is why Cunningham's coincidences felt composed. The viewer binds whatever falls in the window, composer's intention or not. (Binding is also what makes the motion-capture chapter's foot-contact problems so visible: a footfall sound against a foot that hasn't landed breaks the illusion fast.)

    The ear subtracts; the eye adds. Hearing a mixture, the brain extracts — it can follow three voices at once. Looking at a scene, it fills in. The practical consequence, from the visual-music tradition, is restraint: map few things. A piece that tries to make every instrument visible in a body produces clutter the eye cannot parse, where the ear parsed the music effortlessly; a piece that picks one or two structurally meaningful lines and gives each a body — Concerto Barocco's two violins — reads. The field's craft rule is that each source should have exactly one job.5

    Map to structure, not level. An onset is a discrete event; mapping it to a discrete movement event (a hit, a drop, a cut) is not a style choice but a category match. Loudness, by contrast, is something the ear already tracks precisely — so the beginner's equation of loud music = big movement is redundant: it re-presents information the audience already has, and redundancy is the definition of dullness. Beat and downbeat map naturally to structural cuts (the formation change, the entrance); a section boundary to a scene change. And granularity nests: the coarsest visual change belongs on the coarsest musical event. A formation change mid-phrase reads as an error for the same reason a scene cut mid-bar does in film.6 Balanchine's genius, in these terms, was to work the structural rows of this table and leave the level rows alone.

    Conformance, complementation, contest. The musicologist Nicholas Cook's triad names the spectrum formally: media that are redundant with each other (conformance — the color organ, the pulsing blob, mickey-mousing at its worst), media that relate without redundancy (complementation — what the filmmaker John Whitney called complementarity, correspondence at the level of tension and release rather than beat for beat), and media that vie for the same terrain, each imposing its logic on the other (contest — Cunningham's coexistence, Bausch's collage). The film theorist Michel Chion's added value — sound's expressive charge reading as though it came from the image, and vice versa — exists only where the relationship isn't conformant: if the dance has already announced what the music is about to do, there is nothing to add. This is the perceptual case for counterpoint, and the reason visualization is a high-wire act: it lives one step from conformance, and only structure keeps it on the wire.7

    Time is addressed differently at different levels. Music organizes time as cyclic (phase in a returning cycle — a tala's sam, a clave, an eight-count), linear (a one-way trajectory to a climax — a sonata, a build-drop), or suspended (no pulse — an alap, an ambient drone), and almost every tradition mixes these at different structural levels: cyclic at the bar, linear across the piece. Dance does exactly the same — the count is cyclic, the phrase's arc is linear, the held stillness is suspended — and a choreographer's relation to the music is partly a decision about which level's time-mode to share. De Keersmaeker shares Reich's cyclic micro-time exactly and builds her own linear arc above it; Cunningham shares only the outer duration.8

    The test¶

    The commons also holds a test, built for reactive visuals, that is exactly the experiment Cunningham was running: the swap test. Play the dance against a different piece of music. If nothing reads as wrong — no hit lands on nothing, no build arrives at no target — the dance was never coupled to that music's structure; it was merely moving. If something clashes, the coupling was real. A dance that passes the swap test for every score is independent in Cunningham's sense; a dance that fails it was visualizing or counterpointing. The companion mute test — watch with the sound off — is Humphrey's "the eye is faster than the ear" as a procedure: does the movement hold its own pacing when the ear's information is removed? And the measurement lesson the commons draws from dance-generation research is worth repeating here, because it is the spectrum's moral: papers report a beat-alignment score and a separate human preference study side by side, because a dance can be mechanically on the beat and move no one. Sync is necessary at one position on the spectrum and irrelevant at another; it is never sufficient anywhere.9

    Where this leaves the synthetic dancer¶

    Every music-to-dance generator in the movement-generation chapter is, in this chapter's terms, a mickey-mouser: it consumes onset and beat features and learns to put accents on them. That is the bottom of the spectrum — the position at which binding does the most work and structure the least — and it explains both why generated dance so often looks "on the music" and why it so rarely looks musical. Visualization needs the music's architecture, counterpoint needs a second line, independence needs a choreographer willing to let the viewer bind. None of that is in the audio features. The spectrum is the to-do list.
