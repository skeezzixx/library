    Composing for choreography¶

    Writing music for dance is a discipline of its own, and most of its history is the history of composers learning — sometimes resentfully — what dancers need from a score. The needs are specific: durations that can be danced, phrases that can be counted, climaxes that land where a body can land, forms whose returns a choreographer can stage. This chapter follows the great collaborations for what they reveal about those needs, then states them plainly as a brief — because the commons holds, from its music-theory side, exactly the vocabulary for what a composer delivers: form archetypes, tension arcs, and phrase-level formal function.

    Petipa's minutage¶

    Before Tchaikovsky, "ballet composer" was a trade: Cesare Pugni, Ludwig Minkus, Riccardo Drigo turned out serviceable, four-square numbers to order, and the order was precise. Marius Petipa gave his composers a minutage — a written plan specifying, number by number, the character, tempo, meter, and bar count of each dance: so many bars of a lively waltz for the entrance, an adagio of such a length for the pas de deux, a variation of sixteen bars, a coda. For The Sleeping Beauty (1890) and The Nutcracker (1892) he handed Tchaikovsky instructions of this kind, down to the measure, and Tchaikovsky — who had admired Delibes's Sylvia (1876) and believed ballet music could be real music — wrote to them and transcended them.1

    What the minutage reveals is the nineteenth-century ballet score's form: a suite of closed numbers, each a danceable unit with a stated character, ordered for contrast. The grand pas de deux — entrée, adagio, variation, variation, coda — is itself a small suite with a fixed grammar. In the commons' form vocabulary this is the suite/medley archetype: the composer's contract is ordering, not development — self-contained units chained by tempo and character contrast, nearer to set-sequencing than to symphonic argument. That is also why the scores were so freely cut, reordered, and interpolated by ballet masters: a suite licenses it. Tchaikovsky's achievement was to honor the suite grammar while smuggling in a linear arc above it — the leitmotifs, the tonal plan, the through-line of Sleeping Beauty's Lilac Fairy music — so that the numbers add up to a drama rather than a program. (Adolphe Adam had gestured at this with Giselle's motifs in 1841; Tchaikovsky finished it.)2

    Stravinsky: the count breaks, then becomes the floor¶

    The Rite of Spring (1913) is the canonical case of music outrunning the dancers' ability to count it. Nijinsky's choreography required the cast to count Stravinsky's irregular, shifting meters aloud; Diaghilev brought in Marie Rambert, trained in Dalcroze eurhythmics, to drill the rhythm into the company; and the dancers still could barely hold it — the riot is remembered, but the counting crisis is the choreographic fact. Additive, shifting meter had arrived in the theatre before the bodies were ready.

    Balanchine's Stravinsky is the other half of the story. Apollo (1928) taught Balanchine, by his own account, that he could eliminate — that the score's clarity licensed a stripped choreography. Orpheus (1948) and above all Agon (1957) were written in conversation: the famous exchange in which Balanchine specified durations ("how long?" — "about two minutes") and Stravinsky composed to them is the minutage reborn between equals, and the choreography's structure mirrors the serial score's so closely that the dance is analyzed as a twelve-tone object. Stravinsky's ballet music, from Firebird to Agon, is a forty-year demonstration that the composer who writes to the dance need not write down to it.

    Graham's commissions and Copland's scenario¶

    Martha Graham was the twentieth century's great commissioner. Appalachian Spring (1944) was written by Aaron Copland to her scenario — the manuscript is titled "Ballet for Martha" — and she choreographed to the finished score; the order was scenario, then music, then dance. Samuel Barber (Cave of the Heart), William Schuman (Night Journey), Gian Carlo Menotti, Norman Dello Joio, Paul Hindemith (Herodiade) followed the same pattern. Graham treated the scores as Petipa had treated his — cut, reordered, re-timed to the dance as it emerged — and composers learned that a commission for dance is a commission for material, not for a fixed text.

    Cage: the composer is given a duration¶

    Cunningham's composers were given, in principle, one fact: how long. John Cage's own later scores formalized the idea as time brackets — windows within which a sound may occur, flexible inside, fixed outside — and the Cunningham roster (David Tudor, Morton Feldman, Christian Wolff, Earle Brown, Pauline Oliveros, eventually Radiohead and Sigur Rós for Split Sides, 2003) was asked to deliver a thing of a given length and nothing else. The choreomusicology chapter covered what that did to the relationship; for the composer it meant liberation from the minutage and from the dancer's count alike, and the commons' process form archetype describes much of what resulted: the composer authors a rule and a starting state, and the form is the rule's audible trace. A rule running to completion needs no choreography to agree with it, and for thirty years none did.

    Process music and process dance: Glass, Reich, Childs, De Keersmaeker¶

    The minimalists turned Cage's separation into a new kind of union. Philip Glass and Lucinda Childs met on Einstein on the Beach (1976, with Robert Wilson); their Dance (1979, with Sol LeWitt's film décor) sets Childs's additive, permutational phrase structures against Glass's additive, permutational music — the same archetype on two channels. Steve Reich and Anne Teresa De Keersmaeker never collaborated directly, but Fase (1982), Drumming (1998), and Rain (2001) are the closest the repertory comes to a dance and a score being one process: phasing made visible. This is the composer's process form and the choreographer's accumulation or permutation — the chance chapter's subject — recognizing each other as the same idea, and it is why those works feel inevitable rather than illustrative.

    The contemporary commission¶

    Today's dance composer works in one of three modes. In the room: Thom Willems with Forsythe, composing electronically as the dance is built; Owen Belton with Crystal Pite; Nitin Sawhney in long partnership with Akram Khan. To a brief: Joby Talbot's full-evening scores for Christopher Wheeldon (Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, 2011; The Winter's Tale, 2014) or Max Richter for Wayne McGregor (Infra, 2008; Woolf Works, 2015), written from a scenario and a timing plan — the minutage in a new suit, with the composer now expected to deliver the arc. As found material: the choreographer who edits an existing recording, or commissions from a producer who delivers stems — Jlin for McGregor's Autobiography (2017) — which the choreographer re-cuts. And a growing fourth: the choreographer-composer — Hofesh Shechter's percussion scores, Ohad Naharin's collages and pseudonymous tracks, Sharon Eyal's live DJ. In the commercial world the question barely arises: the track exists first, and the choreography is made to it.

    What composers actually deliver has changed accordingly: a rehearsal mix early, a tempo map and click track for unison and for film, stems so the choreographer can thin or thicken a passage, cue points marked, and — always — a willingness to be cut. The composer who understands dance writes in units that survive surgery.

    The brief, stated¶

    All of this history reduces to a set of things a choreographer needs from a score, and the commons' music-theory notes supply the vocabulary to ask for them precisely.

    • A declared form archetype. A form is a grammar of what is allowed to recur, contrast, and resolve — strophic, verse-chorus, AABA, rondo, sonata, through-composed, arc, suite, process. Each licenses a different kind of return, and returns are what a choreographer stages: a rondo's refrain is a recurring formation; a sonata's recapitulation is a homecoming the dance must earn; a process form is a ramp with no sections at all. Most real pieces mix archetypes across levels — verse-chorus at the song, sentence/period at the phrase — and the choreographer needs to know the archetype per level to know where the stageable returns are. A score whose archetype is undeclared is a score the dance will fight.3
    • A named arc on a named channel. Every tradition shapes intensity over time, but the shape (monotonic build-and-drop, repeating small lifts, a teleological harmonic resolution, discrete steps, a live-negotiated escalation) and the channel (density, tempo, harmony, texture) both vary — EDM and gamelan arc on density with no harmonic progression at all; the Hindustian jhala arcs on tempo over a static drone; only the sonata family is genuinely harmonic. A choreographer rides the arc, and needs to know which channel is carrying it, so the dance can carry the same one or deliberately a different one. "Where is the climax, and what is building it?" is the single most useful question a choreographer can ask a composer, and the composer should be able to answer it in one sentence.4
    • Regular hypermeter, or marked exceptions. Dancers count in eights because Western music groups bars in fours and eights; a score whose hypermeasures are regular is countable, and one that expands or elides a phrase must say so, because the expansion is exactly where the dancer's count will break. Tchaikovsky's squareness was a virtue; Stravinsky's irregularity needed Rambert.
    • Phrase-level function, not just length. An eight-bar unit is a sentence or a period, and the difference is the difference between an accelerating fragmentation and a question answered; cadence strength tells the dancer how hard a phrase may end. The commons' common-practice note puts it as the score's own contract: a period's antecedent closes weaker than its consequent; never end on a half cadence. A dance that ends a phrase on the music's weak cadence feels unfinished, and the dancer will not know why.5
    • Durations and cue points. Balanchine's two minutes; the oboe the run starts on; the stopwatch length Cunningham's composers were handed. Whatever else the score is, it is a clock the dance shares.

    What the machine lacks¶

    The music-to-dance generators of Part VII are fed audio features — onsets, beats, spectral energy — which is to say they are handed the bottom of this brief and none of the top. They know where the beat is and nothing about whether this is a rondo's refrain or a sonata's recapitulation, whether the arc is on density or harmony, whether this phrase is a question or an answer. The composer's form knowledge is precisely the structural information the generators lack, and precisely what a choreographer uses. Adding it — a form-level conditioning signal, an arc curve, a phrase-function label — is a research program the commons' own notes already sketch from the music side.
