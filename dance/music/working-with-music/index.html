    How choreographers work with music¶

    Part IV · Music and dance — chapter 2 of 3 · ← Choreomusicology: the relationship spectrum · Composing for choreography →

    The previous chapter was about the relationship; this one is about the room. How do dancers and choreographers actually hold a piece of music in the body, rehearse to it, edit it, and perform with it? The practices are old and mostly unwritten, and they fall into three families — counting, breathing, and cueing — that correspond to three different ways of addressing time. Underneath them sits a small amount of rhythm theory and perception research that the commons holds, and that makes sense of habits dancers usually learn as folklore.

    Counts¶

    The universal studio unit is the eight: five, six, seven, eight and go. Ballet counts it, jazz and commercial dance count it, hip-hop counts it, the phrase in most Western dance is some number of eights. Why eight? Because most Western music is built in hypermeasures — bars group into fours and eights the way beats group into bars — and an eight-count is two bars of 4/4 heard as one unit. The count is the music's metric hierarchy taken one level up from the beat, and dancers count at that level because it is the level at which phrases begin and end.1

    Three things about counts are worth making explicit, because they are where studio practice and music theory disagree productively.

    The count is a grid; the phrase is a grouping — and they are independent. Music theory's formal statement (Lerdahl and Jackendoff's Generative Theory of Tonal Music) is that grouping structure — motif, phrase, section — is a separate hierarchy from metrical structure, and a group can begin on a weak beat. Dancers rediscover this constantly: the movement phrase wants to start on and-seven and run across the bar line into the next eight, and a choreographer who chops every phrase to the count produces the squareness that reads as "counting." Good phrasing crosses the counts; the counts are how you find your place, not how you shape the line. Music theory also offers the dancer something sharper than bar numbers: a unit of eight bars is not just "eight bars" but usually a sentence (idea, repeated idea, then a continuation that fragments and accelerates to cadence) or a period (a question-phrase ending weakly, an answer-phrase ending strongly). Choreographers feel this even when they can't name it — the movement that "arrives" on the period's consequent, the accelerating fragmentation that matches the sentence's continuation — and the strongest musical choreographers (Balanchine again) work at exactly this level.2

    Not all meter is divisive eights. The commons' rhythm note separates two axes that most people fold into one time signature. Construction: divisive meter halves and thirds a span (4/4 as 2×2); additive meter concatenates unequal cells — the Balkan aksak of 7/8 as 2+2+3 or 9/8 as 2+2+2+3. Reference: hierarchical meter has strong and weak positions around a downbeat; a timeline — the Afro-Cuban clave, the West African bell pattern — is a fixed asymmetric reference the whole ensemble entrains to, with no privileged beat. Dancers in those traditions count differently because the music is built differently: a Bulgarian rǎchenitsa is danced quick-quick-slow because its 7/8 is 2+2+3, and the step pattern is the meter's cells made visible; salsa dancers argue about "on 1" versus "on 2" because they are choosing which event of a timeline to weight, not which beat of a hierarchy; an Ewe dancer whose feet move in two while the arms move in three is performing the music's cross-rhythm, a 3:2 that is the piece's basis rather than its decoration. The same note distinguishes polyrhythm (two groupings in one span), polymeter (different bar lengths realigning at their common multiple — a six-count phrase against eight-count music meets it every twenty-four, the engine of much Childs and De Keersmaeker), and metric modulation (a shared subdivision carrying the dancer across a tempo change). These are separate settings, and a choreographer working outside Western pop had better know which one the music is using before counting anything.3

    South Asian counting is recited. Kathak and bharatanatyam dancers learn rhythm as spoken syllables — bols, konnakol — and recite the composition (padhant) before dancing it; the tala's cycle is held by the cycle's first beat, sam, which dancer and drummer must arrive on together. Flamenco's twelve-count compás likewise lives in the palms and the mouth before the feet. Counting there is not a grid laid over the music but the music's own grammar spoken aloud; it is the atlas pages' business and the chance chapter's frame-versus-realization distinction in action.

    Breath¶

    Modern dance's alternative to the count was breath phrasing, and it is a genuine different time-mode, not a softer count. Humphrey's fall and recovery is a breath rhythm — the suspension at the top, the fall, the rebound — and her Water Study (1928) was danced with no music at all, the ensemble phrasing on shared breath. Graham's contraction sits on an exhale. Release technique, contact improvisation, and Gaga all phrase from the body's own durations, and much contemporary work is "counted" only in the sense that dancers share an approximate clock while phrasing internally. Breath time is the choreomusicology chapter's suspended mode: no pulse, placement by gesture and contour. Its cost is ensemble precision — which is why breath-phrased companies rehearse unison for months and why even Humphrey used counts to set the ensemble before releasing them to breathe.

    Cues¶

    The third family keys movement to events rather than clock. Ballet rehearsals are full of it — "go on the oboe," "the run starts on the cymbal" — and it is how dancers survive music whose counts are unreliable or absent. Cunningham's company made the limit case a method: since the music was unknown until performance, the dancers timed their phrases with stopwatches, a clock that was neither musical nor bodily but simply elapsed seconds, and cued one another by sight. Cue-based practice is event-driven; count-based is clock-driven; the distinction will matter when we get to the conductor.

    Groove, the pocket, and being "on top"¶

    Dancers talk about being on top of the beat, behind it, in the pocket — and the perception research the commons holds says something counterintuitive about it. Studies of groove find that exaggerating microtiming does not increase groove; it degrades it. A quantized and a naturally performed version can rate equally; groove falls off as timing is pushed beyond a performance's natural magnitudes; expert listeners reject deviations of roughly forty milliseconds that novices accept; and listeners apply genre-appropriate standards (swing and funk are rated comparably despite different absolute timings). Groove emerges from coherence — timing consistent with genre and context — not from the size of the deviation.4 For the dancer the translation is direct: the pocket is not "slightly late"; it is consistently placed relative to the music's own feel, and a dancer who performs lateness as a style reads as dragging. Combine that with the binding asymmetry — late reads wrong faster than early — and the professional convention falls out: place the accent on or a hair ahead of the beat, with the consistency of the music's own players. Balanchine's "on top of the music" was not taste; it was the perceptual sweet spot.

    The pianist and the conductor¶

    Two musicians inhabit the dance studio, and each embodies one family of practice.

    The rehearsal pianist is the ballet world's great unsung collaborator. In class, the pianist improvises — hears the teacher mark a combination ("and a-one, and plié, and stretch…"), infers its meter, tempo, and character, and plays something that fits, live, dozens of times a day. In rehearsal, the pianist plays reductions of the orchestral score at the tempo the choreographer wants, with counts pencilled above the staff and cues marked ("here the boys enter"). Choreographers who can't read music work through the pianist; those who can (Balanchine, Morris, De Keersmaeker) still rely on one. The pianist is, in effect, a human playback system with zero latency and infinite patience, and a good one shapes the phrasing of an entire company.

    The conductor has the harder job, and it is the job the commons' latency note describes precisely. A dance conductor must follow the stage — a ballerina's balance, a jump's preparation — while holding the orchestra together, and the naïve way to do that is to react: watch, then beat. Reaction has a floor. Every stage of any reactive chain costs real time (the note's numbers are for audio-to-visual systems — tens of milliseconds of buffering and analysis, a frame or two of render and display, ~70–100 ms for even a fast chain — but the structure is the same for a human watching a dancer and moving a baton), and by the time the orchestra sounds, the dancer has moved on. The fix is the one the note ranks first: anticipate. A good dance conductor predicts the dancer — reads the preparation and leads the landing — exactly as a predictive beat tracker reports a beat before a causal one could find it. The limit case is also in the note: a pre-authored cue sheet on a known track has zero detection latency, because nothing needs detecting, only playback against a shared clock. That is what a recorded score is, and it is why recorded music, for all its deadness, is so much easier to dance to than a conductor who reacts.5

    Which is the real argument in the live-versus-recorded debate. Mark Morris's insistence on live music is not nostalgia: live musicians and dancers form a feedback loop — the players breathe with the dancers, the dancers lean on the players — and the loop is only good if the musicians anticipate. A conductor who merely follows produces the worst of both worlds: the dead tempo of a recording plus the lag of a reaction.

    The studio: editing, looping, clicking¶

    Modern studio practice is mostly a history of playback technology. The piano reduction gave way to the cassette (rewound a thousand times to the same phrase), then to the CD, then to the laptop and the DAW, where a choreographer now loops eight bars indefinitely, slows a passage without changing pitch, and — increasingly — edits the music: cuts, repeats, reorders, layers. Bausch's collages, Fosse's vamps extended until the number is staged, the contemporary choreographer who trims a commissioned score by a third in the last week: editing is normal, and composers who write for dance know it (the next chapter). Forsythe's practice of having Thom Willems compose in the rehearsal room made the editing simultaneous with the composing.

    Commercial dance adds the click track and in-ear monitors, so that forty dancers in an arena hit unison against a mix they can barely hear; music-video and film dance shoot to playback, one phrase at a time, with the count called over it. And the contemporary concert stage has borrowed the DJ: Sharon Eyal's Ori Lichtik plays live, Hofesh Shechter composes and mixes his own percussion scores, Ohad Naharin assembles and sometimes writes (under a pseudonym) the collages Batsheva dances to. The line between choreographer and music editor has become thin, and the studio's working memory is now a timeline in software.

    Silence¶

    Every family of practice has a silent form — the counted silence, the breathed silence, the cued silence — and choreographers use silence as a musical resource: the held stillness before the drop, the section danced to feet and breath only, Robbins's Moves in its entirety. Silence is where the dancer's own time becomes audible, and where the mute test is run on the audience in real time: does the movement carry its own pacing?

    For the generated dancer¶

    The music-to-dance generators of Part VII count — they consume beat and onset features and learn to accent them — but they neither breathe nor cue, and they are reactive by construction: a frame is generated from the audio up to that frame. This chapter's lessons apply without translation. A generator that accents on the detected beat is late by its detection latency and reads late faster than early; the fix is prediction or a pre-authored cue sheet, which for a known track is trivially available. A generator that exaggerates microtiming for "feel" degrades groove, exactly as a human does. And a generator that cannot phrase across the counts — that cannot begin a movement on and-seven — will produce the squareness every dance teacher has spent a career correcting.
