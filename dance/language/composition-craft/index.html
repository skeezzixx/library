    The craft: composition best practices¶

    Every art accumulates a layer of working wisdom that isn't theory and isn't technique — the things experienced makers tell you at the point where your piece is almost working. Dance is unusually lucky here: its craft lore got written down, by masters, and two texts tower over the genre. Doris Humphrey's The Art of Making Dances (1959) is the canon — the distilled judgment of a first-generation modern choreographer, finished just before her death, and still the book working choreographers actually quote.1 Lynne Anne Blom and L. Tarin Chaplin's The Intimate Act of Choreography (1982) is the standard classroom companion. Add one modern protocol for feedback and you have this chapter: best practices, as documented practice.

    Fair warning about the genre: craft rules are compressions of experience, not laws. Every rule below has been productively broken by someone in the shelf. What makes them worth learning anyway is that each one names a failure mode you will otherwise discover expensively — and that they translate, with almost embarrassing directness, into design heuristics for anyone building a system that composes.

    Humphrey's physics of attention¶

    The most quoted part of Humphrey's book is the blunt checklist near its end, and the items earn their fame:

    Symmetry is lifeless. Two-dimensional design is lifeless. The eye is faster than the ear. All dances are too long. A good ending is forty per cent of the dance. Monotony is fatal; look for contrasts. Don't be a slave to, or a mutilator of, the music. Don't intellectualize; motivate movement. Don't leave the ending to the end.

    What reads like aphorism is really a physics of audience attention, and it repays unpacking.

    Symmetry is lifeless because a symmetric stage picture resolves — nothing is owed, nothing impends. Asymmetry is unfinished business, and unfinished business is watchable. (Her corollary: symmetry spends meaningfully when you want stability, ceremony, an ending — it's lifeless as a default, potent as a choice.)

    The eye is faster than the ear — visual pattern is grasped almost instantly, where musical pattern needs time to unfold. Practical consequence: movement repeated as often as its accompanying music becomes boring sooner; the eye finished earlier. Any system that varies music and movement on the same schedule has this exactly wrong.

    The stage itself has physics. Humphrey mapped the proscenium stage as a field of unequal forces: center stage is power; upstage center is remote majesty (entrances of gods and queens); downstage corners are intimate and confessional; the diagonals are the strongest travel paths. You can defy the map, but the audience reads by it either way — placement is never neutral.

    All dances are too long needs no unpacking. It is the single most confirmed observation in the history of the art form.

    A good ending is forty per cent of the dance — and don't leave the ending to the end: design it early, because everything before it is, structurally, approach. An ending discovered in the final rehearsal week reads exactly like one.

    Phrase-craft¶

    Blom and Chaplin's classroom contribution starts smaller: the phrase as the basic unit of composition — a span of movement with a beginning, a development, and an arrival, most naturally sized to a breath. Their craft rules cluster around two disciplines:

    Shape the phrase's energy deliberately. A phrase has a dynamic arc — where it peaks, how it ends (a punctuating accent versus a dissolve versus a suspension that hands momentum to the next phrase). Uniform arcs are the phrase-level version of Humphrey's monotony warning, and the effort chapter's anti-monotony rule — never more than two consecutive phrases with the same effort signature — is the same discipline made mechanical.

    Develop before you invent. Their deepest advice, and the least intuitive for beginners: when a piece feels thin, the reflex is new material, and the reflex is almost always wrong. A dance usually needs less material, more thoroughly explored — which is what the operator catalog is for. The sixteen manipulations exist so that one good motif can honestly fill ten minutes. A piece built from six ideas lightly touched reads as a variety show; a piece built from one idea genuinely developed reads as a world. (The chance chapter's gamut sizes — always dozens, never hundreds — are this same judgment, measured.)

    The rehearsal room¶

    The craft of making is distinct from the craft of the made thing, and modern practice has conventions worth naming plainly.

    Dances are made on bodies, not on paper. The near-universal contemporary method is to bring structures, tasks, and images into the studio and develop material on the specific dancers — whose bodies, histories, and offers shape the result so strongly that a work re-set on a new cast is understood to be a translation. The purest version is tasking: give the dancers a generative problem ("phrase A backwards at half tempo while descending"; "a solo that never leaves the wall's shadow") and curate what comes back. A choreographer running tasks is, in this subject's terms, precisely a sequencer plus a human gate — the dancers are the generator, and the craft is in the task design and the selection.

    Video replaced notation as working memory. The universal modern habit: film everything, because tomorrow nobody — including the choreographer — fully remembers today's material. (What video doesn't capture is the subject of the notation chapters, coming.)

    Marking is a tool, not a failure. Dancers rehearse structure at reduced energy ("marking") to save bodies; choreographers must see full-out runs to judge dynamics, because — Humphrey again — movement reads slower and weaker from the house than it feels on the floor.

    Editing, and the outside eye¶

    Humphrey's "all dances are too long" implies the discipline: cutting is where pieces are made. The craft lore here is identical to every other art's — kill your darlings; the section you love most is the first suspect — with one dance-specific sharpening: length errors are usually repetition-count errors. The material is right; it happens four times instead of two. (Recall the eye's speed.)

    Because the maker's eye adapts — you cannot see your piece by week six — mature practice builds in an outside eye. At the institutional end this is the dance dramaturg (Pina Bausch's Raimund Hoghe is the famous case; Anne Teresa De Keersmaeker has worked with Bojana Cvejić): a professional watcher whose job is the piece's logic, not its steps. At the collegial end it's a trusted colleague at a late run-through. Either way the principle is the same: fresh perception is a resource the maker cannot self-supply.

    Feedback: the Lerman protocol¶

    Which raises the hardest craft problem in any collaborative art: how to receive opinions without surrendering the work. Dance's canonical answer — now exported to theaters, art schools, and writing workshops everywhere — is choreographer Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process, a four-step structure for feedback sessions:2

    1. Statements of meaning. Responders say what was meaningful, evocative, striking — before any evaluation. (The artist learns what actually landed, which is data no opinion contains.)
    2. The artist asks. The artist questions the responders — about the passages they are unsure of, on their agenda, not the room's.
    3. Neutral questions. Responders may ask genuinely neutral questions ("what governed the costume choice?" — not "why was the costume grey?", which smuggles a verdict). Forming a truly neutral question forces the responder to locate their reaction before broadcasting it.
    4. Permissioned opinions. "I have an opinion about the ending — would you like to hear it?" The artist may decline.

    The design insight is that CRP treats the artist's continued authority over the work as the resource to protect — unsolicited fixes are the failure mode, because they quietly transfer authorship to the loudest responder. Readers of the architecture chapter will recognize the structure: CRP is a human gate with a designed protocol, engineered so the gatekeeper stays the artist.

    The craft table, translated¶

    Because this library's reader builds generative systems, the translation deserves to be explicit. Each craft rule is a system heuristic wearing rehearsal clothes:

    Craft rule System analogue
    All dances are too long Aggressive length priors; generate short, extend only on evidence
    Symmetry is lifeless (by default) Penalize resolved/symmetric states except at designated cadences
    The eye is faster than the ear Visual material needs a faster variation schedule than its music
    Design the ending early Plan form backward from the ending, not forward from the opening
    Monotony is fatal Enforce contrast floors (the effort-signature rule, mechanized)
    Develop before you invent Exhaust the operator catalog before enlarging the gamut
    Stage geography Placement priors — position carries meaning before movement starts
    The outside eye Fresh-judge evaluation: never validate on the maker's adapted eye
    Critical Response Process Gate protocol: meaning-data first, opinions by permission, artist keeps authority

    None of this replaces judgment — Humphrey's whole book is a plea for judgment against formula. But it is the accumulated record of where judgment usually errs, and that, for makers and for system-builders alike, is exactly what best practices are.
