    Codified vocabularies¶

    Part II · Movement language — chapter 1 of 6 · ← Street and club styles · Technique as vocabulary →

    Before any question about generating movement comes a stranger fact worth sitting with: several of the world's dance traditions long ago did to movement what language did to sound. They drew boundaries in the continuous flow of the body, named the segments, and built a culture of transmission on the names. A ballet class in Tokyo, Buenos Aires, and Paris runs on the same French words; a bharatanatyam dancer's hands speak a gesture lexicon fixed in treatises two thousand years old; a b-boy's six-step is as precisely specified as a tendu, with no academy anywhere in sight.

    This chapter is about what codification is, what it buys, what it costs, and how differently it can be governed — questions that turn out to matter enormously the moment you want a machine anywhere near choreography, because a codified vocabulary is, among other things, a dataset with labels.

    Ballet: the state-sponsored lexicon¶

    The deepest codification project in Western dance began as absolutist politics. Louis XIV — a serious dancer who performed as the sun in Ballet de la Nuit — chartered the Académie Royale de Danse in 1661, and his ballet master Pierre Beauchamp fixed the five positions of the feet that still open every ballet class on earth. By 1700, Raoul-Auger Feuillet had published Chorégraphie — the word originally named a notation system — and the French terms were on paper: plié, tendu, jeté, arabesque, pirouette.1

    Today the working lexicon runs to a few hundred named steps, and the names are doing at least four jobs at once. Transmission: the vocabulary crossed three centuries and every border without losing definition. Correction: a teacher can say "your arabesque is behind your shoulder" only because arabesque picks out a thing with a right way to be. Chunking: a dancer's working memory holds a phrase as six names, not six hundred joint angles — the same compression trick as chess players seeing structures instead of pieces. Assessment: entire examination systems (the Royal Academy of Dance syllabus, the Vaganova method's graded levels) exist because the units are enumerable.

    Note what the lexicon deliberately excludes, though — a detail the gamut chapter should make ring loudly. Ballet names its positions and steps, but the connective tissue between them lives in the training, not the list. And note that ballet is not one vocabulary but a family of dialects: Vaganova, Cecchetti, French school, Balanchine's American inflection — same core lexicon, systematically different accents, arms, and musicality. Vocabularies drift, even policed ones.

    The mudra systems: when gestures mean¶

    Indian classical dance codified earlier, deeper, and toward a different end. The Natya Shastra — the Sanskrit performance treatise compiled somewhere around the turn of the first millennium — and the later Abhinaya Darpana specify the hasta mudras: twenty-eight single-hand gestures plus around two dozen two-hand combinations, each named, each specified finger by finger, each carrying meanings.2 Pataka (the flat hand) can be a forest, a river, denial, or a slap, depending on context and placement; the same physical sign is reused across semantic fields the way a phoneme is.

    This is the crucial difference from ballet: bharatanatyam's vocabulary is semantic. The tradition splits its own practice into nritta — pure dance, rhythm and geometry, built from unit steps called adavus (about a dozen families, over a hundred variants, drilled like scales) — and abhinaya, expressive narration, where the mudras function as a sign language telling stories from the epics. A single dancer narrates: gods, demons, lovers, weather. Ballet's names point at shapes; the mudras point at the world.

    Hawaiian hula sits in the same family with a different emphasis: the hands depict — moon, wave, cliff, rain — but the dance exists to carry the mele, the chant; movement is the memory palace of an oral literature. Transmission is institutional but familial: the hālau (school) under a kumu hula, lineages guarded like archives, because for centuries the dance largely was the archive.

    Flamenco: a grammar with few nouns¶

    Flamenco is the instructive counterexample: a rigorously codified tradition with surprisingly few named units. What's fixed is the palo — the song form, with its compás (the rhythmic cycle, like the twelve-beat of soleá) — and a set of structural events: the llamada (the dancer's call for attention), the remate (the phrase-closing punctuation), the desplante. The movement material itself — braceo (arm work), floreo (wrist and finger circling), zapateado (footwork) — is described by manner more than enumerated by name. A bailaora doesn't execute step #47; she improvises within compás, in conversation with singer and guitarist, deploying a personal vocabulary shaped by convention.

    Flamenco codifies the frame and leaves the material personal — the exact inverse of ballet, and a live demonstration of the frame-versus-realization split the chance chapter found running through jazz, raga, and Cunningham alike. A vocabulary, it turns out, can live in the nouns or in the grammar.

    The street: canon without an academy¶

    The most remarkable codification story is the most recent, because it happened with no academy, no state, no notation, and no tuition — and produced a vocabulary as stable as any of them.

    Breaking, born in the Bronx in the 1970s, organized itself into a canon any practitioner worldwide can recite: toprock (standing opener), downrock (floorwork — the six-step is its scale exercise, as codified as any adavu), freezes (held shapes — baby freeze, chair), power moves (windmill, flare, headspin). Popping arrived from Fresno with Boogaloo Sam and the Electric Boogaloos carrying its own named techniques — the pop itself, waving, tutting; locking from Don Campbell in LA — the lock, points, wrist rolls. Each with named founders, named moves, and ferociously maintained boundaries (conflating popping and locking, or calling any of it "breakdancing," marks an outsider instantly).

    The governance mechanism is the cypher and the battle: canon is maintained by competitive consensus — you bite (copy without transformation), the circle knows; you innovate on the foundation, the circle ratifies it, and your move may enter the canon with your name attached. Transmission ran through crews and VHS tapes, then YouTube, which acted as an accidental global academy — and the whole arc reached a surreal milestone when breaking, an art form born on cardboard, debuted at the Paris Olympics in 2024, judging criteria and all. Institutions came to the vocabulary, half a century after the vocabulary organized itself without them.

    What codification buys, costs, and decides¶

    Lay the traditions side by side and the anatomy of codification comes clear.

    What naming buys is the same everywhere: chunking (working memory holds names, not joint angles), correction (you can only fix what you can point at), transmission across time and distance, assessment, and — subtlest — remix affordance: units with names and boundaries can be recombined, quoted, and taught out of order. Every generative system in Part V is downstream of this property.

    What it costs is thinkability. A vocabulary is a hypothesis about which distinctions matter, and it quietly makes everything between its entries harder to see, teach, or ask for. Ballet's lexicon has no word for most of what Pina Bausch put on stage. This cost is real enough that an entire century of dance — the next chapter — is best read as a series of responses to it, and one of them (Gaga) responds by refusing names on principle.

    And someone decides. An académie under royal charter, a sabha of gurus and treatises, a family lineage, a cypher's rolling consensus — each governance model produces a different kind of vocabulary: ballet's is precise and slow-moving; the street's is precise and fast, absorbing innovation in years rather than generations; flamenco's guards the grammar and frees the nouns. When you build a vocabulary of your own — a gamut — you are implicitly choosing a governance model too, even if the whole académie is you.
