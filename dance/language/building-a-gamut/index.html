    Building a gamut¶

    Choreographers rarely make dances out of "movement" in general. They make a gamut — a finite, named set of movement units built for one piece — and then compose with that. The word is Cunningham's, borrowed from music, and the practice is close to universal even among choreographers who'd never use the term: Trisha Brown's Primary Accumulation is thirty movements; Cunningham's Torse is sixty-four phrases expanded from five torso positions; Lucinda Childs built entire works from a single step pattern permuted through space. If you've ever designed a synth patch bank for one track, or a constrained palette for one visual piece, you know the move: the vocabulary is a composition decision, made before composing begins.

    This chapter is about how that vocabulary gets built well — which turns out to be a genuine design discipline with named failure modes, most of them invisible until you try to recombine the units and things break. The dance tradition worked these problems out by hand over decades; the same lessons apply directly if the thing recombining your units is a rule system or a model.

    Start from a coverage checklist, not from taste¶

    The naive way to build a vocabulary is to improvise material until you have "enough," then name what you kept. The predictable result is a set that clusters around its author's habits — twenty units that are secretly five, all sharing a dynamic range, a level, a relationship to weight. Any generator downstream (human or machine) can then only recombine within that cluster.

    The corrective is an independent checklist of movement classes, scored against your set. The best-established one is Ann Hutchinson Guest's Movement Alphabet1: a claimed-complete list of fifteen "prime actions from which all movement derives," in six groups — any action, stillness · flexion, extension, rotation · travelling, direction · support, spring · balance, falling · motion toward, motion away, destination, any form of relating. Treat completeness as a strong pedagogical claim rather than a theorem (it has been taught for forty years, which counts for something), and it becomes exactly what a vocabulary-builder needs: fill the empty cells, and your set spans the space of movement instead of sampling one corner. (The alphabet also has a plate — the fifteen classes laid out visually, for exactly the kind of staring the list rewards.)

    Look closely at the list, though, and five of the fifteen aren't performable actions at all. Motion toward, motion away, destination, and form of relating are specification modes — ways of defining a movement (by its trajectory, by its endpoint, by its relationship) rather than movements themselves. Strip those out and the performable core is about ten verb classes: travel, turn, spring, gesture into a direction, flex, extend, transfer weight, balance, fall, be still. Ten verbs sounds small. It's the right order of magnitude — the historical gamuts that worked are all counted in dozens, never hundreds, a point the systems chapters return to with the receipts.

    State-changing verbs must be first-class units¶

    The subtlest thing about Guest's alphabet is what it chooses to promote. Support (a weight transfer), balance, and falling are alphabet entries — full verbs — rather than adverbs or transitions hiding between other moves. That looks like a taxonomic quirk and is actually the deepest design rule in the chapter:

    anything that changes state that other units will read deserves its own named unit.

    Fall is the canonical case. A fall isn't just a shape in time — it writes state: the body ends off-balance, with momentum, and only certain movements can legally happen next. If your vocabulary treats falling as something that just happens inside transitions, no rule system can see that state, and sequences will be generated that a body cannot perform. Worse, falls create an obligation: for every direction your vocabulary can fall, it needs at least one recovery unit that reads the off-balance state and resolves it. A vocabulary with falls and no recoveries deadlocks — the generator paints the dancer into corners.

    This is a special case of a distinction worth making for the whole set: which units are atomic (self-contained, no dependencies — a gesture, a turn in place) and which are entangled (they read or write state that other units depend on — falls, catches, weight transfers, anything with momentum). Atomic units can be recombined freely. Entangled units need their dependencies declared, or recombination breaks in ways that only show up two units downstream. Map this before composing, not after.

    And one more promotion from the alphabet worth honoring: stillness is an item, not a gap. A rest is a unit with duration, level, and shape — Cage's lesson transplanted to the body. It also happens to be the single most useful edit point when movement is destined for camera, which is worth remembering the moment your dancers are synthetic.

    Give every unit a type signature¶

    The practical form of "declare the dependencies" is borrowed from programming: give each unit a type signature — what it requires to begin (preconditions) and what's true when it ends (postconditions). For movement, six fields cover the state that matters:

    Field What it records
    support which base bears weight — feet, one foot, seat, back, hands+feet…
    level floor / low / mid / standing / air
    balance balanced, suspended, off-balance (and which way)
    momentum magnitude and direction, including rotational
    facing relative orientation change, never absolute
    free limbs what's available for the next unit to use

    Then unit B may follow unit A exactly when A's postconditions satisfy B's preconditions, field by field, with wildcards where a unit genuinely doesn't care. Legality stops being a judgment call and becomes checkable by inspection — which is the entire foundation the gamut+rules architecture builds on.

    Two field-specific notes, both earned the hard way. Weight is the treacherous field: dance pedagogy independently names incomplete weight transfer as the root cause of most transition failures, so annotate support changes with special care. And facing must be stored relative — a unit that "ends facing stage left" is really a unit that ends rotated 90° from where it began; store the absolute version and you'll reject joins that were fine and duplicate units to compensate.

    One honest caveat from the notation world: every system surveyed, Guest's included, carries a standing bias. If floorwork matters to your vocabulary — and in contemporary movement it will — don't inherit a default support list; name the floor supports explicitly (back, side, seat, hands-and-feet) as first-class values.

    The operator layer: transforms, not more units¶

    A gamut of a few dozen units would exhaust itself quickly if units were all there was. The second layer is operators: transformations applied to units to generate variants. Dance pedagogy converged on a remarkably stable catalog decades ago — Blom & Chaplin's sixteen ways to manipulate a motif2 (repetition, retrograde, inversion, size, tempo, rhythm, quality, instrumentation, force, background, staging, embellishment, change of level, additive, fragmentation, combination), with William Forsythe's ~thirty geometric operations — extension, extrusion, folding, bridging, avoidance, reorientation, compression — as the improvisation-side twin.3

    The structural fact that matters more than either list: operators are material-independent. Forsythe's catalog reads like a graphics API precisely because it applies to whatever the dancer already knows — the vocabulary and the operator set are orthogonal layers. That's why roughly thirty concepts constitute a literacy a practitioner internalizes rather than a repertoire they memorize, and it's the standard to hold your own operators to: an operator that only works on one unit isn't an operator, it's a variant that snuck into the wrong layer.

    Two constraints keep an operator layer sound:

    • Closure — applying any operator to legal material must yield legal material. An operator that can produce output outside the vocabulary's own rules poisons everything downstream.
    • Survivability — the operand must remain itself under the transform. A reaching curve compresses beautifully; a walking step compresses into nonsense. Part of authoring a unit is knowing which operators it survives.

    Not all operators are equally automatable¶

    If a rule system (or an agent) will be applying these operators, the catalog needs one more sorting: by mechanizability — how much judgment the operator secretly requires. The sixteen sort cleanly into three tiers:

    • Tier 1 — fully mechanical. Repetition ×n, tempo change including freeze, re-facing and relocation, level transposition, mirroring, size augmentation/diminution, retrograde. Safe to auto-apply.
    • Tier 2 — mechanical plus a light curation pass. Fragmentation (take the first, middle, or last third), re-accenting within the same duration, flipping one effort quality bit, instrumentation (move the phrase to a different body part — undefined for weight-bearing units), appending an ornament.
    • Tier 3 — judgment-heavy. Whole-quality reimagining, inversion, redesigning the background behind a motif, stacking several operators at once. Offer these as prompts to a human; don't auto-apply them.

    The tiering isn't invented — it's empirical. Every teaching syllabus carries tiers 1–2; only the full scholarly catalog carries tier 3. And it comes with a trap worth framing on the wall: mechanizable to specify is not the same as feasible to execute. Retrograde is the cleanest tier-1 operator on paper and one of the hardest things a body can be asked to do with any unit that bears weight or carries momentum — playing a fall backwards is not a fall. A catalog inherited from pedagogy encodes what a trained human can be asked to attempt, not what a generator can safely emit; check every "mechanical" operator against the physics before trusting its tier, and restrict the rough ones to gesture-dominant material.

    What a finished gamut looks like¶

    Pulling the threads together, a well-built gamut is:

    1. Small — dozens of units, chosen, not harvested;
    2. Spanning — scored against an independent class list, empty cells filled deliberately;
    3. Stateful — state-changing verbs promoted to first-class units, entanglements mapped, every fall paired with recoveries;
    4. Typed — each unit carrying pre/postconditions over support, level, balance, momentum, relative facing, free limbs;
    5. Transformable — a separate, closed operator layer, tiered by how much judgment each operator hides.

    None of this yet says how units get sequenced, what makes a chain of them legal in practice, or who judges the result — that's the architecture chapter. And the question of how a unit is performed, as opposed to what it is, has its own axis entirely: effort and quality, next.
