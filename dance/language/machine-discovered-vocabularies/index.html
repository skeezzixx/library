    Machine-discovered vocabularies¶

    Everything so far in this part has had a human building the vocabulary — naming it like ballet, training it into bodies like Graham, assembling it by hand like a gamut. The question this chapter takes seriously is whether the building itself can be delegated: point a model at hours of dance video and ask it to find the units — no labels, no choreographer, no Movement Alphabet — the way one might hope a model would find the phonemes of an unfamiliar language from raw speech.

    The honest headline for mid-2026: yes, at the level of recurring poses and recurring short segments — and no, at the level of named, typed, performable units. The machine finds clusters; a human still decides what is a unit. That gap is the interesting part, and understanding exactly where it sits is what lets you build a pipeline that uses discovery for what it's good at. Let me take the field apart by stages and by method family, then say plainly what a working system would look like.

    The pipeline, in four stages¶

    Every discovery approach decomposes the same way:

    • Capture — video to skeleton, the business of the captured-body chapter: 2D keypoints or, better, world-grounded 3D bodies.
    • Tokenize or segment — turn the continuous skeleton stream into candidate pieces: either quantize each moment into a finite set of codes, or find boundaries that cut the stream into segments.
    • Cluster — group the pieces into recurring types, so that "this kind of thing" becomes a countable category.
    • Name and type — give the categories names a dancer can use and signatures a rule system can check. This is the stage no published method performs.

    The first three stages have real, working literature. The fourth is the human gate, and it has not moved.

    Family one: codebooks of poses¶

    The dominant method is the VQ-VAE — a vector-quantized autoencoder. Train a network to compress a short window of pose through a bottleneck, but force the bottleneck to pick its value from a finite codebook of learned entries. Every moment of movement is then represented by an index into that codebook, and each codebook entry — each code — is a discovered unit pose.

    The dance-specific proof is Bailando (2022), a music-to-dance system whose authors named its vocabulary component with unusual candor: the choreographic memory.1 Its design choices are worth reading as vocabulary decisions:

    • It trains separate codebooks for the upper and lower body, so any dance becomes a sequence of paired codes — a limb-instrumentation split, made structural.
    • The learned codes are human-interpretable: the paper shows them, and they read as recognizable dancing-style poses, discovered with no labels at all.
    • A GPT then composes codes into sequences conditioned on music, with an actor-critic term rewarding beat alignment — and the authors sell the whole thing as interpretability: "the choreography process becomes selecting and sorting the quantized positions from the choreographic memory, instead of a black box."

    Hold that last sentence; it's going to matter.

    The general motion-tokenizer lineage — built for text-to-motion, but the tokenizers are agnostic to what drives them — shows where the engineering went next:

    • T2M-GPT — plain VQ tokens; a compact discrete space that generates well but forces fine detail into a finite codebook.
    • MoMask (2024) — residual vector quantization: several codebooks stacked, each encoding what the previous one missed, which cuts reconstruction error sharply.2
    • 2026 hybrids — DC-Motion keeps discrete tokens for structure and a continuous residual for detail; causal tokenizers stream; language-guided tokenizers shape the codebook toward nameable concepts.3

    The trade-off the tokenizer literature states outright is one you already know from the chance chapter: a small discrete codebook is easier to compose with but loses detail; a large one keeps detail but stops being a vocabulary. That is gamut sizing — dozens, not hundreds — rediscovered as a reconstruction-error curve.

    Family two: discovering segments¶

    A pose is not a gesture. A gesture is a span with a beginning and an end, and the second family goes after exactly that boundary problem — unsupervised temporal segmentation of skeleton streams.

    The 2025 method to know is skeleton motion words.4 A sequence-to-sequence temporal autoencoder learns a latent representation that keeps the joints disentangled; the latent sequence is cut into short patches and quantized, yielding discrete "motion words"; clustering the words then recovers semantically meaningful action segments — without labels, and without anyone deciding the segment length in advance. A 2026 successor makes the quantization hierarchical across space and time, so coarse units decompose into finer ones — a first, data-driven hint at the unit/modifier layering the gamut chapter built by hand.5 Beneath these sit the earlier rungs: Motion2Language's unsupervised segmentation synchronized to language, frame-wise discrete-feature structure extraction, and the classic hidden-Markov-model and hierarchical-clustering primitive-discovery work of the 2000s and 2010s.6 None of it is dance-specific; all of it runs on the skeleton streams dance capture produces.

    What the machines keep rediscovering¶

    Step back from the two families and a pattern jumps out that should feel familiar by now. Every one of these systems that generates well has re-derived the two-layer architecture of the chance chapter from data: a codebook — small, finite, inspectable, the gamut — plus a sequencer (a GPT, a masked transformer) that decides only continuity. Bailando's "selecting and sorting units" is Cunningham's charts with the charts learned. Nobody set out to rebuild the gamut+sequencer split; it's what works.

    And the same step back shows exactly what the machines do not supply — which is the list of everything a gamut needs beyond a pile of clusters:

    • Names. Codes are indices. A dancer cannot be told "do 47."
    • Type signatures. No code carries pre- and postconditions; legality is learned implicitly by the sequencer, which means it can't be checked, only trusted.
    • The unit boundary. Is the unit a pose, a patch, a phrase? Each family picks a granularity by architecture, and the choice is a design decision no model makes for you.
    • Performability. A frequently recurring cluster is not thereby a masterable unit — it may be a transition, an artifact of the capture, or a fragment of something larger.

    Each of those is a job the architecture chapter assigned to the human tier, and discovery leaves every one of them there.

    How you'd know a discovered vocabulary is good¶

    There is no standard evaluation — which is itself useful to know, because it means you get to assemble one from first principles, and the principles are already in this part:

    • Coverage — score the clusters against an independent class list; Guest's Movement Alphabet is sitting right there as the checklist. Empty cells tell you what the corpus, or the method, can't see.
    • Stability — the same vocabulary should reappear across random seeds and across different corpora of the same style; a vocabulary that changes every run is noise with labels.
    • Interpretability and performability — can a dancer name the centroid of each cluster and perform it? This is a human check, and it's decisive.
    • Downstream — does generating over the codebook beat generating over raw poses? If the units don't help the sequencer, they aren't units.
    • Size — a usable vocabulary lands in the dozens. A 512-entry codebook is a feature space, not a gamut, until it has been clustered again — and that second clustering is where taste re-enters.

    What it would take¶

    Put the pieces together and a working discovery pipeline is not speculative — it is a sequence of existing components with a human gate in the right place:

    1. Capture dance video into world-grounded skeletons (GVHMR for archival footage, FreeMocap for your own room).
    2. Discover candidates with both families — a pose codebook for the unit-pose layer, motion words for segments — and keep the candidate count generous.
    3. Cluster down to dozens with the Movement Alphabet as coverage scorer, so the result spans rather than clusters around whatever the corpus over-represents.
    4. Gate: a dancer or choreographer names each candidate, performs it, and prunes — the machine clusters, the human decides what is a unit.
    5. Type the survivors with pre/post signatures (support, level, balance, momentum, facing, free limbs) and build the legality graph.

    Discovery, in other words, is best understood as a proposal generator for a gamut — it replaces the blank page, not the choreographer. That's a real gain: the blank page is where hand-built vocabularies cluster around one taste, and a model that has watched a thousand hours proposes cells the author would never have filled. It also reframes the latent-spaces chapter: a codebook is precisely a latent space discretized into a gamut — the hybrid position that chapter argued was the interesting one, with the discretization now learned.
