    Technique as vocabulary¶

    The previous chapter ended on the cost of a lexicon: it quietly decides what is thinkable. Modern dance is what happened when a generation of American and European choreographers found ballet's vocabulary unthinkable for them — wrong body, wrong subjects, wrong relationship to gravity — and, instead of extending the word-list, did something structurally stranger: each built a technique, a daily training system that produces a body with particular capacities and tendencies, and let that trained body be the vocabulary.

    The distinction is worth holding precisely. Ballet stores its vocabulary in names — a lexicon any trained body can execute. A modern technique stores its vocabulary in the instrument itself: train Graham technique for a decade and your torso can do things — and reaches for things — that an equally virtuosic ballet body doesn't. Three storage formats for movement vocabulary run through this whole subject: the name-list, the trained body, and (held for later) the operator set. This chapter is the second one.

    Graham: the dramatic torso¶

    Martha Graham's technique is built on one mechanical seed: contraction and release. The contraction is a deep, breath-initiated hollowing of the torso — pelvis curling under, spine curving, the body's center gripping as if punched or sobbing — and the release is its opening. Add the spiral (rotation organized around the spine) and a deliberate return of dance to the floor — falls, kneels, ground work, at a time when ballet treated the floor as merely what you rise from — and you have an instrument purpose-built for her repertoire's subject matter: Greek tragedy, psychological extremity, desire and dread made muscular.

    The historical irony: by the time of her death the "rebellion" was a syllabus. Graham technique is taught worldwide with named exercises in fixed sequence — bounces, breathings, spirals, pleadings — as codified as anything in the Vaganova method. A technique that produces a distinctive body becomes a lexicon over time, because pedagogy demands units. Codification, apparently, is less a choice than a fate.

    Humphrey and Limón: the body as pendulum¶

    Doris Humphrey — Graham's great contemporary, and the author of the craft bible the best practices chapter leans on — seeded her technique differently: fall and recovery, which she framed as the drama of movement itself, "the arc between two deaths." The vertical stillness of perfect balance and the horizontal stillness of collapse are both inert; everything alive happens between them — the yielding to gravity, the catch, the rebound, the suspension at the top of the arc where weight goes briefly silent. Weight, breath rhythm, swing, momentum: the technique trains a body that rides physics rather than defying it (ballet's whole aesthetic of effortless verticality being precisely the defiance).

    Her dancer José Limón built the now-dominant refinement — Limón technique — organizing the same physics around breath and the sensation of weight moving through body parts in succession. For a builder of movement systems the Humphrey–Limón line is the important one, because it puts momentum and gravity into the vocabulary itself — exactly the state the gamut chapter insisted must be first-class, discovered here as an aesthetic rather than an annotation.

    Cunningham: the chance-ready body¶

    Merce Cunningham's technique reads differently once you know (from the chance chapter) what it was for. He needed dancers who could survive his sequencer: any movement following any other, continuity decided by hexagram. So the technique manufactures the necessary affordances: a strong vertical ballet-like leg base underneath a spine treated as a limb — his phrase — with the torso trained through enumerated positions (upright, curve, arch, twist, tilt) that could be recombined with any step, any direction, any rhythm. Clarity over expressivity; every direction equally "front" (chance decides facing, so the body must be omnidirectional); no movement allowed to depend on an emotional preparation, because its neighbor tomorrow might be anything.

    It is the clearest case in dance history of a technique reverse-engineered from a compositional system — the training exists so that the generator's output is performable. Any synthetic-dance pipeline contains the same dependency, usually undeclared: what the generator may emit is bounded by what the executing instrument was trained on.

    Release and contact: subtracting the vocabulary¶

    The postmodern generation — Judson and after — turned the critique inward: if Graham's technique encodes Graham's drama in every trained torso, then technique itself is a style you can't take off. Two responses became traditions of their own.

    Release technique — descending from Mabel Todd's The Thinking Body through Skinner Releasing and into the training of most contemporary dancers today — trains subtraction: skeletal alignment, efficiency, imagery ("the skull floats"), letting go of held tension, so that movement arrives with minimal stylistic residue. A body trained to be less marked — the pedestrian, unforced look that defines so much contemporary European stage dance descends from this.

    Contact improvisation, initiated by Steve Paxton in 1972, removes the vocabulary's other pillar: pre-composition. Two bodies share weight through a rolling point of contact, and the material is whatever physics offers — falls, lifts, counterbalances — discovered live, never fixed. There are trainable skills (giving weight, following the contact point, falling safely) but deliberately no steps. It survives worldwide as a practice with no repertoire at all: the limiting case of technique-as-vocabulary, where the vocabulary has been dissolved entirely into capacity.

    Gaga: the anti-vocabulary with a vocabulary problem¶

    The most discussed contemporary case is Gaga, Ohad Naharin's movement language, developed at Batsheva Dance Company. Its classes ban mirrors, visitors, and demonstration-and-copy; instead a teacher speaks a continuous stream of sensation images — float, quake, melt, groove, flesh hanging on bone, effort traveling to the fingertips — and dancers research the sensations in their own bodies, never converging on a shared shape.1 It is explicitly an anti-vocabulary: no named positions, no correct form, nothing to copy.

    And yet: Batsheva dancers are recognizable across a stage in seconds — the loosed spines, the grounded volatility, the fast redistribution of effort. Refusing shape-names while training shared sensation-names simply relocates the vocabulary one layer down, from geometry to texture. (The reader of the effort chapter will recognize the layer: Gaga's words live on roughly the axis Laban factored — quality, not position.) The honest summary is that Gaga is a codified vocabulary of qualities with an enforced norm against codifying shapes — a fascinating data point that the vocabulary layer is inescapable, and the only real choice is which layer you name.

    Forsythe: the vocabulary of operations¶

    The third storage format, previewed. William Forsythe's Improvisation Technologies trains neither names nor a particular body but a set of operations — extend a line, fold it, extrude it, avoid it, reorient it — applicable to any material the dancer already has. A dancer literate in the ~thirty operations carries a generative system, not a repertoire; aim it at ballet vocabulary (Forsythe's own dancers were classical) and you get his hyperextended, off-axis deconstructions; aim it at pedestrian movement and you get something else entirely.

    The gamut chapter treated this catalog as engineering material — the operator layer, with its tiers and closure rules. Seen from this chapter's angle it is the endpoint of a century's argument: vocabulary stored as names (ballet), then as bodies (Graham to Gaga), then as transformations — which is, not coincidentally, the layer a machine can hold most naturally, and the doorway to the latent-space question of whether the material itself can go continuous.

    The lesson for instrument-builders¶

    Every technique in this chapter is a claim shaped like: train these capacities daily, and a recognizable movement world becomes available. Abstract one step and it applies to any generative instrument: the training regime is a vocabulary decision. A model's "technique" is its corpus and objectives — what it practiced daily — and its affordances and habits follow from that as surely as the Graham torso follows from the bounces. Cunningham had to invent a technique so his generator's output would be performable; builders of synthetic dancers inherit the same problem with the arrow reversed: the generator's habits are set by what its body of training could do. Choose the practice, and you have — silently — chosen the style.
