    Dance¶

    Choreography solved a problem seventy years before anyone asked a machine to: how to build a limited vocabulary of movement and a set of rules that recombine it into something worth watching. This subject covers the art form whole — the traditions and their makers, the languages of movement and the systems for writing them down, the strange marriage with music, the chance procedures and algorithms, the dressed and lit body on a stage, and the fully synthetic dancer — but that solved problem is its center of gravity, and the chapters radiate out from it.

    The shape of the subject¶

    Eight parts. Chapters are self-contained — you can read any one cold — but within a part they're ordered, and the parts themselves zoom from the territory down to the machinery:

    • I · The territory — what dance is and what it's for; a world atlas of everything anyone calls dance; ballet's long history; the modern revolt; street and club styles. (coming)
    • II · Movement language — how vocabularies of movement get built, taught, described, and — the open frontier — discovered by machines from video. (complete)
    • III · Writing it down — Labanotation, Benesh, motif notation; video and digital scores. (coming)
    • IV · Music and dance — both directions of the marriage: choreographers using music, composers writing for bodies. (coming)
    • V · Chance, systems, algorithms — Cunningham to code: the generative tradition and the architecture it converged on. (complete)
    • VI · The staged body — costume, props, environment, light. (complete)
    • VII · The synthetic dancer — the plausible body, motion capture, movement generation, rendering, the pipeline end to end. (complete)
    • VIII · The shelf — reference pages on the choreographers themselves. (coming)

    Available now¶

    Movement language (read in order, or dip in):

    • Codified vocabularies — what happens when a tradition names its movements: ballet's lexicon, the mudra systems, hula, flamenco, and the street's uninstitutional canon.
    • Technique as vocabulary — modern dance's alternative: build a training system that produces a body, and let the body be the vocabulary.
    • The craft: composition best practices — Humphrey's principles, phrase-craft, the rehearsal room, editing, and Lerman's feedback protocol.
    • Building a gamut — how a piece-specific movement vocabulary is actually assembled: units, state, operators, and the traps between them.
    • Effort and quality — Laban's answer to a question most vocabularies forget to ask: not what the body does, but how.
    • Machine-discovered vocabularies — can a machine find the units from video, unsupervised? Yes for poses and segments, not yet for named, typed units — what works, and what a working pipeline would look like.

    Chance, systems, algorithms:

    • Chance and rule-based choreography — Cunningham's coin tosses, Brown's accumulations, Childs's permutations, Halprin's cycles: what seventy years of generative choreography actually did, and the two-layer architecture all of it shares.
    • The gamut+rules architecture — the engineering synthesis: transition legality, the three transition policies, the human gate, and why the rendering of a machine's proposal is a design decision.
    • Computational choreography: a history — thirty-five years of choreographers composing with computers, LifeForms to AISOMA, and the five patterns that held the whole way.
    • Choreographing in latent spaces — the continuous counter-position: learned motion manifolds, their pre-mathematical ancestors, and what survives the loss of the unit.
    • Locomotion without a choreographer — where motion comes from when nobody writes it: mechanics, oscillators, evolution, optimization, objectives, rules, repertoires — for humanoid and non-humanoid bodies, and the non-human performers already on stage.

    The staged body (read in order):

    • Dance costume: a history — four centuries of what dancers wore, read as a history of what the body was permitted to do: the Sun King's skirts, the tutu as machine, the painters' theatre, the pedestrian revolt, the couture return.
    • Dressing the genre — why each genre wears what it wears: the tutu, the pleated fan, the tracksuit, the tango slit, the fringe — as affordance, signal, and picture; then costume for the camera, and the wardrobe of a rendered dancer.
    • Props and environment — the set as picture, terrain, and partner: Noguchi and Graham, Cunningham's independent events, Bausch's dirt and water, Brown on rooftops, the object as vocabulary — and the rendered stage's one hard rule.
    • Light and the rest of the picture — lighting as dance's second choreography (side light, the cue as time, Rosenthal to Tipton), hair and makeup as genre and as vocabulary, the five rooms, and what the rendered picture inherits.

    The synthetic dancer (read in order):

    • The plausible body — what real bodies can and cannot do, as the realism rubric every synthetic dancer is judged against.
    • The captured body: video to skeleton to animation — the state of the art and the free tooling for extracting 3D dance from video, and the clever repairs (learned priors, physics simulation) that make the result look alive.
    • Generating movement — music-to-dance and text-to-motion generation in mid-2026: mechanisms, failure modes, the suspect metrics, and the direct-to-video newcomers.
    • Rendering the dancer — skeleton and keyframes to pixels: how pose-conditioned video generation actually behaves, its motion budget, and how to make it hold still where you need it to.
    • The pipeline, end to end — every stage and its owner, the budgets that bind, composing for the camera, and one phrase walked through the whole chain.
