    Props and environment¶

    Part VI · The staged body — chapter 3 of 4 · ← Dressing the genre · Light and the rest of the picture →

    A dancer stands somewhere, and what is there changes the dance. The bare stage is not an absence of design but a choice — Cunningham's, Balanchine's, Judson's — and the history of what choreographers have put on the stage (or left off it, or danced on instead of it) is a history of three distinct relationships between the body and its surroundings. The set can be a picture the body moves in front of; it can be terrain the body moves through, which alters the movement; and it can be a partner — an object handled until the handling is the vocabulary. Most design decisions are a mix, but the three are worth keeping separate, because they answer different questions, and because only one of them survives intact into the rendered world this part is building toward.

    The picture: painted drops and the painters' theatre¶

    For two centuries the set was a backdrop: painted perspective, wings and borders, a picture the dancers were figures in. The Ballets Russes brought in the painters — Bakst's saturated Orientalism, Benois's fairground Petrouchka, Picasso's curtain for Parade and his Tricorne (1919), Goncharova, Matisse, Derain — and turned the backdrop into modern art, but it remained a backdrop: something to dance in front of. The picture relationship is not inferior; it is the one under which the costume and the stage image can be composed as a single painting, and the costume history is largely its story. Its limit is that the body and the set do not touch, and so the set cannot change how the body moves. For that you need a sculptor.

    Terrain: Noguchi and Graham¶

    Isamu Noguchi's first set for Martha Graham, Frontier (1935), is a section of fence and two ropes running from it up and out to the sides of the proscenium — a V of rope that makes the stage into a plain receding to infinity. It is not a picture of space; it is space, and the dancer's relation to the fence is the dance. Over some twenty collaborations between 1935 and 1966 Noguchi gave Graham sets that were sculptures the body completed: the Shaker-spare house frame and rocker of Appalachian Spring (1944); the wire "dress" of Cave of the Heart (1946) that Medea steps into and wears as a radiating cage of brass; the bed of Night Journey (1947), a bone-like object that is also Jocasta's marriage bed and her death; the rope and tape of Errand into the Maze (1947), a path to be walked. The dancers sat on, climbed, held, and entered these things. Noguchi's own description was that the set is an extension of the dancer, and the consequence for choreography was that Graham's movement had to negotiate objects — lean, grip, pass through — and the negotiation became content. He built for Balanchine too (the silk curtain and lyre of Orpheus, 1948), but the Graham sets are the definition of terrain: the set as a thing the body's physics has to reckon with.

    Independent events: Cunningham's visual artists¶

    Cunningham's answer was structural rather than sculptural. Because the chance procedures of the systems chapters held that music, movement, and décor should be made independently and share only the time of performance, his sets were events that happened to co-occur with the dance. Robert Rauschenberg, designer from 1954 to 1964, brought a freestanding combine onstage for Minutiae (1954) and for Story (1963) assembled the set fresh at each venue from whatever he found there. After him came the artists: Frank Stella's colored banners on wheeled poles for Scramble (1967); Jasper Johns's translation of Duchamp's Large Glass into movable panels for Walkaround Time (1968); Andy Warhol's helium-filled Mylar Silver Clouds drifting through RainForest (1968), which the dancers bumped and nudged whether or not they intended to; Robert Morris's light column moving across Canfield (1969); Nam June Paik's electronics for Variations V (1965). The dancers were told almost nothing about the set in advance. The relationship is neither picture nor terrain — the set doesn't depict and it doesn't constrain — but a third thing, co-presence, and it taught two generations that the stage picture need not be unified to be whole.

    Terrain, again: Bausch's difficult stages¶

    Pina Bausch's designers — Rolf Borzik until his death in 1980, then Peter Pabst — made terrain the signature of tanztheater, and did it with materials rather than sculpture:

    • The Rite of Spring (1975): the stage covered in peat, which the dancers churn, fall into, and wear by the end.
    • Café Müller (1978): a room of chairs and tables that a man frantically clears from the path of a sleepwalking woman.
    • Kontakthof (1978): a dance hall — chairs along the walls, a piano, a stage with a curtain.
    • Arien (1979): ankle-deep water across the entire stage.
    • 1980 (1980): a lawn of real turf.
    • Nelken (1982): thousands of silk carnations the dancers must move through and flatten.
    • Palermo Palermo (1989): a breeze-block wall that collapses in the first minute, after which the piece is danced on rubble.
    • Vollmond (2006): a great rock and a stream of water; the dancers end drenched.

    The mechanism is worth naming exactly. A difficult surface does two things: it alters the movement (running in peat, slipping on wet stage, stepping over flowers) so that trained bodies lose their polish and show effort, which is tanztheater's subject; and it marks time — the costumes soil, the stage degrades, and the audience sees the evening's duration written on the set. The gowns of the costume history are chosen because the terrain will ruin them. Terrain, in Bausch, is an engine for producing the unrehearsable.

    Site: the building as score¶

    The postmodern generation took the dancer out of the theatre and let the place do the set's work. Trisha Brown's early-1970s pieces are the canon: Man Walking Down the Side of a Building (1970), exactly that, in a harness, perpendicular to the wall of a SoHo building; Walking on the Wall (1971, at the Whitney), dancers tracked along the gallery walls parallel to the floor; Roof Piece (1971, restaged 1973), dancers on a dozen SoHo rooftops across ten blocks relaying movement one to the next like semaphore, so that the piece's form was the city's geometry and the transmission's decay. Meredith Monk's Juice (1969) used the Guggenheim's ramp as a spiralling stage. Anna Halprin's outdoor deck in Kentfield, where so much of the RSVP work was made, is a site that became a method. Site work reverses the usual order of making: the place is given, and the choreography is what the place affords — a stairwell affords descent, a façade affords vertigo. It is the purest case of terrain, and it fed directly back into the theatre as an appetite for stages that resist.

    Apparatus and aerial¶

    Where the site is vertical, dance meets rigging. Bandaloop (Amelia Rudolph, from 1991) dances harnessed down the faces of buildings and cliffs, the wall as floor and gravity as a sideways force — a literal rotation of the choreographic frame. Elizabeth Streb's "PopAction" is the opposite temperament: trampolines, walls, rotating trusses, and a company trained to hit surfaces, with impact as the vocabulary and the apparatus as an opponent. Aerial silks, the trapeze, and the hoop came to dance from circus and arrived in force through the Cirque du Soleil fusion of the 1990s; harness "flying" has been a theatre technology since Peter Pan. Apparatus work is terrain in its strongest form — the object is not negotiated but depended on — and it generates vocabularies no floor can, which is why it tends to hold its own performers and its own schools rather than blend.

    Partner: the object as vocabulary¶

    The third relationship is the oldest. In a great many traditions the prop is not scenery but a unit of the movement language, and the codified-vocabularies chapter could have been written from props alone:

    • Sleeves and veils — the Chinese shuixiu, the "water sleeves" of Beijing opera, extend the hand by a metre of white silk, so that a flick, a toss, a gather become named gestures with named meanings; kathak's dupatta and the veil of raqs sharqi work the same way.
    • Fans — flamenco's abanico, the Chinese and Korean fan dances, the geisha's fan: the fan snaps, giving the arm a percussive ending it cannot produce alone.
    • Sticks and poles — Morris dance's clashed sticks, Gujarati dandiya raas, the Philippine tinikling whose bamboo poles, clapped together by kneeling players, set both the rhythm and the risk: the prop generates the timing and punishes error.
    • Swords, spears, staves — the Scottish sword dance, Chinese sword forms, the weapon work of Thai khon: the object imposes geometry (you cannot swing a blade any way you like).
    • Ropes, ribbons, poi — the Māori poi, the Chinese ribbon dance, Fuller's wands: the prop extends the body's trajectory into the air and draws it.
    • Chairs, hats, canes — musical theatre's kit. Fosse's bowler is an extension of the hand and a mask for the face; the chair in "Mein Herr" (Cabaret, 1972) is a partner, a barre, and a stage.

    The mechanism across all of these is the one the costume history found in cloth: the object renders the movement's dynamics — it lags, snaps, sweeps, or rings — and in return it demands a technique. Schlemmer's rods and Nikolais's elastic bands (Tensile Involvement, 1953, the stage strung into a cat's-cradle the dancers play) are the twentieth century's abstract versions. And from the generative project's side, the prop is the most honest test of a movement vocabulary: a gamut of gestures with a fan in the hand is a different gamut, because every unit has a new ending.

    The rendered stage¶

    All of which has to be re-asked for a dancer whose stage is generated. The pipeline chapter left costume, set, and score open; for the set, the commons now holds a clear and slightly painful answer, and it turns on which of the three relationships you want.

    The picture relationship survives, with one rule that practitioners learn expensively: decide whether the set must be consistent or identical, because they are different problems. Consistency — every shot plausibly belongs to the same place and look — is what generative tools provide: style references, moodboards, a location adapter, a locked prompt and seed make outputs that share a palette and a rendering language. Identity — the same wall, the same chair, the same light, the same pixels from shot to shot — is what no generative method provides, because diffusion has no memory between samples; every generation re-derives the room, and the residual difference reads exactly as a set rebuilt between takes. If the set must not change, stop trying to make generation more consistent and reuse pixels: one plate, generated once, fed back as conditioning so the model paints only the dancer's region (the envelope-mask technique of the rendering chapter); or one plate shared by every shot with the same framing; or a composite, the dancer matted over the plate — total control of the background at the cost of a compositing stage and of the dancer no longer being lit by the room behind her.1

    The corollary is the most common mistake in the field. A style reference locks a look, not a place: Krea 2's references transfer palette, line, texture, lighting, and composition — the documented example is a cartoon cat as reference producing a cartoon horse — and asking them for the same room from a new angle gets a different room in the same style, every time. An environment adapter trained on one image learns that image, not that place: one photograph contains one viewpoint, and no training schedule recovers the reverse angle that was never in the data; even the published small-data success for scene adaptation (some forty clips, deliberately spanning day and night, wide and close, static and moving camera) learned a look and a motion grammar — costume texture, grading, torchlit ambiance, camera behavior — and not a navigable location. The practical division of labor that emerges is: adapters and references for global style and mood, reference-image conditioning for structural and asset fidelity — the architecture, the signage, the chair that has to be the same chair.2

    And here the three relationships separate. Picture is available on those terms. Terrain is mostly not: the synthetic dancer cannot yet churn peat or slip on water, because the body's interaction with a surface is exactly the physics the plausible-body chapter listed as the render's weakest tell — contact, weight, resistance — and a set that is a conditioning plate is, by construction, a set the body does not touch. (An identical background also forbids the camera to move, which the rendering chapter explains.) Partner is the frontier: a fan or a sleeve is a second moving object sharing the motion budget with the dancer, and the commons records nothing yet on how well that goes. Noguchi's fence, Bausch's carnations, and the water sleeve remain, for now, the stage's privileges — and the reason a rendered dancer so often stands in front of something rather than in it.
