    Light and the rest of the picture¶

    Part VI · The staged body — chapter 4 of 4 · ← Props and environment · The plausible body →

    A dancer is seen only as lit. That sentence is the whole of lighting design, and it took the theatre until the twentieth century to take it seriously, because until electricity the choice barely existed. This chapter covers the three things that complete the stage picture after costume and set: the light, which decides what of the body the eye can resolve and when; hair and makeup, which decide what the face and skin are at distance and, in several traditions, who the dancer is; and the room — proscenium, black box, round, gallery, screen — which decides where the eye is. It closes with what a dancer made of pixels inherits from each.

    Light¶

    A short history of being able to see the dancer¶

    Candles and oil lamps, then gas (from the 1810s) with its dimmable but dangerous glow, then limelight (1830s), the first beam that could isolate a single body on a stage — the phrase survives — then electric incandescence from the 1880s and with it the dimmer board and the possibility of composing light in time. Two figures saw what that meant before the technology was good enough to do it. Loie Fuller, whose silk-and-wand costumes were covered under costume, understood in the 1890s that colored electric light on moving cloth was a dance, and patented the rigs. Adolphe Appia, the Swiss theorist, argued that light was the "living" element of the stage, that a three-dimensional body needs three-dimensional light and sculpted space rather than painted flats, and built his "rhythmic spaces" of steps and platforms for Émile Jaques-Dalcroze's eurhythmics school at Hellerau (1912). Appia's body-in-plastic-light is the premise every dance lighting designer has worked from since.

    The profession itself is American and mid-century. Jean Rosenthal (1912–1969) is its founder: she lit Martha Graham's company from the 1930s, was New York City Ballet's designer from its beginnings, and lit West Side Story and Fiddler on the Roof on Broadway — and she is credited with the single most important decision in the field, which is that dance is lit from the side. After her: Thomas Skelton (the Joffrey, Ailey), Nicola Cernovitch (the original Revelations, 1960), Beverly Emmons (Graham, Cunningham, Meredith Monk), and Jennifer Tipton — Paul Taylor, Twyla Tharp, Trisha Brown, Jerome Robbins, the Wooster Group, a MacArthur Fellowship in 2008 and a generation of students at Yale — whose cold, precise, unhurried white light and whose command of duration (how long a change takes, and what that length means) made lighting a compositional voice of its own. In the contemporary European and British repertory the names are Michael Hulls (Akram Khan, Russell Maliphant), Lucy Carter (Wayne McGregor), Mark Stanley (New York City Ballet's resident designer), and — unusually — the choreographer himself: William Forsythe designs much of his own light, typically hard, low, white, and frankly exposed.

    Why side light¶

    Drama lights faces. The standard theatrical method — Stanley McCandless's, from 1932 — crosses two front lights at about forty-five degrees, one warm and one cool, so that the actor's face is modelled and visible from every seat. Dance lights bodies, and front light is the enemy: it flattens the torso, washes the limbs into the floor, and throws the shadow behind the dancer where it joins the backdrop. So dance hangs its light on booms in the wings — vertical pipes carrying lamps at shin height ("shin-busters"), mid height, and head height — and lights the body from the sides, low. The consequences are exactly the ones dance wants:

    • The body is modelled as sculpture: one side lit, one in shadow, the limbs separated from each other and from the trunk.
    • The floor stays dark, so a body can be brilliant in a black field — and a jump reads as a jump, because the dancer rises out of shadow into light.
    • Shadows fall across the stage, not onto the backdrop, and the dancers' own shadows become part of the picture rather than a doubling of it.
    • Faces are secondary, which suits an art form where the face is rarely the point.

    Around the side light the designer has back light (from upstage, to rim the body and lift it off the backdrop — the edge light that makes a silhouette crisp), top light (down, for pools and isolation, and for carving a face into planes), a minimum of front fill, and the cyclorama behind everything for fields of color and the implied sky. Gobos cut texture into beams; followspots isolate a soloist in a large house. These are the tools; the art is in three variables that costume and set do not have.

    The cue as choreography¶

    Light has angle, color, intensity, and — the variable that is dance's — time. A lighting cue is a transition from one state to another with a duration, and the difference between a zero-count bump and a thirty-count fade is the difference between a cut and a dissolve. A dance's lighting plot is therefore a second score running alongside the choreography: a sequence of states and durations that decides what the eye is allowed to see at each moment, and how that permission changes. This is why good dance lighting is so often invisible — its work is to direct attention, which is to say to edit, and editing is noticed only when it fails. Color deserves the same respect: warm against cool on opposite sides gives the body a modelled skin tone; a saturated cyclorama behind dim side light turns dancers into cut-outs; and the LED era (additive color mixing, the loss of tungsten's warm dimming curve) has changed the palette's physics in ways designers are still absorbing. Moving lights — Vari-Lite in 1981, ubiquitous since the 2000s — added light that moves to the score, and projection (from Nikolais's pattern slides to Klaus Obermaier's Apparition (2004) and Adrien M & Claire B's Pixel (2014) with Mourad Merzouki) added light that responds to the dancer, tracked by camera — the thread the computational-choreography chapter follows.

    The most useful way to hold all of this: for dance, light is frequently the set. A great many pieces have no scenery at all, and what defines the space — the diagonal corridor, the downstage pool, the wall of cyc — is light. Humphrey's map of the stage's strong and weak regions (the craft chapter) is, in practice, usually drawn in light rather than in scenery.

    Hair and makeup¶

    Hair and makeup are costume's continuation onto the body itself, and they answer three questions: what survives distance, what the hair does as a moving object, and — in several traditions — which character this is.

    Distance. Stage makeup exists because a face at twenty metres is a blur. Exaggerated brows, lined eyes, false lashes, contoured cheekbones — all of it is a resolution rule made flesh: draw the face's few important edges heavily enough that they clear the viewer's acuity threshold at the back of the house. Fosse's white gloves and the cartoon mouse's are the same logic applied to hands. The classical bun is a distance decision too — a clean head line, no occlusion of the neck and shoulders, nothing to whip into the eyes in a pirouette — and a uniformity device, the corps as one head repeated.

    Hair as a moving object. Long, loose hair is a costume element that moves with the cloth-lags-the-body physics the costume history described — and contemporary makers use it as such. Bausch's women with their long hair down (Kontakthof, The Rite of Spring) carry a second, freer dancer on the head; a hair whip is a gesture with a visible decay. Irish step dance's curled wig is the inverse: a stiff frame around the motionless upper body.

    Makeup as character vocabulary. Several traditions have made makeup a codified language, of the kind the codified-vocabularies chapter describes for movement:

    • Kathakali's vesham types — green-faced pacha for noble heroes, kathi for arrogant kings with a knife-shaped red mark, three thadi (bearded) types, black kari for demons, radiant minukku for women and sages — are applied over hours, with the chutti (a ridge of rice-paste and paper built along the jaw) that frames the face as a mask. The audience reads the character's moral class before a step.
    • Jingju, Beijing opera, paints the lianpu face in a color code — red for loyalty, white for treachery, black for integrity and fierceness, gold and silver for gods and spirits — so that the face is the dramatis personae.
    • Noh uses masks rather than paint, and the famous mechanism belongs here: a noh mask has no fixed expression; tilted up toward the light (terasu) it brightens and seems to smile, tilted down (kumorasu) it clouds and grieves. The expression is a product of the face's angle to the light — lighting and makeup collapsed into one object.
    • Bharatanatyam's red alta on fingertips and soles outlines the hand's geometry so every mudra reads as a drawn figure, and traces the foot's placement in a color the eye tracks.
    • Butoh's white — the rice-powdered, sometimes shaved body of Hijikata Tatsumi and Kazuo Ohno, and the ash-grey of Sankai Juku — erases the individual, turns skin into a screen that takes light, and is the single feature by which the form is recognized. It is a refusal of costume that became the genre's costume, and a lighting decision as much as a makeup one: a white body in side light is sculpture; in darkness it is the only thing that exists.

    The room¶

    Where the audience sits decides what choreography can be. Five rooms, and what each costs.

    The proscenium is a picture frame, and everything in this part assumes it: a single shared viewpoint, depth (upstage far, downstage near), wings for entrances and booms, a fly tower, a frame that makes Humphrey's stage geography meaningful — center as power, upstage center as remote majesty, the downstage corners intimate, the diagonals the strongest lines. The proscenium is also why side light works: there is a side.

    The black box has no frame. Dancers are at arm's length, the light hangs from a grid, the floor is the same floor the audience's chairs stand on. It loses the picture — there is no composed frame to read — and gains presence: breath, sweat, the sound of the feet. Much of postmodern and contemporary dance was made for this room and reads badly when promoted to the proscenium, where its scale looks thin.

    The round, the thrust, the arena have no front, so there is no upstage and no hidden side. Choreography for the round must be all-sides legible — a phrase that only reads from the front is a phrase that most of the house cannot see. Competitive ballroom is danced in this room; so are the ritual and social dances that are the art form's origin, and the cypher of the street styles is the round with no seats at all. Ailey in an arena, a powwow in a circle, a ballroom final on a floor ringed by judges — these share a geometry and a problem.

    The gallery and the site. The props chapter covered the site. The white box is its recent cousin — Boris Charmatz's Musée de la danse (from 2009), Xavier Le Roy's Retrospective (2012), Tino Sehgal's constructed situations — where the audience walks, the work is durational, and the viewer chooses both distance and duration. The choreographic unit becomes the encounter rather than the phrase.

    The screen. The camera is the only viewpoint, and the director has chosen it. Berkeley's overhead lens made a picture no house could see; Astaire's full-body long take made the screen a proscenium. This room's rules are Part VII's — the pipeline chapter on shot size selecting movement scale — and the next section turns to them.

    What the rendered picture inherits¶

    The synthetic dancer of Part VII has, by now, a wardrobe rule and a stage rule. The light, the face, and the room hand down three more, and each is already in the commons from the animation project this pipeline shares its tools with.

    The lighting plot is a color script — build it before you generate. Animation studios keep a continuity bible (model sheets, a palette bible, location anchors) and, from Pixar's practice, a color script: a row of small tableaux mapping color and lighting mood across the whole story, anchored on a few tent-pole images at the core emotional beats — part inspiration, part continuity. The generative translation is direct: one style-anchor image per location, a short color script with one thumbnail per major beat so each scene's prompts inherit a locked palette rather than improvising one, and a fixed prompt block, logged seed, and adapter weights per scene, changed one variable at a time. The models have no memory between generations; drift is fought only by deliberately repeating reference, seed, and prompt. Every lighting designer will recognize this document: it is the cue sheet, written for a system that forgets.1

    Light is part of the pose, not a post-effect. Limited animation — the UPA lineage, Mercury Filmworks on Hilda today — puts expressiveness in held poses rather than fluid in-betweens, and treats light and shadow as shapes locked into specific held poses, not simulated continuously. In a keyframe-and-interpolate pipeline this is the premise, not a style: the model adds plausible physics between two stills but no intent of its own, so each keyframe must work as a self-sufficient lit picture, with a fixed key light per shot — the rendering chapter already warned that a mismatched key between stills becomes an invented lighting change. Rosenthal's side light and the noh mask's tilt say the same thing from the stage: the body's expression is its angle to a light you chose, so choose it per frame and hold it.2

    Author for the delivery room, not the monitor. The stage has always known that the picture is made for a particular viewer at a particular distance under particular light; stage makeup is that knowledge. The commons holds the projected-image version, and it matters for any synthetic dancer who will be shown somewhere: a projector's rated contrast is measured in a black room, and one documented case fell from about 400:1 to about 67:1 against a room's own wall reflections before any house light was added; black pixels project no light, so "black" is the color of the wall; detail finer than the eye's roughly one-arc-minute acuity at the room's actual viewing distance is wasted authoring effort. The practitioner defaults that follow — bold silhouettes on a black field, two or three dominant colors per cue, no fine gradients outside true darkness — are a lighting designer's dance plot in other words: side-lit body, dark floor, saturated cyc. A seated single-screen viewer, by contrast, gives sustained attention and can be rewarded with detail and slow reveals; a club crowd glances for hours and needs immediacy. The same render dies on a laptop or in a club — author for the one you are shipping to.3

    The picture, complete¶

    Costume, set, light, face, room. For the stage these were always one composition — Fuller and Nikolais made them literally one, and every choreographer who has ever said no set, just light was composing with the full kit by subtraction. For the synthetic dancer the same five now have rules of their own: an identity-anchored wardrobe that the model can hold; a plate that is reused rather than regenerated, and a body that therefore stands in front of its stage rather than in it; a color script and a fixed key per shot; a face designed to read at the delivery distance; and a room that is always, in the end, the screen. Part VII built the body; this part dressed, placed, and lit it. What the body moves to is Part IV, next.
