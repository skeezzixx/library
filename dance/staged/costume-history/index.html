    Dance costume: a history¶

    Part VI · The staged body — chapter 1 of 4 · ← Locomotion without a choreographer · Dressing the genre →

    Every other chapter in this subject treats the body as if it were naked. It never is. What a dancer wears decides what the audience can see of the movement, what the movement can be at all, and who the audience thinks is moving — and the history of dance costume is, read correctly, a history of permission: each reform in dress is a reform in what the body was allowed to do in public. The heroes of this chapter are mostly the people who took something off.

    It helps to name, up front, the three jobs a costume does, because the whole history is a negotiation among them:

    • Affordance — what the garment lets the body do and lets the eye see. A hoop skirt permits arms and forbids legs; a unitard permits everything and hides nothing.
    • Signal — what the garment says about who is moving: rank, role, gender, genre, century.
    • Picture — what the garment does to the stage image: silhouette, color, the way fabric trails and catches light.

    One physical fact underlies all three. Cloth lags the body. A skirt, a sleeve, a length of silk follows the limb that drives it with a delay and an overshoot, so fabric renders velocity and trajectory, which the bare body only implies. That makes costume the first motion- amplifier in the art form — and, as the synthetic-dancer chapters found from the other direction, the first thing a rendered body gets wrong. The history below is partly the history of choreographers discovering what that lag could do, and partly of their refusing it.

    The court: costume as rank¶

    Ballet begins as court spectacle, and court costume was built to display the wearer, not the movement. When Louis XIV danced Apollo in the Ballet de la nuit (1653) — the role that made him the Sun King — he wore gilded rays, a plumed headdress, a stiff short skirt, and heeled shoes; the dancing was a noble walk made ornate.1 Through the eighteenth century the professional stage inherited the look: men in the tonnelet, a hooped, wired skirt standing out from the hips like an inverted bell; women in panniered gowns to the floor; both in wigs, heels, and — for serious roles — masks. The costume made the dancer a moving emblem. It also made legs irrelevant, and the technique followed: the beauty of eighteenth-century dancing lived in the carriage of the torso, the épaulement, and the arms, because that is what could be seen.

    The reforms came as removals. Marie Camargo, in the 1730s, shortened her skirts to the ankle so that her entrechats and cabrioles — small beaten jumps the gown had been hiding — could be seen, took the heels off her shoes, and, scandalously, wore close-fitting drawers underneath. Her rival Marie Sallé went further in London in 1734, dancing her Pygmalion in loose muslin drapery with her hair down and no pannier, no wig, no mask: the first time a star danced the body rather than the rank.2 Jean-Georges Noverre's Lettres sur la danse (1760) made the argument in print — masks, tonnelets, and panniers were obstacles to expression — and Maximilien Gardel danced unmasked at the Paris Opéra in 1772, which is usually taken as the moment the face entered ballet. Then the Revolution finished the job: neoclassical fashion put women in light, high-waisted chemise dresses, and the Paris Opéra's costumier Maillot supplied the flesh-colored knitted tights that still carry his name. By 1800 the legs existed.

    Romantic and classical: the tutu as machine¶

    Once the legs existed, the costume was redesigned around showing them, and the result is the one garment everyone can name. Its two forms are two different technologies.

    The Romantic tutu arrives with Marie Taglioni in La Sylphide (1832): a bell of white tulle falling to mid-calf, a fitted bodice, bare shoulders — the silhouette attributed to Eugène Lami and repeated in Giselle (1841) and every ballet blanc after. Read it by the three jobs. As picture it is weightlessness: a soft, translucent mass that moves a half-beat behind the body, so that the sylph seems to float rather than to jump. As affordance it hides the working leg and shows the feet — which is exactly the deal pointe work was making. The dancer's effort (the plié, the thigh) disappears under tulle; the result (the rise, the hover) appears below it. As signal, white tulle became the uniform of the spirit world, and a century later, Balanchine could put a corps in it and the audience would know the act was about the unattainable before a step was danced.

    The classical tutu is the opposite bargain. Through the Petipa decades in St. Petersburg the skirt rises — knee, thigh, hip — until by The Sleeping Beauty (1890) and Swan Lake (1895) it is the flat, horizontal pancake: ten or a dozen layers of stiff net graduated in length, tacked together and often wired with a hoop, standing straight out from a boned bodice. It hides nothing below the hip. It exists to show technique: the Italian virtuosas who arrived in Russia in the 1880s and 90s — Virginia Zucchi, Pierina Legnani with her thirty-two fouettés — brought a legwork the bell would have buried, and the costume shortened to meet it. It also does something subtler. The disc draws a horizontal line through the dancer's hips, which makes every deviation of the leg from that line — the extension to ninety, the arabesque above it — measurable by eye. The pancake tutu is a reference plane worn on the body. The boned bodice, meanwhile, fixes the torso as a column, so that the line of the leg reads against a still trunk. Nothing about the garment is decorative; it is an instrument for the legible display of a specific technique, and it remains the standard because the technique does.3

    Two footnotes to the story belong here. The pointe shoe evolved in parallel, from Taglioni's darned satin slippers to the blocked, pasted toe-boxes of the late nineteenth century, to Freed (founded 1929) and, in 1993, Gaynor Minden's polymer shank — the one piece of dance costume that is unambiguously a machine, and it gets its own treatment in a deep dive off ballet's history. And Karinska — Barbara Karinska, Balanchine's costumier — rebuilt the tutu in 1950 for Symphony in C: shorter, softer, unhooped, six or seven layers loosely tacked so the skirt bounced with the step instead of sitting rigid. The "powder-puff" tutu is the classical machine re-tuned for a faster, more off-balance technique — the costume following the movement, again.

    The painters' theatre¶

    The Ballets Russes (1909–1929) made costume the co-equal of choreography, and the way they did it was to hand the job to painters. Léon Bakst's designs for Cléopâtre (1909) and Schéhérazade (1910) put harem trousers, bare midriffs, and saturated orientalist color on the Paris stage and reorganized fashion within a season; his costume for Nijinsky's L'Après-midi d'un faune (1912) — the piebald, skin-tight suit — made the dancer a painted surface. Alexandre Benois's Petrouchka (1911), Natalia Goncharova's folk-art Coq d'Or (1914), Matisse's Chant du Rossignol (1920): the modern art world entered the theatre through the wardrobe. Two of these collaborations mark the outer limits of what costume can be. Picasso's Parade (1917) put the Managers in three-metre Cubist constructions of cardboard and cloth — the costume so large it dictated the movement, the dancer reduced to the construction's legs. And Coco Chanel's Le Train Bleu (1924) did the opposite: knitted bathing suits and sportswear, real clothes from a real beach, the costume as contemporary fashion and nothing more.4

    Oskar Schlemmer's Triadic Ballet (Stuttgart, 1922) is the Bauhaus version of the Picasso pole: padded, spiralled, and disc-shaped costumes that turn the body into geometric figures — a sphere for a skirt, a spiral for a torso — and a movement vocabulary derived from what the figures can do. His Slat Dance and Pole Dance (1927) strapped rods to the limbs so that every gesture drew a line in space. Schlemmer is the ancestor of every choreographer who has let the costume generate the movement, which makes him the hinge to the props chapter.

    Modern: costume as manifesto¶

    American modern dance began as a costume decision. Loie Fuller's Serpentine Dance (1892) was hundreds of yards of silk on bamboo or aluminum wands that extended her arms, whirled under colored electric light, and she patented both the garment and the lighting rig — the dancer vanished into fabric and light, and fabric-plus-light was the dance.5 Isadora Duncan took the opposite road to the same destination: a Greek tunic, bare feet, bare legs, no corset. The costume was the ideology — nature against artifice, the body against the ballet machine — and audiences in 1900 understood it as such before she moved. Ruth St. Denis's Radha (1906) and the Denishawn repertory dressed the body in a scholar's orientalism; Mary Wigman's German expressionism put it behind masks (the 1926 Hexentanz).

    Martha Graham is the decisive case, because she used costume to make a technique visible. Lamentation (1930) is a dancer seated on a bench inside a tube of stretch jersey; nearly every movement is a contraction, and the tube — pulled, stretched, strained against — shows the contraction as a change in the fabric's shape. The costume is an amplifier tuned to one movement principle. Graham designed her own costumes for decades — the long, full, bare-footed "Graham dress" that lets the fall and the spiral read — and when Halston dressed her company in the 1970s and 80s he was working inside a grammar she had already fixed.

    Alwin Nikolais went all the way to Fuller's conclusion and built what the syllabus calls the total-design body. From Masks, Props and Mobiles (1953) through Tensile Involvement, Noumenon (both 1953), Kaleidoscope (1956) and Imago (1963) he put dancers in stretch-fabric sacks, extended them with rods and discs, lit them in projected pattern, and wrote the sound himself — the dancer became a moving shape in a designed field. Critics called it dehumanizing; Nikolais called it decentralizing the human. Either way it is the clearest twentieth-century statement that costume, light, and movement are one composition.

    The neutral body: leotard, tights, unitard¶

    The counter-movement was to take all of that off and show the body as a body. Its material precondition was chemistry — DuPont's spandex (1958) made the stretch garments we now think of as the dancer's default — but the aesthetic preceded the fiber. Balanchine had already stripped The Four Temperaments of its designed costumes by 1951 and danced it in practice clothes, and the leotard ballets — Agon (1957), Episodes (1959) — made black leotards and pink tights on a bare stage into a genre of their own. The argument is the one Humphrey's craft chapter made about everything else: remove what isn't the dance. The practice uniform says this is the movement, and only the movement.

    Cunningham reached the same surface by a different road. With Robert Rauschenberg as designer (1954–64) his dancers wore leotards and tights that were dyed, painted, or otherwise treated as canvas — most famously Summerspace (1958), where costumes and backdrop share the same pointillist speckle, so that the bodies half-dissolve into the field. Under Jasper Johns's advisorship (1967–80) and after, the Cunningham unitard became the neutral instrument the chance procedures needed: a body that can be combined with any other, in any order, because it is visually interchangeable. The chance chapter describes why that interchangeability was structural, not merely tasteful.

    Judson: the pedestrian body¶

    The Judson Dance Theater (1962–64) took the last step. Sneakers, jeans, T-shirts, sweatpants: Rainer's Trio A (1966), Paxton's walking pieces, Trisha Brown's early work were performed in what the dancers had on. Yvonne Rainer's "No Manifesto" (1965) — no to spectacle, no to virtuosity, no to glamour and transcendency of the star image — is a costume policy as much as a movement one. Ordinary clothes make the claim that dance is ordinary movement, performed by people, and that the audience should look at the task and not the body. The look of the rehearsal room became the look of the concert, and it never entirely left: contemporary dance's default wardrobe of loose trousers, socks, and muted neutrals is Judson's inheritance, however far the movement has traveled from its ideology. Twyla Tharp carried it into the big houses — her company in sneakers and sweats on opera-house stages in the 1970s — and the sneaker has been a legitimate dance shoe since.

    Tanztheater and after: costume as social sign¶

    Pina Bausch reversed the polarity once more. Her dancers, under Marion Cito's costumes from 1980, wear evening clothes: silk slips and long gowns, high heels, men in suits — and then dance in dirt, water, and leaves until the gowns are ruined. The costume is not neutral and not an amplifier; it is a social fact. It tells you these are men and women at a dance hall, a funeral, a party, and the dancing is what happens to such people. Every tanztheater company since has known that a dress can be a dramaturgy.

    Forsythe and De Keersmaeker show the two contemporary poles. Forsythe dressed his Frankfurt dancers in black and practice-wear, and when he wanted more he went to Issey Miyake (The Loss of Small Detail, 1991) and to Stephen Galloway, the company's own designer — costume as extension of the choreographer's formal thinking. De Keersmaeker's Rosas danst Rosas (1983) put four women in grey dresses and socks, a uniform that made their accumulating, repeating phrases read as one machine with four parts; for Drumming (1998) and Rain (2001) she went to Dries Van Noten, whose light, colored, ordinary-looking garments catch the air as the dancers run — the cloth-lags-the-body principle, deliberately exploited.

    The couture return¶

    The last forty years have seen fashion return to the stage it left with Chanel, and the collaborations are worth knowing as a list, because each one is a distinct theory of what costume is for:

    • Jean Paul Gaultier for Régine Chopinot (the 1980s, Le Défilé 1985) — the runway as choreography, the costume as the piece's subject.
    • Gianni Versace for Maurice Béjart (1980s) — glamour restored to the male dancer.
    • Christian Lacroix, Gaîté Parisienne for American Ballet Theatre (1988) — haute couture period spectacle, the Bakst tradition revived.
    • Rei Kawakubo for Cunningham's Scenario (1997) — the "lumps and bumps" dresses from her Body Meets Dress collection, padded protrusions that redraw the dancers' silhouettes and so change what their movement means; Schlemmer's idea, by way of Comme des Garçons.
    • Gareth Pugh for Wayne McGregor's Carbon Life (2012); Riccardo Tisci for Cherkaoui and Jalet's Boléro (2013); Iris van Herpen for Benjamin Millepied (Neverwhere, 2013) — the fashion house as co-author of the stage picture, and in van Herpen's case of the body's physics, since her 3-D-printed and laser-cut structures move like nothing cloth does.

    What the history says¶

    Three through-lines, to carry into the next chapter, where the same garments are read genre by genre.

    First, the costume usually comes before the technique it shows. Camargo's short skirt made the beaten jump worth doing; the rising tutu met the Italian legwork and then demanded more of it; Graham's tube made the contraction a visible event. When you see a genre's uniform, assume it was tuned to display something, and ask what.

    Second, removal is the recurring gesture, and it never finishes. Mask, wig, pannier, hoop, corset, shoe, costume itself — each removal re-reads the body, and each neutral uniform (tights, the leotard, the sweatpants) becomes a costume in its turn, with a meaning the next generation strips again.

    Third, cloth is motion made visible. Fuller's silk, the Romantic bell, the bata de cola, Van Noten's shirts — fabric lags and overshoots, and so it renders the dynamics the body only implies. That is the deepest reason costume matters to this subject's generative project: a synthetic dancer who moves well but whose clothing hangs dead or swims wrong will look wrong for reasons the viewer cannot name, and a synthetic dancer dressed in a rigid disc or a unitard has simply avoided the problem. The plausible body needs a plausible skirt.
