    Dressing the genre¶

    The history established the lens — every costume is a settlement among what it lets the body do, what it tells the audience, and what it does to the picture. This chapter turns the lens on genres: the uniforms each tradition has converged on, and the reasons behind them, because the reasons are almost never decorative. A genre's costume is a compressed record of what that genre most wants seen, what it needs to hide, what its floor and its audience are like, and what its dancers do for a living. Read the uniform and you have read half the technique.

    Two working rules before the survey. First, look for the instrument: a surprising number of dance costumes contain a sound-maker or a motion-amplifier — bells, nailed heels, fringe, a train — and the garment exists to carry it. Second, look for the reference line: costumes frequently draw a fixed line on the body (a hem, a waistband, a pleat) against which the technique's deviations become measurable. Both rules recur below.

    Ballet¶

    The classical tutu was covered as history; as a working garment its points are these. The pancake's disc is a reference plane: it marks the hip height so the leg's extension above it is legible from the back row. The boned bodice holds the torso as a still column against which the limbs read. Pink tights and pointe shoes continue the line of the leg to the floor without a break at the ankle — which is also why stage tights are pink and not skin-matched in traditional productions: they are drawing a line, not imitating skin. The Romantic bell does the opposite trade, hiding the working leg to sell weightlessness. For men: tights to show the line and the jump, a short tunic or doublet that stops above the hip for the same reason the tutu does. Costume also carries rank — in a Petipa ballet the corps is dressed identically and the ballerina differently, and the eye is told whom to follow before anyone moves. The rehearsal look — leotard, tights, a chiffon skirt — became a performance genre with Balanchine and has stayed one: it signals this is about the steps.

    Modern and contemporary¶

    The contemporary default — bare feet or socks, loose trousers or a slip dress, muted color, a lot of bare skin — is Judson's inheritance with three functional additions:

    • Socks are a friction technology. They let a dancer slide, spin, and drop into floorwork on a sprung marley floor; bare feet grip, socks glide, and a choreographer choosing between them is choosing a movement quality. (Gaga work is typically barefoot; much floor-heavy release-based work is in socks.)
    • Knee pads under or over trousers, because contemporary floorwork spends knees.
    • Muted, matched neutrals, because the choreography is the content and a strong color pulls focus to one body — unless pulling focus is the point, in which case a single red dress in a grey ensemble is a choreographic device.

    The Graham dress, the Cunningham unitard, and the tanztheater evening gown remain available as quotations, and contemporary makers use them as such: a unitard says formal, a suit says social, a slip says vulnerable. The audience reads the citation whether or not the maker intended it.

    South Asian classical¶

    Bharatanatyam's stitched costume is a design solution to a geometric problem. Its signature is the pleated fan sewn between the legs, which lies closed when the dancer stands and opens into a triangle in araimandi — the half-seated, turned-out plié that is the technique's base position. The fan makes the araimandi visible as a shape, and makes a lazy one visible as a sloppy one; it is a reference line in the second-rule sense. Around it: temple jewelry that frames the face and neck (the eyes and the head carry abhinaya, expressive acting, so they are lit with metal), the red alta on fingertips and soles that outlines every mudra and foot placement in a color the eye follows, and — the instrument — the ghungroo, rows of small brass bells strapped at the ankle, so that every strike of the foot is heard. Kathak's costume varies the same ideas for a spinning technique: the long flared anarkali whose skirt swings out in the chakkar turns and renders their speed, and ankle bells in greater number — a hundred or more per ankle in mature dancers — because kathak's footwork, tatkar, is the percussion. Odissi drapes a sari with a front fan for the same reason bharatanatyam stitches one, adds silver filigree and the white pith headpiece, and keeps the torso bare enough that the tribhangi — the three-bend posture — can be seen as three bends.

    Kathakali belongs here as the limit case: an enormous pleated skirt built over a waist bundle, headdresses, and makeup that takes hours and encodes the character's moral type by color — a costume that is also a character vocabulary, taken up in the next chapter under makeup.

    Flamenco¶

    Flamenco wears its instruments openly. The shoes carry nails driven into heel and toe, so the zapateado is struck, not scuffed; the bata de cola — the long, trained, ruffled dress — is an apparatus with its own technique (it is kicked, swept, and turned, and a dancer trains for years to make it obey), and it renders the sweep of the body's turns as a trailing arc; the mantón, the fringed silk shawl, is a prop that turns the arms' carriage into a shape; the fan and the castanets complete the kit. The ruffles (volantes) on the skirt do Fuller's job in miniature, lagging and overshooting the hip's movement. Men wear high-waisted trousers, a short jacket or waistcoat, and heeled boots — the line of the torso and the percussion of the feet. Nothing in the outfit is arbitrary; flamenco's costume is a percussion section and a motion amplifier with a person inside.

    Street and club styles¶

    Street costume is the everyday clothing of a community with functional modifications, and the modifications tell you the technique.

    • Breaking — nylon tracksuits, because nylon has low friction against a floor and a windmill or backspin needs the back and shoulders to slide; a cap or beanie for the same reason on headspins; flat-soled sneakers (the Puma Suede and the Adidas shell-toe are canonical) for footwork and freezes. The silhouette also signals: the Bronx in the late 1970s, the crew, the era.
    • Locking — Don Campbell and The Lockers fixed the look in the early 1970s: striped socks, knickers, suspenders, the apple-boy hat. Locking's sharp, pointing, freezing vocabulary reads better with high-contrast horizontals on the limbs; the costume is partly a legibility device.
    • Popping — looser clothing that hides nothing of the hit; the style's isolations are the content, and a baggy shirt that snaps with the pop amplifies it.
    • House — loose, light, sweat-ready clothes for a footwork-driven style danced for hours in a club.
    • Waacking — 1970s disco glamour, because waacking is 1970s disco glamour: the arm work quotes Hollywood divas and the costume completes the quotation.
    • Voguing — in the ballroom scene the costume is the category. "Realness" categories are judged on whether the walker passes as the named thing (executive, schoolboy, femme queen), so the garment is the competition's substance, not its dressing.
    • Krump — baggy clothes for a style built on chest pops, stomps, and arm swings; early krump's painted faces were a signal of the form's street-theatre origins.

    The lesson for the rest of the subject: codified vocabularies that form without institutions (the codified-vocabularies chapter) also form their costumes without institutions — and the fashion industry then absorbs them, which is how the tracksuit and the sneaker arrived in the opera house.

    Social and competitive: tango and ballroom¶

    Argentine tango's costume is built around the embrace and the pivot. A woman's slit skirt permits the gancho (the hooked leg) and the boleo (the whipped free leg) and signals both; heels tip the posture forward into the partner and give the foot a small, hard pivot point for turns; men wear a suit cut to move and leather-soled shoes that turn on a wooden floor. Fabric that catches — lace, sequins on the embrace side — is avoided because the couple's torsos slide against each other.

    Competitive ballroom is the genre where the costume has been most thoroughly engineered for a specific viewer: a panel of judges thirty metres away under arena lighting, watching many couples at once. Standard (waltz, foxtrot, tango, Viennese, quickstep) gowns carry floats — long panels of light fabric from the shoulders or wrists — because floats trail the sway and swing of the frame and make it visible at distance; the man's tail suit is literally a stiff frame that holds the topline the technique demands. Latin costume goes the other way: cut away, bare, and fringed, because fringe amplifies hip action the way floats amplify swing, and saturated color plus crystals so a couple can be picked out of a crowded floor. The tan, the hair, the man's number pinned on his back — every element is a visibility decision for a judge's eye under specific conditions. It is the closest dance comes to costume as instrumentation.

    Tap, Irish step, and the shoe as instrument¶

    Tap's shoe is the instrument: metal plates screwed to toe and heel, with the screws' tightness and the plate's alloy chosen for tone, and the floor matters as much as the shoe. Irish step dance keeps the upper body and arms still by rule, so its costume — the stiff, embroidered solo dress, the hard shoes with fiberglass tips, the soft ghillies, and since the 1990s the curled wig — frames a motionless torso above a machine-gun foot. Riverdance (1994) loosened the dresses and darkened the palette, but the division of labor it dresses is unchanged.

    Other traditions, briefly¶

    Hula's kahiko (ancient) and ʻauana (modern) styles are distinguished as much by costume as by movement — ti-leaf skirts and kapa cloth against fabric dresses — and lei and the pāʻū skirt render the hips' figure-eight. Butoh's near-nudity under white powder is a refusal of costume that becomes the genre's most recognizable one (see makeup). Musical theatre jazz gives Fosse his bowler hats and white gloves — the hat as a prop extending the hand, the gloves so the hands read from the balcony; the same reason cartoon mice wear them.

    For the camera, not the house¶

    Every costume above was tuned for a house: a large room, a single shared viewpoint, a viewer far enough away that silhouette and color survive and texture does not. The camera changes all three conditions, and dance-for-camera costume is a distinct discipline.

    • The house reads silhouette at thirty metres; the camera reads texture and fit at one. Sequins that sparkle in an arena read as noise in a close-up; a seam that no one in the house would see is the whole frame in a medium shot.
    • The camera has a sensor, and sensors have opinions: fine stripes and tight patterns moiré, pure white blows out under key light, pure black swallows the body's detail and leaves a moving hole.
    • Costume must survive motion blur and the cut. Astaire's rule — the whole body in frame, the take long — made his white tie and tails a design for the long shot; Ginger Rogers's ostrich-feather gown for "Cheek to Cheek" (Top Hat, 1935), famously shedding through the number, is the other lesson: cloth that moves beautifully also moves unpredictably, and the camera keeps what the house forgets.
    • The pipeline chapter made the point from the movement side: shot size selects movement scale. It selects costume scale too. A gown that needs the wide shot and a face that needs the close-up are competing for the same frame.

    Busby Berkeley's overhead patterns are the limit: costume designed to be read from directly above, where the dancers are pixels and the garment is a color field. The house could never have seen it at all.

    The synthetic wardrobe¶

    Which brings the chapter to the dancer who is rendered rather than dressed. In the synthetic pipeline costume is a render-time decision: the motion is captured in fitted clothes (the capture chapter warned that skirts, coats, and long hair are occlusion generators) and the wardrobe is applied when the pixels are made. That freedom comes with its own physics, and two of its rules are now documented in the commons.

    Costume is the identity. Generative models hold a character by its distinctive features, and a generic outfit on a generic body drifts toward the model's mean — the dancer becomes a dancer. The practitioner rule is to give the figure one or two unambiguous, fixed-position anchors (an unusual color pairing, a signature accessory, an asymmetric marking) and to name them in every prompt, and to simplify whatever lives only on the back or side, because back-only details — floating hair pieces, asymmetric seams — are the first thing a model mangles when the shot turns. Sheets used to fix the look should be shot in flat light, since theatrical rim light on the reference bakes a light direction into the identity that then reappears, wrongly, under every downstream lighting setup.1 Translated into this chapter's terms: the costume's signal function is doing the identity work, and it should be designed the way the ballroom world designs — for legibility under adverse conditions — rather than the way the contemporary stage does, in matched neutrals that the model cannot tell apart.

    Whatever is constant gets welded on. A character LoRA trained on images that all show the same outfit makes that outfit part of the identity — documented cases of a hair color that became unpromptable because every training image had it. If the synthetic dancer is ever to change costume, the training set must vary costume, and the look is then applied at inference by prompt or by a separate style adapter (a starting balance of roughly character 0.8 / style 1.0 is reported, with full-step sampling because few-step schedules wash the style out). Otherwise plan on one trained identity per costume. Multi-dancer shots with two character adapters in one prompt bleed into each other unless each is masked to its own region — which is, not coincidentally, how the rendering chapter pins backgrounds too.2

    And one rule the commons holds only by implication: loose cloth costs motion. The rendering chapter found that a video model has a finite budget of movement per clip and that background drift spends it; flowing fabric spends it too, and spends it in the place the viewer looks. Until cloth simulation is a solved part of the render, the wardrobe choices that behave are the ones the history already produced for other reasons — the disc, the unitard, the fitted dress — and the Romantic bell and the bata de cola are the wardrobe's hardest problems for exactly the reason they are the stage's most beautiful.
