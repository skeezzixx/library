    The plausible body¶

    Every synthetic dancer is graded against a rubric the audience never writes down: the physics and anatomy of actual bodies, which every viewer has carried since learning to walk. The captured-body chapter showed the repair machinery — priors, simulation — that pulls generated motion back toward that rubric. This chapter writes the rubric out, because you cannot evaluate, prompt, or gate for plausibility without knowing what plausibility consists of, and because the list turns out to double as a design tool: it's the set of things a body does that a skeleton-in-a-video-model has no idea about.

    The reader of Part II will notice that most of what follows is the state that the gamut chapter insisted on typing — support, balance, momentum, weight — now stated as physics rather than as annotation.

    The body is an instrument with ranges¶

    Joints move within ranges, and trained dancers extend those ranges without abolishing them. The specific numbers matter less than the shape of the constraint:

    • Every joint has a range of motion, and ranges couple: hip flexion with a straight knee is limited by the hamstrings, so a high leg in front costs differently from one to the side; turnout is external hip rotation, trained far past the untrained default but still bounded, and faked below the hip (at the knee) exactly the way audiences can see.
    • The spine is segmented, not a hinge: flexion, extension, lateral bend, and rotation distribute across vertebrae, which is why a contraction in Graham technique reads as a wave and a rendered torso that bends at one point reads as a cardboard box.
    • Shoulders float — the shoulder girdle moves on the ribcage before the arm moves in the socket, so a real reach starts in the back; a reach that starts at the elbow is a puppet's.
    • Proportion is identity. Anthropometric ranges are narrow, and the eye is exquisitely tuned to them — limb-length ratios, head size, hand size. Retargeting captured motion onto a differently proportioned figure shifts contact timing (the capture chapter's warning), and a video model asked to animate a stylized figure with a realistically proportioned skeleton is being asked to resolve exactly this conflict.

    Balance: the center over the base¶

    A standing body is a controlled fall. Its center of mass must stay above its base of support — the footprint, or whatever is touching the floor — or it must be moving in a way that will bring a new base under it in time. Everything choreographic about weight lives here:

    • Weight transfer is the fundamental act, and incomplete weight transfer — a step that never fully commits — is the named root cause of most transition failures in pedagogy and of most "floaty" synthetic movement. A body mid-transfer is off-balance by definition, and the next movement must resolve that or the body falls.
    • Suspension (Humphrey's arc) is the instant at the top of a rise where vertical velocity is zero and the body hangs; it is legible precisely because it is brief and physically specific — hold it a frame too long and it reads as levitation.
    • Off-balance is a state, not an error. Falls, tilts, leans are material — but they write a debt (momentum, a shifted center) that a subsequent movement must pay. A generated body that leans without consequence is the uncanniest thing it can do.

    Momentum: the air is not negotiable¶

    Once the feet leave the floor, nothing about the trajectory of the center of mass can change — only the shape of the body around it. This single fact governs every jump and most turns:

    • Flight time is set at takeoff. A vertical jump of thirty centimeters is airborne for roughly half a second; no rendered dancer should hang for two. Apex height and flight time are locked together by gravity, and the eye knows the curve.
    • Angular momentum is conserved in the air: a turning jump speeds up when limbs pull in and slows as they open — which is also how a pirouette is controlled on the ground, through a pivot that adds friction torque. Spotting — the whip of the head — exists to manage perception, and its absence in a generated turn is legible even to viewers who couldn't name it.
    • Acceleration has a cost. Muscles produce bounded force, so sudden movements have ramp shapes: a "punch" (in the effort vocabulary) is a ballistic launch and then a deceleration, never a constant-velocity slide. Uniform easing — constant speed between poses — is a named pathology of interpolation precisely because no real limb moves that way.

    Contact and friction¶

    Feet on floors obey friction. A foot that is bearing weight does not translate; a foot that translates is not bearing weight — and the moment a rendered foot does both, the viewer sees foot-skate, the signature artifact of synthetic motion and the thing an entire tier of cleanup machinery exists to fix. Beyond feet:

    • Contact is bidirectional. A hand on a floor, a partner, or a prop supports weight and changes what the rest of the body may do; two bodies in contact share a balance problem.
    • Ground reaction shapes the body. Landing from a jump flexes knees and hips in proportion to the height fallen; a body that lands stiff either broke or is weightless.
    • The floor is a surface with a height. Floorwork — rolls, slides, inversions — is the region where capture, priors, and generators fail most, and the plausibility test is simple and brutal: which parts of the body are the floor holding up right now, and does the movement honor that?

    The eye reads bodies from almost nothing¶

    Here is the finding that should reorganize how you think about fidelity. In 1973, Gunnar Johansson attached small lights to the joints of walkers and filmed them in the dark: from a dozen moving dots and nothing else, viewers instantly perceived a walking person — and, in the studies that followed, could read gender, mood, effort, even identity of a friend, from the dots alone.1 Biological motion perception is a dedicated, fast, exquisitely sensitive system, and it works on skeleton dynamics, not on appearance.

    Two consequences for anyone rendering a dancer:

    • The skeleton carries the performance. A DWPose stick figure already contains most of what a viewer will feel about the movement; a beautifully rendered body on a wrong skeleton is a beautifully rendered wrong. Spend the fidelity budget on motion before pixels.
    • Dynamics, not poses. Johansson's dots are meaningless as stills. Timing, acceleration shape, the lag of a limb behind the torso — that is what the system reads, and it is exactly what interpolators get wrong while getting every individual frame right.

    Secondary motion and the breathing of stillness¶

    Real bodies are never still, and nothing attached to them moves in sync. Hair, cloth, and soft tissue lag — they arrive a beat after the skeleton and overshoot. A held pose breathes: the ribcage moves, weight shifts between feet in slow sway, the head makes small corrective motions. Animation codified this generations ago as follow-through and moving holds; generated video gets it for free when it gets it at all, and its absence is the "frozen" look — a pose that stopped instead of a body that paused. The rendering chapter returns to this when it discusses what a video model does with a held frame.

    The checklist¶

    Everything above, as the rubric it really is — the list to score a generated dancer against, by eye or by machine:

    • Support — which surfaces bear weight right now, and does the movement honor that?
    • Weight transfer — committed or incomplete? (Incomplete is the first suspect.)
    • Balance — center over base, or moving toward a new base in time?
    • Momentum — does the airborne trajectory obey gravity? Do turns conserve spin?
    • Acceleration shape — ballistic ramps, not constant-velocity slides.
    • Contact — planted feet stay planted; landings flex; hands that touch, hold.
    • Joint ranges — nothing beyond the envelope, and nothing faked below it.
    • Proportion — stable, human, and consistent with the motion's timing.
    • Skeleton first — would this read as a person as Johansson dots?
    • Secondary motion and breath — does stillness still live?

    The captured-body chapter's three repair tiers map onto this list almost item for item — geometric patches for contact, learned priors for ranges and proportion, physics for balance and momentum — which is the real argument for knowing the rubric: it tells you which machinery to reach for when a dancer looks wrong, and, better, what to ask of the generator so it looks right the first time.
