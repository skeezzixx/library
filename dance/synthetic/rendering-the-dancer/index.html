    Rendering the dancer¶

    Everything upstream — a gamut, a sequence, a gate, a captured or generated skeleton, a cleaned trajectory — becomes visible in exactly one place: the render. This chapter is about that last stage as it actually behaves in mid-2026, using the open-weights stack a working pipeline most plausibly runs on — WAN 2.2 and its VACE control branch — as the worked case. The particulars are WAN's; the structural lessons (motion is a budget, conditioning beats tuning, the preview must match the final) are not.

    A fair warning about this chapter's texture: it is the most hands-on in the subject, because the commons learned most of this at the bench, and the bench is where details earn their keep. The numbers here are kept on purpose.

    The stack, and which checkpoint you're really choosing¶

    WAN 2.2 remains the open-weights ceiling as of August 2026 (aggregator sites claiming "Wan 2.7" or "3.0" are fabricating; the official Hugging Face org is the only source of truth). It ships in shapes that matter:

    • TI2V-5B — dense, text- and image-conditioned in one checkpoint, runs a five-second 720p clip on an 8 GB-class card. Iterate here; practitioners report it gets ~90% of composition right.
    • A14B — a mixture-of-experts (two experts, ~14B active per step) needing a 24 GB card at 720p; separate, non-interchangeable T2V and I2V checkpoints. Finish here.
    • The Fun branch — task-specialized A14B checkpoints: -InP (start/end frames), -Control (canny/depth/pose/MLSD + trajectory), -Control-Camera (camera codes), and VACE-Fun, which unions the first two with reference images — and explicitly not camera control.

    That last fact is a structural choice, not a footnote: picking a checkpoint picks which controls you may combine in one generation. For character-driven work you stay on VACE-Fun and buy camera moves another way (below); true camera codes cost you the reference and endpoint conditioning a dancer's identity depends on.

    One protocol under every VACE trick¶

    VACE looks like a pile of features — first/last frame, inpaint, outpaint, pose-drive, reference injection. It is one conditioning protocol, and knowing it is worth more than knowing the recipes:

    • control_video — a full-length frame sequence carrying what you already know. Grey (127 / 0.5) is the literal zero of the control signal — the node subtracts 0.5 from every frame — so grey means "nothing specified, generate from prompt and references." Real pixels pin a frame's appearance; preprocessed frames (pose skeletons, depth, edges) pin structure.
    • control_masks — where control may act: white = regenerate, black = preserve. One mask frame broadcasts; a sequence lets the editable region move over time.
    • reference_image — VAE-encoded and prepended to the control latents (hence the trim_latent output you must honor downstream).
    • strength — how literally to obey, independent of LoRA weights; the first dial to lower when control fights identity.

    Hard constraints: dimensions divisible by 16, frame counts of the form 4n+1 (81, 97, 121). Once the protocol is clear, the "features" collapse: first/last-frame is real pixels at frames 0 and N with grey between; keyframing is the same at any indices; outpainting is grey beyond the original frame with the mask white there; pose-driving is skeletons all the way through. You aren't choosing a mode — you're choosing what to paint and what to leave grey.

    Keyframes: the acting lives in the stills¶

    With first/last-frame conditioning, both stills are enforced as ground truth inside the diffusion — genuine generative in-betweening, not optical-flow interpolation — and the model treats every difference between the two frames as something to explain with motion. Mismatched key light becomes an invented lighting change; mismatched depth of field an invented focus pull; a wide first frame and a tight last frame an invented camera move. So the craft is upstream: make the pair agree on everything except the intended motion.

    And because the model has no notion of intent, only correspondence, the keyframes carry the acting. Classic posing craft exists to make a single drawing unambiguous, and that is all the model has. The checklist for a generated pair:

    • Match key-light angle and depth of field/framing explicitly between the stills.
    • Anchor contact points identically — a planted foot sits in the same place in both frames unless it is meant to travel.
    • Pose the eyeline in both; gaze is content the model can only interpolate, never infer.
    • Prompt the arrival, not just the departure — the model prioritizes prompt coherence and will otherwise ignore your endpoint.

    Then the ceiling: keyframe pose distance has a plausibility limit, past which interpolation morphs. In-betweeners take the shortest path through pixel space; articulated, non-rigid motion — limbs, running, a dancer — breaks first; far-apart poses morph rather than move. (In the latent-space chapter's terms, a straight line between two valid frames cuts through invalid space, and morphing is what leaving the manifold looks like.) The working rules: one clear beat per generation, within three to five seconds (81 frames ≈ 3 s, 121 ≈ 5 s); prompt the easing explicitly, because constant-velocity easing reads as robotic; separate camera motion from character motion.

    The highest-leverage move, though, is the generalization: VACE pins keyframes at any index — grey everywhere, real frames where you want them, mask black there — and an interior keyframe doesn't merely add control, it geometrically forbids the shortcut, because the cheap path no longer satisfies the constraints. One pin at the apex of a beat — the top of a reach, the extreme of a turn — converts the distance ceiling from a cap into a budget: two hops of forty percent instead of one of eighty. Costs: each keyframe is another still that must agree with its neighbors (the mismatch rule applies to each gap independently), and more pins leave less room for the model to invent the connective motion you actually wanted.

    Keyframes beat chaining for the same span. A single long shot is made by seeding clip N+1 with clip N's last frame — extracted before VAE decode, never from the compressed video — but nothing crosses the seam: no velocity, no momentum; generators decelerate toward their own last frame, so naive joins stutter; and drift (color, lighting, identity) is directional and compounds, because each segment builds on the previous output, not the original reference. Keyframes inside one generation carry momentum across the pins. When you must chain: describe continuous low-magnitude motion across the seam, trim the deceleration tail, re-ground on the original reference periodically — or cut instead, which resets drift for free.

    The motion budget is a number¶

    WAN 2.2's two experts are not halves of one thing. The high-noise expert runs the early steps and decides composition and motion; the low-noise expert refines detail. The handoff is by signal-to-noise, not step index — a trained boundary at sigma 0.875 (T2V) or 0.900 (I2V) — so shift plus total steps decide how many steps land above the boundary: your literal motion budget. The ComfyUI default template (shift 8, 20 steps) splits 10/10; the popular 4-step accelerated recipe splits 2/2 — all of a clip's motion planned in two sampling steps.

    That is the mechanism behind the best-known complaint about the lightx2v speed LoRAs: they cut render time ~5× and measurably flatten motion and camera movement (maintainer-acknowledged; the model card claims otherwise — trust the issue tracker). Community mitigations are starting points, not settled numbers: asymmetric strength (~0.6–0.8 on the high-noise expert, 1.0 low), eight steps split 4+4, real CFG (~2–3.5) on the high pass only. And one project rule falls out with force: the preview must share the final's motion settings — LoRA strengths, steps, shift, CFG — or every motion judgment you made while iterating was made on a different film. Either bench with final settings, or accept that previews tell you about framing and identity and nothing about motion.

    Prompt for change, not appearance¶

    WAN was trained against cinematically labelled data, so film vocabulary is a real control surface. In an image-conditioned pipeline the stills already carry subject, costume, palette, and set — spend the prompt on what changes: motion and camera. The vocabulary it answers to:

    • Moves — dolly in, pan left/right, tilt, pull back, tracking, arc, crane, handheld.
    • Lock — "static shot" / "fixed shot." Say it. Omitting camera language does not yield a locked camera; it yields an unconstrained one, and drift toward invented movement is the default.
    • Shot sizes, angles, lenses — the standard film lexicon, all of it live.
    • Speed and direction verbs — "slowly, toward camera" beats generic action words; staged phrasing ("begins with minimal movement, gradually increases") works.
    • Selective locking — "face and eyes remain still, only hair moves."

    Source hygiene matters here: the SEO layer around WAN fabricates parameters — a "motion guidance strength" dial, CFG 7.0–7.5 recommendations — that correspond to no real node input. If advice doesn't name an actual node field, it's invented.

    The background and the dancer draw on one budget¶

    Background drift — the set slowly sliding, warping, swimming under a nominally locked camera — is structural, not a tuning failure: the model has no 3D awareness, so "the camera moved" and "the world changed shape" explain the same pixels, and it picks either. It compounds with length (regularly past ~8 s). And the tension to internalize: there is no separate subject-motion and background-motion control. Every knob that buys a livelier dancer — more high-noise steps, higher shift, lower speed-LoRA strength — buys it for the whole frame. Fix a listless dancer and you've given the wall permission to breathe. Prompt patches bias the split; they don't partition it.

    So decouple by conditioning, not tuning. The objection "my subject is dancing, I can't mask it" assumes the mask must trace the subject. It doesn't: make the control video your background plate repeated every frame, and the mask black everywhere except one generous white region containing the subject's whole range of motion. You are reserving territory, not cutting a silhouette — authored once per camera setup, reused across every clip that shares the framing. Everything outside the envelope is real pixels and cannot drift; inside it, push motion as hard as the shot wants. Costs: the subject can't leave the region; include the floor-contact zone; and a pinned plate forbids a camera move by construction.

    When that envelope also carries a pose skeleton inside it — the combination a dancing character actually needs — one more rule, learned the hard way: a pose patch must sit on grey, not its native black. Black isn't background in a control video; it's a strong instruction meaning dark content here. Full-frame pose videos get away with black because the whole frame reads as one modality; a mixed control video doesn't, and the symptom is unmistakable — a crisp dark frame tracing the envelope boundary. Re-composite the skeleton onto grey 127, and keep the envelope mask binary — a feathered edge is half-specified dark content at half-preservation weight, rendered as a soft dark ring. Residual skeleton hues can still ghost onto the subject (teal torso lines on white fabric; the skeleton's pink eye dots as makeup); what works is CFG ~3 on the high-noise pass plus negatives naming the artifacts, and more denoising steps at finals — safe here because the pose rail specifies motion externally. What does not work: dimming the lines toward grey (tracking degrades before ghosting disappears) and desaturating the palette (hue encodes limb identity on a near-symmetric skeleton — you buy left/right errors, not cleanliness).

    Sparse control over dense; keyframe before control track¶

    VACE-Fun accepts canny, depth, pose, MLSD, and trajectory control in the same checkpoint as reference images and endpoints. They are not equivalent for a stylized dancer:

    • Pose (DWPose skeleton) — sparse; transfers timing and articulation only. The driving source can be phone video of yourself, or a posed rig. The risk is proportion: an adult skeleton drives adult proportions and timing onto whatever figure you're rendering.
    • Depth / canny / MLSD — dense; they transfer the driving footage's shapes, rendering, and volume — actively harmful when the look is chosen to be forgiving of noise.
    • Trajectory — the lightest contract: where a thing goes without dictating how the body gets there. Path constrained, articulation invented.

    And the sequencing rule: keyframes pin where the body is; a control track governs how it gets between them. Reach for the keyframe first — it costs a still, not a performance — and add a control track only when the in-between is what's failing.

    The camera, by rungs¶

    Seven mechanisms, ordered by determinism and integration cost: prompt words; the move encoded in the keyframe geometry; VACE canvas/outpaint; community camera LoRAs (Wan 2.1-era); the Control-Camera checkpoint's actual presets; trajectory instructions; 3D-grounded re-rendering. The highest value per effort is rung two, and it's an inversion of a hazard: a framing delta between the two stills is an invented camera move when accidental and a deterministic one when constructed — generate the still with headroom, derive the end frame as a tighter or shifted crop, and the push or pan is arithmetic rather than obedience. The limit is parallax: these are 2D moves. And the checkpoint split above means rung five is a model swap that surrenders reference conditioning — reserve it for shots with no identity burden.

    Identity: the LoRA, and the loss that lies¶

    A character LoRA for WAN wants 25–40 images across five-plus angles, three lightings, three expressions; a reported 12 GB recipe is rank 32, ~10 epochs, 512 px. Two traps: T2V and I2V LoRAs are not interchangeable — train on the checkpoint type you'll run; and training loss is pixel reconstruction, not identity — low loss does not mean the character looks right. Visual spot-checks every few epochs are the real gate.

    What the eye demands of the frames¶

    Finally, the perceptual floor any render must clear. Apparent motion is a cortical correspondence computation, not retinal persistence: small per-frame displacements relative to the moving shape read as smooth; past a threshold, a slower feature-tracking process takes over and motion reads as judder — so keep displacement small relative to the object, not just the frame rate high. Pin-sharp frames look stroboscopic at any rate; real motion blur is a shutter-angle property (the 180° convention), and generated footage that lacks it needs it added deliberately. Spinning material past half its repeat unit per frame aliases — the wagon-wheel effect — which is a live hazard for turns. These are the reasons a dancer can be perfectly posed in every frame and still look wrong in motion.

    Two routes, one choice¶

    Everything above is the explicit route: motion first, then pixels, with a gate between and a skeleton you can inspect. The generation chapter introduced its alternative — Wan-Dancer and MACE-Dance generating dance video directly from music. Convenience and temporal coherence on one side; control, editability, and a choreographer's hand on the keyframes on the other. For a pipeline built on authored material and delegated continuity, the explicit route is native and the direct route is the benchmark to beat. How the whole chain assembles, end to end, is the last chapter of this part.
