    Generating movement¶

    Part VII · The synthetic dancer — chapter 3 of 5 · ← The captured body: video to skeleton · Rendering the dancer →

    The capture chapter got movement from a body; the emergent-locomotion chapter got it from physics and objectives. This chapter is about the third source, and the one most people mean by "AI dance": models that generate dance motion from music or text. The field is five years old, fast, and — this is the honest frame — has spent those five years rediscovering, in its own vocabulary, most of what Part V already knew. What follows is the mid-2026 state: the lineage, the mechanisms, the failure modes, the metrics and their problems, what's freely usable, and a genuinely new development that changes the pipeline's shape.

    The lineage, briefly¶

    Music-to-dance generation has a clean family tree, and every branch emits the same thing — a sequence of SMPL body poses, no fingers, the convention set by EDGE that the whole field follows (which is what makes the outputs interchangeable downstream):

    • AIST++ and FACT (2021) — Google's dataset (5.2 hours, ten genres, multi-view) and a transformer baseline: the benchmark that made the field.
    • Bailando (2022) — the VQ codebook plus GPT sequencer of the discovery chapter: a learned vocabulary of poses, composed to music.
    • EDGE (2023) — a transformer diffusion model conditioned on Jukebox audio features, and editable: because diffusion fills in whatever you mask, you can pin joints or time ranges and let the model complete the rest — keyframe the dancer, in effect.1
    • Lodge and Lodge++ (2024–25) — the long-form answer: a coarse network first emits sparse characteristic dance primitives, then a diffusion model fills between them; Lodge++ bolts on a penetration-guidance module, a foot-refinement module, and a multi-genre discriminator.2
    • 2025–26 — diffusion is the default, and the edge is in conditioning and scale: ChoreDiffusion injects beat guidance directly into denoising and currently leads the FineDance benchmark on all five standard metrics;3 MotionRAG-Diff retrieves real phrases to anchor long sequences; Danceba and SoulDance (ICCV 2025) refine rhythm-awareness and whole-body hierarchy; TCDiff++ generates group choreography with controllable trajectories; DanceEditor allows iterative open-vocabulary edits; DiscoForcing runs audio-driven control in real time; AtomicDance (ECCV 2026) composes from atomic movements.4

    The benchmarks moved with the models: AIST++ for short-clip fidelity, FineDance — twenty-two genres, dances averaging two and a half minutes — for long-form.

    Text-to-motion, the general-purpose engine underneath, moved in parallel: from MoMask's residual tokens to masked-autoregressive diffusion, next-scale autoregression, token-latent flow models, and streaming generators that emit motion causally; motion-language models (MotionGPT-2, UniMuMo for text, music, and motion together); and datasets that finally admit the long-horizon gap — Motion-X++ for whole bodies, OpenT2M with sequences averaging over ten seconds.5

    How they work, in one layer each¶

    Strip the papers and three mechanisms cover the field:

    • Tokenize and sequence — quantize poses into a codebook, predict token sequences with a transformer (Bailando, the MoMask line). Compact, composable, editable at the token level; loses fine detail to the codebook.
    • Diffuse over motion — treat the whole pose sequence as a signal to denoise, conditioned on audio or text features (EDGE and almost everything since). Smooth, high-fidelity, natively editable by masking; prone to averaging toward "the typical dance" for a given input.
    • Plan coarse, fill fine — emit sparse primitives or keyframes first, then generate between them (Lodge, Wan-Dancer's keyframe planning, retrieval-augmented variants). The only mechanism that holds up past a minute.

    Notice what the third one is. Long-form is solved by hierarchy or retrieval, never by longer context — plan the skeleton of the dance, then fill the flesh — which is the chance chapter's two layers again, and the craft chapter's "design the ending early" in code.

    What the machines keep rediscovering, part two¶

    The discovery chapter observed that generative systems keep re-deriving a codebook plus a sequencer. The generation literature adds a fourth generation of evidence, and it's worth listing because the pattern is now hard to dismiss:

    • Bailando's codebook of dance poses.
    • EDGE's editable joints — pins a choreographer places, diffusion fills.
    • Lodge's characteristic dance primitives — sparse, authored-feeling anchors.
    • AtomicDance's atomic movements.
    • Wan-Dancer's global keyframe planning.

    Every strong system has a primitives-or-keyframes layer and a fill layer. Nobody set out to rebuild the gamut+sequencer split; it is what works, and it's what the benchmarks reward when sequences get long enough for structure to matter.

    Two more readings follow from the record. Physics is patched on, not learned: Lodge++'s penetration and foot modules, and the very existence of a foot-contact metric, say that diffusion over poses does not know bodies have weight — the cleanup tiers still apply to generated motion exactly as to captured motion. And there is no human gate in the benchmark loop: models are trained and scored without anyone selecting, which is the structural reason their output tends toward genre-typical mush. The architecture chapter's gate isn't a nicety these systems lack; it's the missing component that the Cunningham-to-AISOMA record says is load-bearing.

    Where they fail¶

    The failure modes are consistent across papers and demos, and each has a name you now know:

    • Foot-skate and floating — weight never committed; the plausible-body rubric's first item.
    • Self-penetration — limbs through torso; Lodge++ added a module specifically for it.
    • Beat drift — alignment decays over long sequences; retrieval and keyframe planning exist to fight it.
    • Mush in the middle — the diffusion average: every phrase plausible, the whole unmemorable; no climax, no return, no form — the thing the craft chapter warned about under "all dances are too long."
    • Genre collapse — conditioning on genre is weak; multi-genre discriminators are the patch.
    • Anatomical implausibility at extremes — exactly where dance is interesting.

    How they're judged — and why that's suspect¶

    The standard metric suite is worth knowing because you'll meet it in every paper and because its weaknesses are instructive:

    • FID_k and FID_g — Fréchet distance between generated and real dances in a kinetic feature space (velocities, accelerations) and a geometric one (pose shapes). A distance between distributions, not a quality score for any single dance.
    • Div_k and Div_g — average pairwise distance in those spaces; spread.
    • PFC, Physical Foot Contact — EDGE's physics proxy: when the hip is accelerating, a foot should be planted; score the violations. Cheap, reference-free, catches skating.
    • BAS, Beat Alignment Score — how closely kinematic beats (local minima of joint speed) land on music beats. Rewards accents on beats — and penalizes a dancer playing deliberately behind the beat, which is to say it measures alignment, not musicality.

    The critique is now in the literature itself: EDGE argued FID is unreliable for dance and ran human studies; 2025 papers report FID rankings contradicting human preference and push user studies as primary evidence.6 Read through this library's lens, nothing here is surprising. Distribution metrics are Goodhart targets; the checkable tier — PFC, penetration counts, beat alignment — should gate obvious failures, and the judged tier decides whether it's any good. Missing from the suite entirely: any measure of long-form structure (repetition, return, climax), genre fidelity beyond a classifier, and performability — could a body do this? That last absence is the whole gap between a benchmark and a dance.

    The direct-to-video newcomers¶

    Until recently "generate dance" meant generate motion, then render it somehow. Two 2026 systems collapse the pipeline, and they're shaped exactly like yours:

    • MACE-Dance (SIGGRAPH 2026) cascades two experts — a Motion Expert that generates music-aligned 3D SMPL motion, and an Appearance Expert that renders it to video — choosing 3D SMPL over 2D keypoints explicitly for spatial fidelity and robustness. Code is open; the appearance weights are gated.7
    • Wan-Dancer-14B (Wan-AI, July 2026) generates long, rhythmic dance videos directly from music, with weights and inference code released — global keyframe planning over the full track for structure, local temporal refinement for continuity.8 It is the WAN family's own end-to-end answer, which makes it the convenience counterpart to the controllable, pose-conditioned route the rendering chapter describes.

    The trade-off is the usual one and worth stating plainly: direct-to-video buys convenience and temporal coherence; the explicit motion-then-render pipeline buys control — a gate between motion and pixels, editability, a skeleton you can inspect against the plausible-body rubric, and a choreographer's hand on the keyframes. For a project whose whole thesis is authored material and delegated continuity, the explicit route is the native one; the direct route is the thing to benchmark it against.

    What's usable, free, today¶

    • EDGE — code and weights; the editable baseline.
    • Lodge / Lodge++ — code; the long-form reference.
    • Danceba — pretrained weights on Hugging Face (ICCV 2025).
    • Wan-Dancer-14B — weights and inference code (music → video directly).
    • MACE-Dance — code open, appearance weights on request.

    All the motion generators emit SMPL without fingers, which slots directly into the retargeting and cleanup paths of the capture chapter.

    For a working pipeline¶

    Put the chapter's findings to work and the role of these models is clear: they are proposal machines over a vocabulary, not choreographers. Let a generator propose phrases — conditioned on your music, edited at the joints and beats you care about — then run the proposals through the same gate the human tradition never abandoned: the checkable tier (contact, penetration, alignment) by machine, the judged tier by a person, and cleanup (priors, then physics) before any of it reaches a renderer. Used that way, the mush problem and the missing gate solve each other: the generator's breadth becomes a draw of candidates, and curation supplies the form.
