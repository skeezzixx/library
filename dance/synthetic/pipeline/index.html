    The pipeline, end to end¶

    Part VII has walked through the stages one at a time. This chapter puts them in a row, says who — human, rule system, model, simulator — owns each, names the budgets that bind across stages, and then adds the part that no single stage owns: composing for the camera, which turns out to be a design decision taken at authoring time, not a coverage decision taken afterward. It closes with one phrase walked through the whole chain.

    The stages, and their owners¶

    • 1 · The gamut — a small, typed, hand-authored vocabulary with connectors and state-changing verbs first-class (Building a gamut); discovery models may propose candidates (Machine-discovered vocabularies), a human names and types them. Owner: human.
    • 2 · The sequencer — chance, rule, or model, owning an enumerated list of continuity dimensions and nothing else (Chance and rules); generators (Generating movement) are sequencers with a learned vocabulary, useful as proposal machines. Owner: system.
    • 3 · The legality check — the typed pre/post chain, window metric, symmetry quotiented out, one strongly connected component (The architecture). Owner: machine tier.
    • 4 · The gate — interest and performability, kept distinct; throughput budgeted; proposals rendered at the fidelity that fits the human's role. Owner: human tier.
    • 5 · Motion realization — a body performs it and is captured (The captured body), a generator emits it, or physics and objectives produce it (Locomotion without a choreographer). Owner: body, model, or simulator.
    • 6 · Cleanup — geometric patches, learned priors, physics simulation, chosen by failure mode and scored against the plausible-body rubric. Owner: machine tier, human spot-check.
    • 7 · Render — skeleton and keyframes into pixels; background pinned by envelope, pose on grey, motion budget set and matched between preview and final (Rendering the dancer). Owner: model, under conditioning.
    • 8 · Camera and cut — this chapter. Owner: human, at authoring time.

    The budgets that bind¶

    Several quantities are spent across stages and are easy to overspend because no single stage sees the whole bill:

    • The motion budget — in the renderer, literally the high-noise step count; upstream, the one-beat-per-clip rule. Camera motion and character motion draw on it together; a pinned background takes the set out of it.
    • The keyframe budget — every pin is a still that must agree with its neighbors on light, palette, and framing; more pins forbid more shortcuts and leave less for the model to invent.
    • The gate's throughput — candidates per hour a human can actually judge; the generator's output is sized to it, and the machine tier keeps anything obviously invalid from reaching it.
    • Camera-move slots — deterministic 2D moves are cheap to make and still cost a slot in the shot grammar; a locked camera is the default and a move is spent, not free.
    • Level-changing units — the expensive transitions in rehearsal and in rendering; keep them a minority of the vocabulary and rate-limit them per phrase.

    Compose for the camera at authoring time¶

    Here is the stage-to-screen inversion that the rest of this part has been building toward. Humphrey's stage rule — movement looks slower and weaker from the house, so scale it up — runs backward on camera: the lens magnifies. A passage built on breath, a hand, a face is viable material on screen and simply isn't on stage. Which means shot size is a parameter of the composition, not a coverage decision:

    • Wide carries travel, floor pattern, level change, jumps — and loses face and hands.
    • Full (body fills frame) carries whole-body line — the documentation default — and loses the environment.
    • Mid carries torso-led movement, gesture, weight shift — and crops the feet, so footwork and travel vanish.
    • Close carries face, hands, feet, breath — "micro-choreography" — and nothing spatial.

    Two design rules follow. Match material to frame when you author it: pathway-rich and level-rich phrases only pay in wide coverage; gaze- and gesture-rich phrases want mid or close; floor material wants a floor-height camera, because high angles flatten floorwork into a pattern on the ground. And scale counterpoint is a form — alternating macro sections (travel, level, wide) with micro sections (hands, face, breath, close) is screen-native, has no stage equivalent, and is the solo performer's substitute for the textural contrast an ensemble gets for free. The shoot rule that falls out: plan three frames — wide, mid, close — and shoot (or render) each phrase at two or more of them, so every moment exists in more than one size. That redundancy is what supplies the edit's cut points; it isn't a hedge against a bad take. For a generated pipeline the same logic decides what a generation is asked to contain: a wide shot asks the model for whole-body travel, a close-up for a face and a hand — very different reliability propositions, and a reason to keep one moving region per clip.

    Lens facts worth carrying: wide and normal lenses close to the performer preserve toward-and-away dynamics; telephoto compresses depth and kills them — save the long lens for faces and detail, and shoot Z-axis material wide and close in.

    Author the cut points into the material¶

    Editing is usually done to finished material. The stronger move is to schedule the cut points into the performance — roughly one every phrase or two — as a constraint on the generator. Five performed events double as edit licenses:

    • A hold or stillness — tolerates a cut anywhere inside it; the most permissive edit point there is, and the reason stillness earned first-class status in the vocabulary.
    • An accent or hit — cut on the impact; the eye tracks the action across the join and the cut disappears.
    • A turn or spin — a rotation resets the eye-line, which licenses a camera-position change that would otherwise read as a jump. Turns are edit enablers, not just spatial variety.
    • A pass through neutral — weight centered, standing: any two takes align there.
    • An exit from frame — total freedom about what enters next.

    Two structural properties extend the list. Loop closure: a phrase whose final pose matches its opening in shape, weight, facing, and near-zero velocity is loopable and re-orderable — the transition-legality condition applied to one phrase reflexively; make at least the recurring phrase a perfect loop and the edit can repeat or reorder it at will. And Deren's principle, from A Study in Choreography for Camera (1945): temporal continuity survives spatial discontinuity — a movement begun in one location can complete in another, the movement carries the cut while the space goes free. That is the license to shoot one phrase across locations, sets, wardrobe states, or generated backdrops, and in a rendered pipeline it's why a continuous action is the safest thing to place across a background change. Her other line generalizes further: the compositional unit is "whatever is in the frame, including the space," not the body.

    The discipline that still applies: cut on the beat but not on every beat (off-rhythm shot changes read as a palpable disconnect; constant cutting disorients); long takes of fifteen seconds and up read as respect for the movement, fast cutting masks technique; the 180° line still flips a performer's direction; and Astaire's rule — either the camera dances or the performer does — means one mover per moment. And the fallback that keeps it all honest: any sequence that passes the legality check is performable in one take, so coverage buys options; it never rescues a join that was illegal.

    One phrase, through the chain¶

    An eight-count phrase, start to finish:

    1. Gamut. Units drawn from the vocabulary — a travel, a reach to apex, a turn, a pass through neutral, a hold — each with its signature; the turn and the hold chosen partly because they are edit licenses.
    2. Sequence and check. The sequencer orders them; the legality graph confirms the chain — facing relative, momentum honored, the weight transfer into the turn complete. The transition into the reach starts on count six of the previous unit, as transitions do.
    3. Gate. Of several candidate chains, a person picks the one that reads as necessary, and confirms a body could do it in the space.
    4. Motion. A dancer performs it and is captured (GVHMR from the footage, or a two-webcam FreeMocap rig) — or a generator proposes it over the same units; either way it's cleaned: contact pinned, a prior for the jitter, physics if the floor is involved.
    5. Skeleton and keyframes. The cleaned motion becomes a DWPose control track composited onto grey inside a binary envelope mask; the background plate repeats outside it, pinned. One interior keyframe at the reach's apex forbids the shortcut. Three stills — opening, apex, close — agree on light, depth of field, and the planted foot.
    6. Render. VACE-Fun, 81 frames, a static shot stated in the prompt and the prompt spent on the motion arriving at the apex; motion settings identical between the iteration pass and the final; steps raised at finals to scrub the skeleton ghost.
    7. Frame and cut. The phrase exists at wide and at mid, so the travel reads in one and the reach in the other; the edit cuts on the turn into the mid shot, holds through the stillness, and — because the phrase closes its loop — can repeat it under the chorus.

    Every stage in that walk is one of this part's chapters, and every budget is spent in view.

    What this part leaves open¶

    The pipeline is complete from gamut to frame, but the frame isn't finished: the dancer wears something, stands somewhere, is lit somehow, and moves to music. Those are Parts VI and IV, and the plausible-body rubric is the only piece of them the pipeline already holds. The synthetic dancer, in other words, now has a body, a vocabulary, a system, and a camera — and is waiting on a costume, a stage, and a score.
