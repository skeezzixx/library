    The captured body: video to skeleton to animation¶

    Part VII · The synthetic dancer — chapter 2 of 5 · ← The plausible body · Generating movement →

    Part VII begins here, pulled forward by a practical question: if you point a camera at a dancer — or at seventy years of dance footage — what can you actually extract, with what tools, at what cost, and how do you make the extracted skeleton move like a person instead of like a haunted marionette? This chapter maps the pipeline end to end as it stands in mid-2026, with particular attention to the two things worth caring about most: what's genuinely free, and the increasingly clever machinery — learned priors, physics simulation — that separates plausible animation from sliding, jittering approximation.1

    The pipeline has five stages, and it helps to name them up front, because tools slot into stages, not into the whole:

    video → 2D keypoints → 3D body in world space → cleanup → retarget to a rig → render

    The interchange body: SMPL¶

    First, the format that makes the ecosystem hang together. Nearly all current research emits SMPL (or its hands-and-face extension SMPL-X): a parametric human body — a fixed-topology mesh driven by per-joint rotations plus a handful of shape parameters. It matters to you for one reason: it is the lingua franca. Pose estimators emit it, motion generators consume it, converters to FBX and Blender exist and are boring. When evaluating any tool in this space, "can it get to SMPL?" is the portability question, the way "can it export stems?" is in audio.

    Stage one: 2D keypoints¶

    Per-frame 2D pose detection is mature, fast, and free — MediaPipe's 33-landmark body, and the RTMPose/DWPose family that produces the colored-limb skeletons your video-generation stack already consumes as control input. Two things worth knowing: 2D is done as a research problem for ordinary footage (the interesting failures are all downstream), and 2D alone is already useful — a DWPose skeleton drives pose-conditioned video generation directly, no 3D lift required. The 3D stages below exist for when you need the body, not just the picture of one: editing, retargeting, physics, or building a motion corpus.

    Stage two: the lift, and the world-grounding problem¶

    Lifting 2D detections to a 3D body was never really the hard part per-frame; the hard part is world grounding — recovering the global trajectory and orientation so that a dancer who travels, turns, and jumps does so in the world instead of swimming in place at the camera's origin. This is precisely the dance-relevant half: travel and facing are choreography.

    The state of the art (single ordinary camera, no markers, no suit):

    • WHAM (CVPR 2024) made world grounding respectable — motion context plus learned foot-contact cues to pin trajectory to the ground.2
    • GVHMR (SIGGRAPH Asia 2024; TPAMI 2026) is the current default. Its trick is a coordinate system: predict pose in gravity-view coordinates — a frame defined by gravity plus the camera ray, uniquely inferable per image — which removes an ambiguity the network otherwise wastes capacity resolving. It emits SMPL-X, grounds camera-space motion more accurately than WHAM, and its core network is roughly seven times faster (~0.28 s vs ~2.0 s); on the standard per-pose accuracy benchmark the two are within a fraction of a millimeter.3
    • OnlineHMR (CVPR 2026) does the same job causally — streaming, for live settings.

    All of this is research code: free on GitHub, real to run (a GPU, a Python environment, no support desk), and worth it when you want the best monocular result on footage you don't control — archival dance video being the canonical case.

    When you do control the room, triangulate instead. Multiple cheap cameras beat one clever network. FreeMocap — free and open source — turns two or more ordinary webcams into a markerless rig: MediaPipe's 553 landmarks per frame (body, both hands, face) triangulated into 3D, exported to native Blender, FBX, or CSV; its April 2026 release added reprojection-outlier rejection, so one badly placed camera no longer corrupts the whole solve.4 Its sibling Pose2Sim takes any camera mix (phones, GoPros) into an OpenSim biomechanical skeleton — the tool when joint kinematics need to be right, not just plausible-looking.5

    What dance does to all of this¶

    Dance is close to a worst case for capture, and the failure modes are specific enough to plan around:6

    • Floorwork breaks the priors. Standing-biased pose priors and foot-contact heuristics misfire when weight is on the back, side, or hands — the same standing bias the notation systems carry, now baked into models trained on walking-heavy data. Rolls and inversions are where monocular methods hallucinate hardest; multi-camera capture is the honest fix.
    • Spins flip orientation. Left/right ambiguity under fast rotation produces the characteristic snap where the body turns 180° between frames. (Recall that near-symmetric skeletons carry limb identity almost entirely in subtle appearance cues — at speed, those cues blur away.)
    • Fast motion blurs and jumps. Large inter-frame displacement defeats tracking; power moves defeat it so thoroughly that BRACE — the breakdancing dataset — had to be partly annotated by hand because automatic capture failed on exactly the material it exists to cover. Shoot 60 fps or better for anything percussive.
    • Costume occludes. Skirts, coats, long hair, hats — everything the staged-body chapters will celebrate is an occlusion generator. Capture passes want fitted clothing; wardrobe is a render-time decision, which pose-conditioned video generation happily accommodates.

    The standard benchmark, incidentally, is AIST++: 5.2 hours, 1,408 sequences, ten genres, multi-view with known cameras — worth knowing both as a corpus and as the substrate most music-to-dance generation is trained on.7

    The clever part: making it move like a person¶

    Raw output from every one of these systems shares a look: feet that skate, joints that jitter, poses that are individually fine and collectively boneless. The repair techniques are where the field has gotten genuinely clever, and they sort into three tiers by what the fix can see — a tiering worth internalizing because it recurs everywhere in this territory.

    Tier one: geometric patches. Detect foot-contact frames from velocity and height, then pin the foot with inverse kinematics for the contact's duration; filter the jitter. Cheap, local, ships as a checkbox in commercial tools ("foot locking"). It fixes the single most visible artifact and nothing else — and a pin that fights the data can buy you a knee pop in exchange for the slide.

    Tier two: learned priors — denoising by projection. Train a generative model of how humans actually move — VPoser for single poses, HuMoR for whole trajectories, motion-diffusion models as the current generation — then pull noisy estimates toward what the model believes.8 Readers of the latent-spaces chapter will recognize this instantly: it is the manifold, used as a repair strategy — off-manifold capture gets projected back onto the thin surface of plausible human movement. And it inherits the manifold's caveat with teeth: a prior is only as good as its corpus's coverage, so a prior trained on walking-heavy mocap will "correct" your floorwork toward standing. For dance, check what the prior was fed before letting it edit.

    Tier three: physics — legalization by embodiment. The deepest fix, and the one most worth understanding. Train a reinforcement-learning controller that drives a simulated humanoid — mass, joint limits, friction, gravity — to track your noisy reference motion: the DeepMimic line (2018) through AMP to PHC (Perpetual Humanoid Control), the current workhorse, which tracks large noisy corpora robustly.9 The output is whatever the simulated body actually did — and therefore feet cannot slide (friction is real), momentum is conserved, balance is kept or visibly lost. Physics repairs what the other tiers cannot even represent: forces, weight, contact — precisely the fields the gamut chapter called the treacherous ones, and pedagogy's root cause of transition failure. Seen from this subject's angle, tier three is the legality graph gone continuous: simulation as a checker that cannot be argued with. The costs: an RL training loop, and a subtle one — the controller is itself a performer with an accent, and extreme stylization comes back slightly flattened. Sound familiar? It's the LifeForms lesson again: every tool's easy path shows up in the output.

    The tiers compose — prior-then-physics is the strong pipeline — and choosing by failure mode keeps it cheap: skating feet, tier one; jitter and odd poses, tier two; impossible dynamics or contact-rich floorwork, tier three.

    And there's an interactive version of the whole idea. Cascadeur — a standalone keyframe animator with a genuinely usable free tier — packages the priors inside the authoring loop rather than as after-the-fact repair: AutoPosing completes a natural pose from a few dragged handles (a learned pose prior as a direct-manipulation tool), and AutoPhysics nudges keyframed motion toward ballistic and momentum correctness while preserving the animator's intent.10 It is, in this subject's vocabulary, a human gate with a physics-literate assistant — worth knowing even if your pipeline is mostly generative, because it's the fastest way to hand-fix the ten seconds the pipeline got wrong.

    Stage five: retargeting, the quiet quality-killer¶

    Captured motion lands on your character through retargeting — mapping one skeleton's motion onto another's proportions — and this unglamorous step is where free pipelines usually lose their realism. Different limb lengths shift contact timing; a clean capture retargeted carelessly skates again. The free path is settled: Blender (with Rokoko's free plugin or native tooling) and Mixamo for auto-rigging; Cascadeur's retargeting sits in its paid tier. Budget a cleanup pass after every retarget, and treat rig choice as an aesthetic decision — proportions are style, as anyone who has watched the same motion on two differently-built dancers already knows.

    The free stack, summarized¶

    Tool Stage What it does Cost
    MediaPipe / DWPose 2D keypoints; VACE control skeletons free
    GVHMR / WHAM / OnlineHMR 3D lift world-grounded SMPL(-X) from one video free (research code, GPU)
    FreeMocap 3D multi-cam webcams → triangulated 3D → Blender/FBX free, open source
    Pose2Sim 3D multi-cam biomechanics-grade kinematics free, open source
    Rokoko Vision cloud lift video → FBX, plugins for Blender/Unity/UE free ≤15 s clips
    DeepMotion cloud lift video → FBX, foot-lock modes free ~60 s/month
    Cascadeur assist/author AutoPosing, AutoPhysics keyframing free tier; Pro ~$396/yr

    (Caps and prices verified August 2026; this table is the rotting kind of knowledge — expect drift within a year.)

    What this opens¶

    Two doors, both of which later chapters walk through. Capture turns footage into corpus — the raw material for learned movement spaces and for the unsupervised gesture-discovery program (chapter 10's research sweep, coming) starts as exactly these skeletons. And capture turns performance into control signal: a cleaned 3D take, re-rendered from any angle through the pose-conditioned video pipeline, is the bridge between a real dancer's phrasing and a synthetic dancer's body — the subject of the rendering chapter ahead.
