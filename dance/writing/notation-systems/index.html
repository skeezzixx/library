    Notation systems¶

    Music has had a durable written form for a thousand years; dance has had a dozen, none of them universal, and most dancers alive cannot read any. That asymmetry is the first fact about dance notation and the most revealing one: movement resists being written down not because nobody tried but because what to write is genuinely unclear. Every notation system that has survived more than a decade is, at bottom, a theory of what the unit of movement is — the named step, the direction-and-duration of a body part, the body's position at an instant, the rotation of a limb about a joint — and the systems disagree. That makes this chapter a companion to the gamut chapter and the machine-discovery chapter, which ask the same question from the maker's and the machine's side. Here it is asked from the archivist's.

    The problem¶

    Dance is the ephemeral art: it exists in performance, and until film it left nothing but memory, prose, and pictures. The consequences were severe. Repertoire died with its dancers; revivals were reconstructions from the bodies of those who had learned them; authorship could not be proven and so, for most of history, could not be owned. A notation promised three things — preservation (the work survives its makers), transmission (a stranger can learn it), and analysis (the work can be studied on paper, compared, decomposed) — and each system below is stronger at one of these than the others.

    Feuillet: the path and the step¶

    The first real system is Chorégraphie, ou l'art de décrire la danse (Raoul-Auger Feuillet, 1700), recording the system developed under Pierre Beauchamp at Louis XIV's Académie Royale de Danse — which is why it is called Beauchamp-Feuillet. Its page is a map: a line traces the dancer's path across the floor, and along the path sit symbols for the steps — plié, élevé, sauté, cabriole, tombé, glissé, tourné — with small marks for the foot's position and bar lines tying the track to the music printed above. It is elegant and reads almost at sight once learned; hundreds of baroque dances survive in it, and reconstructions of them are the basis of everything we know about how the court danced.1

    What it encodes is the floor pattern and the named step; what it drops is almost everything else — the torso, the arms (minimally marked), the face, the quality. It could do that because its unit was the codified step of a closed vocabulary: the reader already knew what a cabriole was, so the page needed only to say which and where. Feuillet notation is the gamut chapter's lesson in 1700: a vocabulary-level notation is only as good as the shared vocabulary, and when the vocabulary changed — Romantic ballet's new technique, the rise of the torso and arms — the system became unreadable within two generations.

    The nineteenth century tried again repeatedly. Arthur Saint-Léon's La Sténochorégraphie (1852) and Friedrich Zorn's Grammatik der Tanzkunst (1887) used stick figures under the music; Vladimir Stepanov's Alphabet des mouvements du corps humain (1892) wrote movement on a musical staff, anatomically, by body part — and it is the one that mattered historically, because the Mariinsky used it to record the Petipa repertory. Those scores, taken to the West by Nikolai Sergeyev, gave the Royal Ballet its Sleeping Beauty (1939) and gave Alexei Ratmansky the basis for his reconstructions (Paquita, 2014; Sleeping Beauty, 2015) — a rare case of notation doing exactly the preservation job it was invented for, a century late.

    Labanotation: the direction, the level, the duration¶

    Rudolf Laban published Schrifttanz in 1928, and the system — Kinetography Laban in Europe, Labanotation in America, developed by Albrecht Knust, Ann Hutchinson Guest, and the Dance Notation Bureau (founded 1940) — is the most complete movement notation that exists.2 Its logic:

    • A vertical staff, read bottom to top, so that time flows upward and the page's left and right are the body's left and right.
    • A center line dividing the staff: the innermost columns carry support (what the weight is on), then leg gestures, body, arms, head, outward.
    • Each movement is a symbol whose shape gives direction (nine basic directions), whose shading gives level (low, middle, high), whose length gives duration, and whose column gives the body part. Pins, hooks, and bows refine — contact, rotation, touch, the relation of one part to another.

    The unit, then, is a body part moving in a direction, at a level, for a duration — finer than the step, continuous rather than positional, and independent of any particular vocabulary. That universality is the system's strength and its cost. It can record a Graham contraction and a Balinese agem with equal precision; a full score of an evening work takes hundreds of hours and a trained notator; few dancers read it fluently; and the Effort side of Laban's thinking — how the movement is done — needs a separate notation altogether (the effort chapter). Labanotation is the analysis system par excellence, which is why scholars use it and companies mostly don't.

    Two things made it matter beyond the archive. It made choreography copyrightable: Hanya Holm's dances for Kiss Me, Kate (1952) were the first registered in Labanotation, and the US Copyright Act of 1976 named "choreographic works" explicitly — a fixed, written form being what the law needed. And its simplified cousin, Motif Notation (developed by Hutchinson Guest and Valerie Preston-Dunlop), records only the essential idea of a movement — a turn, a rise, a travel — leaving the rest to the performer. Motif notation is a gamut-level score: it writes the units and the operators and not the realization. The chance chapter's deliberately incomplete scores and the gamut chapter's unit layer have a notation already; this is it.

    Benesh: the position at the moment¶

    Benesh Movement Notation (Rudolf and Joan Benesh, 1955) is the dancer's notation. Its staff is five lines like music's — top of head, shoulders, waist, knees, floor — read left to right in time with the score written above, and the notator marks where the hands and feet are, as seen from behind, at each significant instant, with small signs for bending, contact, and the direction of travel. The Royal Ballet and much of the ballet world use it; its practitioners are choreologists, and a company choreologist can notate at rehearsal speed.3

    Its unit is the position at a moment — a frame. Benesh records the key instants and trusts the trained body to move between them, which is why it is fast to write and fast to read and why it is weak exactly where Laban is strong: dynamics, the path between positions, anything continuous. Readers of Part VII will recognize the architecture immediately: Benesh is keyframes and interpolation, sixty years before the pipeline — the choreologist draws the keyframes, and the dancer is the interpolator. Its assumptions (a trained ballet body, a known vocabulary that fills the gaps) are the assumptions of every keyframe system, and its failure modes are the same: where the in-between is the content, the notation is silent.

    Eshkol-Wachman: the limb as a rotating axis¶

    Eshkol-Wachman Movement Notation (Noa Eshkol and Avraham Wachman, 1958) is the mathematician's system. The body is a set of limbs, each a line segment rotating about the joint at its base; every movement is a rotation of that segment, written as numbers on a spherical coordinate grid — horizontal and vertical angles, amounts, and the kind of rotation (plane, conical, about the axis). The page is a table: rows for limbs, columns for time. It is entirely vocabulary-free and species-free — it has been used to notate animal behavior, sign language, and autism diagnostics as readily as dance — and it is the closest any notation comes to a joint-angle skeleton file. The capture chapter's BVH and SMPL representations are Eshkol-Wachman with a sensor instead of a notator. Its cost is legibility: nobody reads a movement off an EW score the way a pianist reads a chord; one computes it.4

    Four answers to one question¶

    Set the systems side by side and they are four theories of the unit:

    System Unit Strong at Silent on
    Feuillet (1700) the named step, on a floor path baroque repertory; floor pattern torso, quality, anything outside the vocabulary
    Labanotation (1928) body part × direction × level × duration analysis; any vocabulary; copyright speed of writing; quality (needs Effort)
    Benesh (1955) the position at a key moment ballet rehearsal; speed; music alignment the in-between; dynamics
    Eshkol-Wachman (1958) the limb's rotation about its joint precision; cross-species; computation human legibility
    Motif (1960s–) the essential idea (unit + operator) scores that leave realization open the realization itself

    The table is the subject's map in miniature. Feuillet and Motif are gamut-level: they write vocabulary and leave the body to fill it in. Laban is description-level: it writes what the body does, finely. Benesh is keyframe-level. Eshkol-Wachman is skeleton-level. Every pipeline in Part VII passes through these same four levels — vocabulary, motion description, keyframes, joint angles — and the notators found each of them by hand, a generation before anyone asked a computer.

    Why notation never beat video¶

    It is usually said that video killed notation, and the timing fits: portable video arrived in the 1970s, and within a decade the rehearsal tape had replaced the notator as a company's working memory (the craft chapter describes the habit). But the deeper reasons are structural and worth stating, because they are the same reasons a recording never replaced a score in music:

    • Cost. Notating is slow and expert; filming is instant and free. Preservation chose the cheap option.
    • Legibility. Few dancers read any system; all dancers can watch.
    • Completeness versus structure. Video captures appearance — everything, from one viewpoint, flattened, with no indication of what matters. Notation captures structure — what the dancer intends, at the level the notator chose, with everything else discarded. A video of Giselle tells you what one cast did; a score tells you what Giselle is. The Stepanov reconstructions were possible because the score had decided what the work was.
    • Transmission. Learning a role from video means copying a performer; learning it from a score means learning the work. Companies with a living tradition (the bodies that know it) need neither; companies reviving a dead one need the score.

    The settled answer is that they do different jobs, and the next chapter is about the thirty-year attempt to build something that does both — a digital score that is video and structure. It ends where this chapter began: with the question of the unit, now asked of an algorithm.
