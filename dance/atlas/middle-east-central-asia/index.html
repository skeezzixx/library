    The Middle East and Central Asia¶

    Part I · The territory › The world atlas of dance — page 4 of 9 · ← Africa and the diaspora · European folk and social dance →

    Atlas page — the world atlas of dance.

    A region where dance is everywhere and concert dance almost nowhere: the dominant functions are social (the line and circle dances of every wedding from the Levant to the Caucasus) and ritual (the Sufi orders), with solo improvised dance at the center of the social forms and a twentieth-century stage tradition — Egyptian raqs sharqi, Iranian and Soviet "national" ballets — built on top. It is also a region where the music's theory is deep and the dance's theory thin, so the commons' maqam note does unusual work here.

    The music the dancer inhabits¶

    Middle Eastern art music is maqam music, and the commons describes its grammar precisely. The atom is the jins — a three-to-five-note module — not the scale; a maqam chains two or three ajnas at a pivot (ghammaz), and its identity is its sayr, the conventional melodic course: which jins opens, the order the others enter, where modulation is licensed, where the piece resolves. Two maqamat can share a scale and differ entirely in sayr. Time splits by genre: a taqsim (solo instrumental improvisation) or layali/mawwal (vocal) is suspended — no enforced pulse, phrasing by breath — while a metered piece runs against an iqa', a cyclic dum/tak pattern (wahda's plain 4/4, maqsum, sama'i thaqil's 10/8 grouped 3+2+2+3), with cadences aligned to the cycle. The intervals include neutral seconds and thirds no twelve-tone stand-in reproduces.1 For the dancer this produces two distinct modes of moving: to the iqa' — the hips and feet marking a cycle whose accent pattern (dum low, tak high) is the choreography's clock — and to the taqsim — the slow, unmetered section of a raqs sharqi solo in which the dancer follows the melodic course with sustained, continuous movement, phrasing on the singer's or the oud's breath. A raqs sharqi solo is built from exactly this alternation, and the suspended-then-cyclic shape the choreomusicology chapter describes is its form.

    The Arab world and the Levant¶

    Raqs sharqi ("oriental dance," belly dance in the West's term) — the solo improvised dance of Egypt and the Levant, rooted in social dancing, developed as a stage and nightclub art in Cairo from the 1920s (Badia Masabni's casino, the film stars Samia Gamal and Tahia Carioca, the later Reda Troupe's folkloric stagings) and globalized from there. Signature: polycentric isolations — hip drops, shimmies, undulations, chest and shoulder articulation — over a grounded base, arms framing, the veil and finger cymbals (sagat) as props. Folk cousins: baladi (the urban-folk ancestor), Saidi (Upper Egypt, with the cane — raqs al-assaya, from the men's stick-fighting tahtib), the Persian Gulf's khaleegy hair-tossing dance, Nubian dances, the Moroccan shikhat. Function: social at its root (women dancing for women at weddings), presentational on stage — and contested throughout the region for exactly that migration.

    Dabke — the line dance of the Levant (Lebanon, Syria, Palestine, Jordan): linked dancers led by a lawweeh twirling a handkerchief or beads, stamping in synchronized steps to the mijwiz or tabl; the wedding dance of the entire region and a diaspora identity marker. Arab Gulf ardah (the sword line dance), Yemeni bara', Iraqi choubi.

    The Sufi orders¶

    The Mevlevi sema (Turkey; Rumi's order, thirteenth century) — the whirling of the dervishes: white skirts, tall felt hats, the right palm up and left down, turning counter-clockwise on the left foot for long minutes in a ceremony of dhikr (remembrance). It is ritual, not performance, in its own terms — the first chapter's efficacy function — though it is staged for tourists and has been a source for contemporary choreographers (Cherkaoui, Ziya Azazi). Other orders have their own movement — the Moroccan hadra, the Egyptian tanoura (a colored-skirt spectacle form of the whirl), the zar trance rituals of Egypt, Sudan, and the Gulf.

    Iran and Persian dance¶

    Iran's classical dance — the refined, courtly solo of the Qajar and Pahlavi courts, with its soft wrists, upper-body focus, and facial expressiveness — was staged as a national ballet under the Pahlavis and driven underground after 1979, surviving in the diaspora (Los Angeles above all). Regional forms are more robust: the Kurdish halparke (line dances), Azerbaijani dances (shared with the Caucasus), Baluchi, Gilaki, Bandari (the Gulf coast, African-influenced), and the Khorasani stick and bakhtiari dances.

    Central Asia and the Caucasus¶

    Uzbek and Tajik dance — the courtly, lyrical solo traditions (the Bukhara, Khorezm, and Ferghana styles) of wrists, arms, and turning, staged and systematized in the Soviet period. Caucasus — Georgian dance (kartuli, khorumi, mtiuluri): the men's astonishing toe-dancing in soft boots, the women's gliding as if on wheels, the national ensembles (Sukhishvili, 1945) that made it a world stage form; Armenian kochari and shalakho; Azerbaijani yalli; Chechen and Dagestani lezginka — the men's leaping, knife-edge dance of the whole North Caucasus. Turkey — beyond the sema, the folk families (horon from the Black Sea, zeybek from the Aegean, halay the line dance of Anatolia). Afghanistan — the attan, the circle dance of the Pashtuns. The Soviet legacy across this whole zone is a distinctive thing: each "nation" given a staged folkloric ballet, a national ensemble, and a conservatory — which preserved and standardized the dances in the same stroke.

    What the region teaches the core¶

    That suspended time is a mode of dancing — the taqsim-following solo is continuous movement without a clock, phrased on melody, the clearest living case of the breath mode the working-with-music chapter describes; that the solo improviser inside a grammar (sayr for the musician, the raqs sharqi vocabulary for the dancer) is a complete model of frame-and-realization; and that social line dance is the region's — and perhaps the world's — default form, the participatory function unbroken.
