    Africa and the diaspora¶

    Part I · The territory › The world atlas of dance — page 3 of 9 · ← East and Southeast Asia · The Middle East and Central Asia →

    Atlas page — the world atlas of dance.

    Africa is a continent of several thousand dance traditions, and any page is a sampling; what follows picks the families that the rest of the world's dance most descends from — because the Afro-Atlantic diaspora carried West and Central African movement to the Caribbean, Brazil, and the United States, and from there into nearly every popular dance of the last century: the Latin American forms, tap and the jazz dances, the street styles, and by way of them the concert stage. Two structural features recur across the continent's traditions and are worth naming before the survey: polycentric movement (the body's parts — hips, chest, shoulders, head — articulated independently and often in different rhythms, against the European tradition's single upright center) and a call-and-response relationship with music in which the drummer leads the dancer and the dancer answers the drum.

    The music the dancer answers¶

    The commons describes West African ensemble drumming — Ewe, Akan, Mande — in terms that explain the dancing exactly. The ensemble organizes not around a downbeat but around a timeline: a bell pattern (gankogui), most often the seven-stroke pattern over twelve pulses, played as an invariant ostinato the whole ensemble entrains to — held, the commons notes, divisively, as a fixed asymmetric reference, not as a counted sum of cells, despite Western habits of writing it "additively." Supporting drums interlock in hocket, each occupying the others' gaps, and the master drummer shapes the form live — section changes, entrances, breaks, and dance cues — through improvised signal phrases, so that the ensemble's "chart" is a recognized vocabulary of cues rather than a score.1 The dance is built on that architecture: the dancers hold the timeline in the body (often the feet in a duple grouping against the bell's ternary — the cross-rhythm that is the music's identity, performed), the master drum's signal phrase is the cue to change step, and the unstated beats the ensemble leaves open are — as the ethnography of Ewe dance puts it — space for the dancers. It is the working-with-music chapter's cue-based practice taken to its limit: a live control channel from drummer to dancer, with form generated in performance.

    West Africa¶

    • Ewe dances (Ghana, Togo) — agbekor (a war dance turned social, the canonical teaching repertoire), gahu, kpanlogo (an urban social dance of the 1960s): the drum-ensemble dances above, taught worldwide through the Ghanaian university dance programs and the ethnomusicology literature.
    • Sabar (Senegal, Wolof) — the explosive, high-kicking, leaping women's dance to the sabar drum ensemble at street celebrations, where the dancer enters the circle one at a time to challenge the drummer — participatory and competitive at once; also Senegal's mbalax pop dance.
    • The Mande djembe dances (Guinea, Mali) — dununba, soli, kuku, and the large repertoire carried abroad by the national ballets (Les Ballets Africains, 1952) and by djembe teaching; Germaine Acogny's École des Sables in Senegal built a contemporary technique on this base (the modern revolt).
    • Yoruba and Fon dance (Nigeria, Benin) — the bata drum dances and the orisha dances each deity's characteristic movement, which crossed the Atlantic intact.
    • Hausa, Igbo, Asante (adowa, the Asante funeral and social dance of subtle hand gestures) and Nigeria's popular-dance inventions (azonto from Ghana, shaku shaku, zanku, the Afrobeats dances) complete the region's living edge.

    Central, Southern, and East Africa¶

    • Congolese ndombolo and the soukous dances — hip-driven, the popular-music dance of Central Africa.
    • Gumboot (South Africa) — the stamping, boot-slapping dance invented by mine workers forbidden to speak, a percussion vocabulary in rubber boots; pantsula — the fast, flat- footed township street dance; Zulu indlamu (the high-kicking warrior dance), Xhosa umxhentso; and the kwaito and amapiano dances that now travel the same routes as the Afrobeats ones.
    • East Africa — Ethiopian eskista (the shoulder-shaking dance, a polycentric signature), the Maasai jumping adumu, Rwandan intore, Kenyan and Tanzanian ngoma.
    • North Africa belongs to the Middle East page as much as here: Egyptian tahtib, Moroccan ahwash, Tuareg dance.

    The Afro-Atlantic: the orisha dances and after¶

    Enslaved Africans carried the Yoruba, Fon, and Kongo religious systems to Cuba (Santería, lucumí), Haiti (Vodou), and Brazil (Candomblé), and with them the dances of the deities: each orisha — Shango, Oshun, Yemaya, Ogun, Eleggua — with a rhythm, a color, a movement signature (Oshun's flowing river hands, Shango's thunder-axe), performed to the bata drums in ceremonies where possession is the goal. These are the first chapter's ritual function in its most vivid surviving form, and they are also the root system of the secular dances around them: Afro-Cuban rumba (guaguancó, yambú, columbia) and from it the son–mambo–salsa lineage (Latin America); Brazilian samba from Bahian samba de roda, capoeira (the Angolan-rooted martial art-dance-game in its roda), maculelê; Haitian yanvalou and rara; Trinidadian and Jamaican forms; and in the United States the ring shout, the juba, the cakewalk, and everything that became tap and the jazz dances (North American vernacular). Katherine Dunham and Pearl Primus (the modern revolt) brought this lineage onto the American concert stage as scholarship and as technique; the contemporary African choreographers — Acogny, Linyekula, Maqoma, Masilo, Chipaumire — are the return journey.

    What the region teaches the core¶

    That form can be generated live by a cue channel — the master drummer's signal phrases are the clearest example anywhere of the gamut+rules architecture's sequencer being a person issuing tokens the ensemble obeys; that polycentric, polyrhythmic bodies are the normal case, and the single-centered body a regional habit; and that the timeline — a fixed asymmetric reference with no privileged beat — is a third way of addressing time, alongside the hierarchical meter and the breath, that a movement generator modeling "the beat" as a downbeat will get wrong.

    Field notes

    Renders theory-genre-west-african (the invariant bell timeline; interlocking hocket; the master drummer's signal phrases as the live form-control channel; cross-rhythm as identity; the divisive-not-additive caution). The dance descriptions are confident-knowledge writing.
