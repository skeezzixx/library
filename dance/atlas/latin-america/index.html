    Latin America¶

    Part I · The territory › The world atlas of dance — page 6 of 9 · ← European folk and social dance · Oceania and the Pacific →

    Atlas page — the world atlas of dance.

    Latin America's dances are the Afro-Atlantic diaspora's most elaborate flowering — West and Central African polycentrism and call-and-response, European couple dance and court form, Indigenous ceremony, all recombined in the Caribbean, Brazil, and the Río de la Plata — and they are, by a wide margin, the world's most exported social dances: the waltz aside, no European form travels like salsa, tango, or samba. Most run the first chapter's social function at home and a presentational one abroad, with competitive ballroom versions of several (ballroom).

    The music every part answers to: clave¶

    The commons describes Afro-Cuban music's organizing principle in one sentence, and it governs the dancing of half the continent. Clave — son or rumba clave, five strokes split into a three-side and a two-side, played 3-2 or 2-3 — is a timeline: a fixed asymmetric reference the whole ensemble entrains to, with an internal direction, so that a piece is in 3-2 or 2-3 and every other part's accents are derived from which side of the cell they fall on; the bass tumbao's anticipations, the piano guajeo, the conga pattern are clave-aligned ostinati; the montuno (the short vamp over which coro-pregón call-and-response and solos layer) is a one-clave-cycle cell, and direction changes only at section boundaries, never by drift.1 The dances answer it precisely — and argue about it. Salsa's "on 1" versus "on 2" debate (the break step on the first or second beat; New York's "on 2" mambo timing) is a disagreement about which event of the clave cell the body weights, not about which beat of a measure; the rumba dancer's hips mark the clave's strokes; the son's contratiempo (the step on the off-beat) is clave's anticipation in the feet. A dancer who is "off clave" is wrong in the way a musician is, and everyone on the floor can hear it.

    Cuba and the Caribbean¶

    • Rumba (Cuba: guaguancó, the courtship game of the vacunao; yambú, slower; columbia, the fast male solo) — the drum-and-voice root form; the orisha dances of Santería (Africa and the diaspora) beside it.
    • Son → mambo → salsa — the son (Oriente, early twentieth century; the casino partner form), the mambo of 1940s Havana and the Palladium in New York (Pérez Prado, Tito Puente — the dance a fast, open, showy partner form), and salsa, the 1970s New York label for the whole family (Fania Records), now danced worldwide in regional styles: Cuban casino and the circle form rueda de casino, Puerto Rican, New York "on 2," LA "on 1," Cali's hyper-fast footwork (Colombia's claim to the world's salsa capital).
    • Cha-cha-chá (1950s, Enrique Jorrín), danzón (the nineteenth-century Cuban ballroom dance, Mexico's too), bolero, mambo; the Dominican Republic's merengue (the two-step hip-sway to tambora and accordion) and bachata (the 1960s bar music, the 2000s global sensual partner dance); Puerto Rico's bomba (drummer follows dancer — the African cue relationship reversed) and plena; Haiti's konpa; Trinidad's soca, limbo, and the Carnival bands; Jamaica's dancehall with its named moves and their inventors, and reggae's skank.

    Brazil¶

    Samba — from Bahia's Afro-Brazilian samba de roda (UNESCO-listed) to Rio's samba no pé, the solo fast-footed Carnival dance of the escolas de samba and their passistas; the partner forms samba de gafieira (the ballroom) and samba-rock; and the ballroom's very different "samba" (ballroom). Capoeira — the Angolan-rooted martial art-dance-game played in a roda to the berimbau: ginga (the swaying base step), kicks, escapes, cartwheels, and acrobatics in a conversation between two players — Angola (slow, low, ritualized) and Regional (Mestre Bimba's 1930s systematization, faster, more athletic); now a worldwide practice and a direct feeder of breaking's power moves and of contemporary dance's floor vocabulary. Forró (the Northeast's accordion couple dance), frevo (Recife's acrobatic Carnival dance with the small umbrella), maracatu, bumba-meu-boi, axé, funk carioca's passinho, and the contemporary companies — Grupo Corpo, Deborah Colker (the modern revolt).

    The Río de la Plata: tango¶

    Tango — Buenos Aires and Montevideo, the 1880s–1900s, the immigrant ports' fusion of habanera, milonga, candombe, and European couple dance: the close embrace, the walk (caminata), ochos, ganchos, boleos, sacadas, the leader's improvised lead and the follower's reading of it — a fully improvised partner dance with a large vocabulary, danced socially at the milonga under its codes (the cabeceo invitation, the tanda of three or four songs, the cortina). Its stage form — tango escenario, the lifts and kicks of Tango Argentino (1983) and Forever Tango — and its ballroom form are different animals; tango nuevo (from the 1990s; Gustavo Naveira, Chicho Frúmboli) re-analyzed the dance's mechanics and opened the embrace. The dressing-the-genre chapter reads its costume; the music (Gardel, Troilo, Pugliese, D'Arienzo, Piazzolla's nuevo tango) is itself a complete art. Milonga (the dance, faster, older) and vals complete the milonga's trio. Argentina's folklórico — chacarera, zamba, gato, the gaucho's stamping malambo (now a competitive male solo with boleadoras) — is the other tradition.

    Mexico, Central America, the Andes¶

    Mexican folklórico — the staged regional repertoire (Amalia Hernández's Ballet Folklórico de México, 1952): the jarabe tapatío (the "Mexican hat dance"), Veracruz's son jarocho and its zapateado on the tarima, Jalisco's skirt-swirling, the danza de los viejitos, the Yucatán's jarana; the pre-Hispanic-rooted concheros and the danza de los voladores (the Totonac flying-pole ritual); and quebradita, banda, cumbia sonidera as living popular forms. Cumbia — Colombia's Caribbean coast (African drum, Indigenous flute, Spanish dress), the continent's most-spread rhythm, with a national style in every country from Mexico to Argentina. The Andes — Peru's marinera (the elegant handkerchief courtship dance) and huayno, the danza de tijeras (scissors dance); Bolivia's diablada, morenada, caporales at the Oruro Carnival; Chile's cueca; Ecuador's sanjuanito. Central America — Guatemala's baile de la conquista, Panama's tamborito, Garifuna punta.

    What the region teaches the core¶

    That a single global state variable — clave direction — can govern every part's accents, the dancer's included, which is the most concrete possible version of the "shared frame" this subject keeps finding; that fully improvised partner dance (tango, salsa) is a two-body generative system with a lead/follow protocol the gamut+rules architecture has no equivalent for yet; and that a social dance's stage and ballroom versions are different dances wearing the same name — a warning for any corpus labelled "tango."
