    East and Southeast Asia¶

    Atlas page — the world atlas of dance.

    The region's great traditions are theatre-dance: forms in which dance, drama, music, mask, and costume are one art, usually with court or temple origins, a codified movement vocabulary, and a theory of the performer's inner state as precise as any in the West. The Western habit of separating "dance" from "theatre" fits them badly, and they repay being read as whole systems.

    Japan¶

    Noh (fourteenth century, Kan'ami and Zeami) — masked, slow, austere music-drama, with a movement vocabulary of kata (fixed forms: the sliding walk suriashi, the stamp, the kamae stance, the small gestures of the fan) so reduced that a single step carries the weight a ballet phrase would. Its mask is the makeup chapter's great mechanism — expression as angle to the light — and Zeami's treatises on hana (the flower, the performer's quality) are a theory of stage presence. Kyōgen, the comic interludes. Kabuki (seventeenth century) is the popular counterpart — kumadori painted faces, the mie held pose, the hanamichi runway — and nihon-buyō, classical Japanese dance, grows out of kabuki's dance scenes into a concert form taught in iemoto schools. Bon odori, the summer festival circle dances, are the participatory stratum; kagura, Shinto shrine dance, the ritual one; butoh (1959–), the modern revolt, is on the shelf. Door: reduction — the technique chapter's lesson that a small vocabulary, deeply done, reads as a world.

    China¶

    Beijing opera (jingju, eighteenth–nineteenth century) organizes performance into four skills — song, speech, acting-dance, and combat — and a role system (sheng, dan, jing, chou) each with its movement conventions: the dan's tiny steps and water sleeves (shuixiu, a metre of white silk extending every gesture — the props chapter's case), the warrior's acrobatics, the painted-face lianpu color code (makeup). Chinese classical dance as a concert form is a twentieth-century construction from opera movement, martial arts, and ballet training; folk and ethnic dance (the ribbon dance, the lion and dragon dances, Tibetan guozhuang, Uyghur sanam, Dai peacock dance) is a vast staged repertoire; and Yang Liping, Tao Ye's numbered works, and Shen Wei are its contemporary edge.

    Korea¶

    Court dance (jeongjae, from the Joseon dynasty) — slow, symmetrical, ceremonial, in layered silk; folk and shamanic dance — salpuri (a solo of suppressed grief with a long white scarf), seungmu (the monk's dance), talchum (masked dance-drama, satirical), buchaechum (the fan dance, a twentieth-century stage creation), and the farmers' percussion dance pungmul with its streamer-hatted drummers. Korea's contemporary scene is large, and K-pop's choreography — precise, unison, camera-built — is the country's most exported movement.

    Indonesia: Bali and Java¶

    Balinese dance — legong (young girls, fast, intricate, eyes and fingers flickering), baris (the male warrior dance), topeng (masked), barong and the trance sanghyang, and kecak (the "monkey chant," a seated male chorus of interlocking cak syllables, staged in its modern form in the 1930s with Walter Spies) — is the region's most kinetic: angular, darting, with the agem base stance and a vocabulary of named positions. Javanese court dance (Yogyakarta and Surakarta bedhaya and srimpi) is its opposite — slow, refined, inward, the dancers nearly floating. Both are inseparable from gamelan, and the commons describes gamelan in terms that explain the dancing: a piece is organized around the gongan, one full gong cycle, punctuated by strictly nested colotomic instruments — the great gong at cycle's end, kenong dividing it, kempul and kethuk subdividing — each a binary division of the level above; a skeletal core melody (balungan) that every elaborating part derives from; and irama, the ensemble-wide density level that shifts in discrete steps at structural points and moves in one direction across a piece.1 The dance rides this exactly: the dancer's phrases end on the gong, the choreography's sections change where irama changes, and Balinese kotekan — interlocking parts each filling the other's gaps at two-to-four times the core rate — is audible in legong's flickering hands. Gamelan is the choreomusicology chapter's strict-hierarchy case in its purest form: hierarchy level is nearly computable from a beat counter, and the dancing knows it.

    Mainland Southeast Asia¶

    Thai khon — the masked dance-drama of the Ramakien (the Thai Ramayana), with its curved-finger, bent-knee vocabulary, the mae bot basic postures, and the demon and monkey roles; lakhon its unmasked cousin; fawn the northern folk forms. Khmer classical dance (Cambodia) — the royal ballet, robam, nearly destroyed under the Khmer Rouge (an estimated ninety percent of its artists died) and rebuilt from the survivors; the hyper-flexed fingers and hands, the slow spiral, the apsara dance; UNESCO-listed. Lao and Burmese court and festival dances complete the family; Vietnamese water puppetry (múa rối nước), in which wooden figures dance on a pond, is the region's most unusual "dancer."

    What the region teaches the core¶

    Three things. That strict hierarchy in the music produces strict hierarchy in the dance — gamelan's nested cycles are the model for a choreography whose section boundaries are computable. That a mask or a painted face can be a vocabulary as much as a movement can. And that reduction is a technique — noh's handful of kata and the Javanese court's near-stillness are the limit of the "fewer units, more deeply" principle the gamut chapter keeps arriving at.
