    The world atlas of dance¶

    Part I · The territory › The world atlas of dance — overview · ← What dance is; what choreography is · South Asia →

    This is the library's breadth layer for dance, built on the tiering rule: everything anyone calls dance gets at least a mention; major traditions far from the core get a real description; the core — Western concert dance — gets full depth in the rest of the subject. The atlas groups by region and lineage, because that is how traditions are actually transmitted, and then indexes by function — ritual, social, concert, competitive, the first chapter's lens — because function is what a dance is for, and two dances from opposite ends of the earth with the same function have more in common than two neighbors with different ones.

    Each tradition page describes its dances by the same five questions: what it is and where; the movement signature (what you would recognize in a silhouette); the music relationship (which the commons often holds in detail from the music side); the function(s) it serves; and the door — the core chapter it best illuminates. Pages cross-link into the core wherever a tradition teaches something the core needs: the mudra systems into codified vocabularies, flamenco's compás into the choreomusicology chapters, the cypher into street styles.

    The tradition families¶

    • South Asia — the eight classical forms (bharatanatyam, kathak, odissi, kathakali, kuchipudi, manipuri, mohiniyattam, sattriya), the folk and martial forms, and Bollywood; the tala/raga grammar the dancer's feet recite.
    • East and Southeast Asia — noh and nihon-buyō, Beijing opera's movement, Korean court and folk dance, Balinese legong and kecak, Javanese court dance, Thai khon, Khmer classical; gamelan's nested cycles.
    • Africa and the diaspora — West African ensemble dance (Ewe agbekor, Wolof sabar, Mande djembe dances), Southern African gumboot and pantsula, Ethiopian eskista, the orisha dances of the Afro-Atlantic; the bell timeline and the master drummer's cues.
    • The Middle East and Central Asia — raqs sharqi, dabke, the Sufi sema, Persian classical and folk dance, Central Asian and Caucasian forms; maqam and iqa'.
    • European folk and social dance — flamenco, Irish step and sean-nós, Scottish Highland, Balkan chain dances and aksak, Scandinavian polska, the waltz–polka lineage, Morris and sword dance, the court-to-ballroom line.
    • Latin America — tango, samba, the son–mambo–salsa lineage, rumba, capoeira, folklórico, cumbia, merengue, bachata, the Andean forms; clave.
    • Oceania and the Pacific — hula, haka, Samoan siva and sasa, Tongan lakalaka, Cook Islands ura, Aboriginal Australian and Torres Strait Islander ceremony.
    • North American vernacular — powwow styles, Appalachian clogging and flatfooting, square and contra dance, tap's roots and the Lindy Hop, the jazz dances, cajun and zydeco; the street styles get their own chapter.
    • Ballroom — the Standard five, the Latin five, American Smooth and Rhythm, and the competitive world that shaped the costume, the technique, and the judging.

    The function index¶

    The same traditions, sorted by what they are primarily for. Most run more than one function; this is the dominant one, and the arrows mark the migrations the first chapter describes.

    Function Traditions (dominant use)
    Ritual Sufi sema; orisha dances (Yoruba, Vodou, Santería, Candomblé); Balinese sanghyang and temple legong; kecak; Kandyan and bharatanatyam's temple ancestors; powwow ceremony; haka as challenge and welcome; Aboriginal ceremony; kathakali's temple theatre; noh's shrine origins; Ethiopian church dance
    Social Balkan chain dances; dabke; the waltz–polka family; square/contra; céilí; tango's milonga; salsa, cumbia, merengue, bachata; garba/raas; bhangra; sabar at celebrations; gumboot; the Lindy Hop; kizomba; the house-music floor
    Concert the South Asian classical forms (→ from temple and court); Javanese and Khmer court dance; khon; noh; Beijing opera; flamenco's tablao and ballet flamenco (→ from juerga); Western ballet and modern (the core); Cloud Gate and the contemporary companies everywhere
    Competitive DanceSport ballroom; Irish step (feis); Highland; powwow contest categories; tap, jazz and "contemporary" competitions; breaking's battles (→ Olympic 2024); krump sessions; voguing's balls; Trinidad's Carnival bands; capoeira's roda (game, not contest, but judged by peers)

    First tier: the rest of the world, at least by name¶

    A growing list, in the spirit of "everything anyone calls dance gets a line." Each entry is a name, a place, and one clause — enough to know it exists and to search for more. Additions welcome; this list is meant to lengthen.

    • Asia: Bhutanese cham (masked monastic dance); Tibetan cham; Mongolian biyelgee (upper-body, seated); Uzbek and Tajik classical dance; Odissi's cousin Chhau (masked martial, three styles); Sri Lankan Kandyan and low-country dance; Burmese zat pwe; Vietnamese múa rối nước (water puppetry — the puppets dance); Filipino tinikling and singkil (bamboo poles) and pangalay; Malay zapin and joget; Okinawan eisa; Japanese bon odori (festival circle dance), kagura, kabuki dance, butoh (the shelf); Korean salpuri, talchum (mask dance), buchaechum (fan dance); Chinese ribbon, lion and dragon dance, the ethnic-minority stage forms, Tibetan guozhuang; Hmong dance.
    • Africa: Egyptian tahtib (stick); Moroccan ahwash and guedra; Tuareg dances; Hausa and Yoruba bata; Ghanaian adowa, kpanlogo, azonto; Congolese ndombolo and soukous dance; Rwandan intore; Maasai jumping dance (adumu); Zulu indlamu; Xhosa umxhentso; Madagascar's hira gasy; South African kwaito and amapiano dances; Angolan kuduro and kizomba.
    • Europe: Basque aurresku; Galician muiñeira; Portuguese fado has no dance but vira does; Italian tarantella and pizzica; Greek syrtaki (1964, for a film), zeibekiko (solo, male), pentozali; Hungarian csárdás and verbunkos; Polish mazurka and polonaise (the latter an Olympic-opening staple); Czech polka (the original); Russian khorovod and the Cossack squat-kicks (prisyadka); Ukrainian hopak; Georgian kartuli and khorumi (the men on their toes in soft boots); Armenian kochari; Sardinian ballu tundu; Breton fest-noz dances; English Morris, rapper and longsword, maypole; the Faroese chain dance (the oldest surviving European chain dance, to ballads).
    • The Americas: Mexican danza de los voladores (the flying pole), concheros, jarabe tapatío; Guatemalan baile de la conquista; Peruvian marinera and huayno, the scissors dance; Bolivian diablada and caporales; Chilean cueca; Argentine malambo (the gaucho stamping dance) and chacarera; Brazilian frevo, maracatu, forró, bumba-meu-boi, axé; Colombian mapalé and champeta; Dominican palos; Haitian yanvalou and rara; Trinidadian limbo and soca; Jamaican dancehall's named moves; Cuban danzón and casino rueda; Cajun two-step; Métis jigging; Inuit drum dance; Mexican-American quebradita.
    • Oceania: Papua New Guinean sing-sing; Fijian meke; Tahitian ʻōteʻa (the fast hip dance often mistaken for hula); Marquesan haka manu; Kiribati dance; Rapa Nui dance; Torres Strait Islander dance.

    The atlas is never finished; the list above is the part of this library most explicitly designed to grow.
