    European folk and social dance¶

    Part I · The territory › The world atlas of dance — page 5 of 9 · ← The Middle East and Central Asia · Latin America →

    Atlas page — the world atlas of dance.

    Europe's folk and social dances are the substrate under its concert tradition — the court dances ballet codified were village dances first, and the ballroom's waltz and polka were peasant turning dances that scandalized the cities — and they remain, across the continent, the participatory function in full health: every Balkan wedding, Irish céilí, Breton fest-noz, and Andalusian feria is a room full of people who know the steps. Two families are treated at depth because the commons holds their music: flamenco and the Celtic–Nordic tune traditions.

    Flamenco¶

    Flamenco is the Andalusian art of song (cante), guitar (toque), and dance (baile), with Romani, Andalusian, Moorish, and Sephardic roots and a documented history from the late eighteenth century; cafés cantantes (1860s–1920s) made it a stage art, the tablao its nightclub home, and the twentieth century's ballet flamenco (La Argentina, Antonio Gades, Cristina Hoyos, Sara Baras, Rocío Molina, Israel Galván) its concert form — three functions of one vocabulary, as the first chapter says.

    The music the dancer drives. The commons' flamenco note is exact. The atom is the compás — a fixed accent pattern cycling under everything. Most serious palos share a twelve-beat cycle but disagree on the accents: soleá accents 3-6-8-10-12; bulería and alegrías use the same accents but count from twelve, so the accented beat is heard first; siguiriya recasts the twelve pulses as five uneven beats (3+3+2+2+2); tangos run a binary 4/4 family. This is a timeline reference, not a hierarchical meter — the palmas entrain to the asymmetric pattern the way a clave does — and a palo is a frame (compás, tempo range, modal center) within which the singer, guitarist, and dancer negotiate the realization live, cued by micro-gesture: the dancer's llamada calls a section change, the falseta (guitar interlude) and the letra (sung verse) never overlap, and the escobilla (the extended footwork passage) is the dancer's solo within the cycle. Escalation — rhythmic intensification, ornament, dynamic climax — happens inside the unchanging compás, never by restructuring it.1 The dance is that grammar embodied: zapateado (the nailed-heel footwork, a percussion part), the braceo (the arms' carriage, the floreo of the hands), the torso's quiebro, the bata de cola and mantón as apparatus (dressing the genre), and — the point — the dancer as conductor: the baile's llamadas and remates (closing accents) drive the musicians, who watch the feet. Flamenco is the working-with-music chapter's cue-based mode with the dancer holding the cues, and the codified-vocabularies chapter's "grammar with few nouns."

    Ireland, Scotland, and the tune traditions¶

    Irish step dance — the rigid torso, arms at the sides, and virtuoso feet: hard shoe (the hornpipe, treble jig, and set dances, with fiberglass-tipped shoes) and soft shoe (reel, slip jig, in ghillies); the competitive feis circuit with its solo dresses and wigs; the céilí group dances; and since Riverdance (1994) a global stage form. Beside it, sean-nós ("old style") — the loose-armed, low-to-the-ground, improvisational solo step dance of the Gaeltacht, battering close to the floor. Scottish Highland dance (the Highland fling, sword dance, seann triubhas) — competitive, balletic, at the Highland games — and Scottish country dance (the RSCDS's codified social sets); Cape Breton step dance, the Scottish emigrants' preserved percussive style.

    The music the dancers chain. The commons: the atom of Irish and Scottish dance music is the eight-bar strain, near-universally chained as AABB and cycled about three times before the next tune; meter is a separate parameter per tune type — reel (4/4), jig (6/8), slip jig (9/8), hornpipe (dotted 4/4) — and a session chains tunes into a set, negotiated live, with dance-typed contexts fixing the genre sequence.2 The step dancer's vocabulary follows: steps are built to the eight-bar strain, the tune type decides the step family, and the céilí's figures are sized to the AABB round. Scandinavia's polska and Norway's springar are the family's other branch, and the commons' finding about them matters for any generator: the long-medium-short beat of the polska is the meter, not a sloppy triplet — motion-capture studies of the dancers' stamping confirm the asymmetry is systematic — and quantizing it to even triplets erases the dance.

    The Balkans and the chain dances¶

    The chain and circle dances of southeastern Europe — Serbian and Croatian kolo, Bulgarian horo, Macedonian oro, Greek syrtos and kalamatianos, Romanian hora and sârba, Albanian valle, the Jewish hora (from the Romanian) — are the participatory function at its purest: linked hands or shoulders, a leader at the head, steps repeated across an open-ended line to music that is often in the additive meters (aksak, Brăiloiu's term) the working-with-music chapter describes — 7/8 as 2+2+3 (rǎchenitsa), 9/8 as 2+2+2+3, 11/16 (kopanitsa). The dancers' quick-quick-slow is the meter's cells; you can read the time signature off the feet. Bulgaria's state folk ensembles (Philip Koutev, 1951) and the international folk-dance movement turned these into stage and recreational forms worldwide.

    The turning dances: waltz, polka, and the court-to-ballroom line¶

    The waltz (Austro-German Ländler and Walzer, late eighteenth century: the closed couple, turning, scandalous) and the polka (Bohemia, 1830s; a European craze by the 1840s), the mazurka, the schottische, and the galop are the nineteenth century's social dances and the root of ballroom; before them, the court's basse danse, pavane, galliard, minuet, and contredanse (from the English country dance — the ancestor of square and contra) are the dances ballet's lexicon codified. Italian tarantella and Salentine pizzica (the trance-healing dance of the "spider's bite"), Spanish jota and sardana, Portuguese vira, Hungarian csárdás, Polish mazurka and polonaise, Russian and Ukrainian hopak with its squat-kicks, Georgian dance (on the Middle East and Central Asia page) fill out the continent.

    Ritual survivals: Morris, sword, and the rest¶

    England's Morris dance (Cotswold, with sticks, bells, and handkerchiefs; Border and North-West clog traditions; rapper and longsword dances in which the linked swords are woven into a star) are ritual-calendar dances with contested origins and a vigorous revival; Basque ezpata-dantza, Portuguese pauliteiros, and the Faroese chain dance (to ballads, unbroken since the Middle Ages) are their cousins.

    What the region teaches the core¶

    That a dancer can be the ensemble's conductor (flamenco's llamadas); that the tune's strain length and meter type are the step's grammar (the Celtic tradition's AABB and the polska's uneven beat); and that additive meter is readable from the feet — three reasons a movement generator should model the music's framework, not its onsets.
