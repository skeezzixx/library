    The modern revolt and after¶

    Part I · The territory — chapter 3 of 4 · ← Deep dive: the pointe shoe as technology · Street and club styles →

    This is the spine of the library's core tier — the lineage from which nearly every idea in Parts II and V comes, and on which the shelf's reference pages hang. It is told here as a lineage because that is how the field tells it: teacher to student, company to splinter, each generation defining itself against the last. The technique chapter covers what these people taught their dancers to do; the costume and props chapters cover how they dressed and staged it; the shelf covers the individuals at length. This chapter is the map between them.

    Before: the soloists¶

    Modern dance begins as a refusal of ballet by women dancing alone. Loie Fuller's Serpentine Dance (1892) — silk, wands, colored light — made the dancer a moving image and the stage a light-box; Isadora Duncan, from around 1900, danced barefoot in a Greek tunic to Chopin, Gluck, and Beethoven, in a vocabulary of runs, skips, and gestures from the solar plexus, and declared in The Dance of the Future (1903) that ballet was deformed and nature was the model. Both were Americans who succeeded in Europe; both claimed the body's "natural" movement as a value; both were, in the first chapter's terms, inventing concert dance outside the only existing concert tradition. Behind them stood two systems of expressive movement — François Delsarte's gesture analysis and Émile Jaques-Dalcroze's eurhythmics — that gave the revolt a pedagogy.

    Ruth St. Denis (Radha, 1906) brought an exoticist, spiritual orientalism to the American stage, and with Ted Shawn founded Denishawn (Los Angeles, 1915–31): the school and company through which American modern dance's second generation passed. Denishawn's repertory was eclectic and its "music visualizations" literal, but its graduates — Martha Graham, Doris Humphrey, Charles Weidman — left it in revolt, and Shawn's all-male company and his farm at Jacob's Pillow (1933) gave modern dance its first festival.

    Germany: Ausdruckstanz¶

    A parallel revolt in German-speaking Europe produced the Ausdruckstanz, expressive dance. Rudolf Laban — the movement theorist of the effort chapter and the notation chapter — ran movement choirs, built a movement analysis, and taught Mary Wigman, whose Hexentanz (1914; the masked 1926 version) and solos of the 1920s were dark, weighted, earthbound, and entirely unballetic; her Dresden school (1920) trained a generation, and its New York branch under Hanya Holm seeded the American line. Kurt Jooss's The Green Table (1932), the anti-war ballet with its masked diplomats, is the one Ausdruckstanz work in the permanent repertory; his school at the Folkwang in Essen is where Pina Bausch later trained. The movement's entanglement with and destruction by the Nazi period — Laban's exile in 1937, Wigman's compromises — broke its continuity, and its deeper influence ran underground until Tanztheater resurfaced it in the 1970s.

    The Graham generation: 1926–1960¶

    American modern dance proper begins with Martha Graham's first independent concert (1926) and consolidates at the Bennington School of the Dance (1934–42), the summer program where the founders taught and premiered side by side. Four lines:

    • Graham built a technique on the breath — contraction and release, the spiral, the fall — and a repertory that moved from austere abstraction (Heretic, 1929; Lamentation, 1930; Primitive Mysteries, 1931) through Americana (Frontier, 1935; Appalachian Spring, 1944) to the Greek myth cycle (Cave of the Heart, 1946; Night Journey, 1947; Clytemnestra, 1958). Her composer-adviser Louis Horst taught composition to the whole generation; Noguchi built her sets; she danced until 1969 and choreographed until 1990.
    • Doris Humphrey and Charles Weidman (company 1928–44) built the other technique — fall and recovery, the body as a pendulum between the two deaths of standing still and lying down — and a repertory of ensemble works (Water Study, 1928; The Shakers, 1931; the New Dance trilogy, 1935–36). Humphrey's The Art of Making Dances (1959) is the craft chapter's canon; her protégé José Limón carried the line (The Moor's Pavane, 1949) and his company survives.
    • Hanya Holm brought Wigman's German weight to America and then to Broadway (Kiss Me, Kate, 1948 — the first copyrighted choreography).
    • The Black modern lineage, too often told as a sidebar and in fact central: Katherine Dunham, anthropologist of Haitian and Caribbean dance, built a technique and a company (Tropics and Le Jazz Hot, 1940) that fused them with modern and ballet and trained a generation; Pearl Primus (Strange Fruit, 1943) did for African and African-American material what Dunham did for the Caribbean; Lester Horton in Los Angeles built a technique (still taught) and a company whose dancers included Alvin Ailey and Carmen de Lavallade; Talley Beatty and Donald McKayle (Rainbow 'Round My Shoulder, 1959) made the repertory; and Alvin Ailey founded his company in 1958 and made Revelations (1960), the most-performed modern dance work in the world. Anna Sokolow's Rooms (1955) and the New Dance Group (1932) carried the period's political strain.

    Cunningham's break: 1944–¶

    Merce Cunningham danced for Graham (1939–45), gave his first solo concert with John Cage in 1944, founded his company at Black Mountain College in 1953, and broke with everything modern dance had assumed: that dance expresses, that music and movement are related, that the stage has a front and a center, that the choreographer chooses. His chance procedures decided continuity; music, décor, and dance were made independently; any point in the space was a center; and the technique — a long, erect, balletic spine over fast, complex legs — produced a body ready for any sequence. The "Events" (from 1964) recombined repertory excerpts for non-theatre spaces; the visual artists (Rauschenberg, Johns, Warhol, Stella) made the company a crossroads of the New York avant-garde; LifeForms (1989) made him the first choreographer to compose on a computer. He worked until his death in 2009. Beside him, Paul Taylor (from Graham and Cunningham; Aureole, 1962; Esplanade, 1975) built the great mid-century American repertory company, and Alwin Nikolais and Murray Louis the total-design theatre.

    Judson and the postmodern turn: 1962–1980¶

    Cunningham's composition teacher Robert Dunn ran a class (1960–62) applying Cage's methods to dance; its students began showing work at Judson Memorial Church in Greenwich Village in 1962, and the Judson Dance Theater (1962–64) became the name for a generation that stripped dance to walking, running, task, and rule. Yvonne Rainer's Trio A (1966), four and a half minutes of continuous, unaccented, unrepeated movement performed with the gaze averted, is the emblem, and her "No Manifesto" (1965) the creed. Steve Paxton made walking pieces (Satisfyin Lover, 1967) and then invented contact improvisation (1972), the duet form of shared weight that became a worldwide practice. Trisha Brown made equipment pieces on walls and rooftops, then the accumulations (1971), then — with Rauschenberg and Laurie Anderson — Set and Reset (1983), the liquid, released, sliding vocabulary the technique chapter describes. Lucinda Childs moved from Street Dance (1964) to the pattern-permutations of Dance (1979) with Glass and LeWitt. Simone Forti's Dance Constructions (1961), David Gordon, Deborah Hay, Meredith Monk, and the Grand Union (1970–76, an improvisation collective) complete the core. Sally Banes's Terpsichore in Sneakers (1980) named it postmodern and is still its standard account.

    The 1980s turned back toward spectacle, virtuosity, and content. Twyla Tharp crossed over — Deuce Coupe (1973) for the Joffrey, Push Comes to Shove (1976) for Baryshnikov — and made the pop-score, rigorously structured, loose-bodied style that fed Broadway and ballet alike. Mark Morris (company 1980) restored music and narrative. Bill T. Jones and Arnie Zane made identity, illness, and race the subject (Still/Here, 1994, and its critical firestorm). Pilobolus (1971) and MOMIX made collaborative body-sculpture; Elizabeth Streb made impact; Garth Fagan, Stephen Petronio, Molissa Fenley, Bebe Miller filled the downtown decade.

    Tanztheater and the European wave: 1973–2000¶

    In Wuppertal, from 1973, Pina Bausch made something that was neither dance nor theatre — Tanztheater: evening-length pieces built from questions put to the dancers, assembled from repeated gestures, speech, song, social dance, and cruelty, on stages of earth, water, and flowers, in evening clothes. The Rite of Spring (1975), Café Müller and Kontakthof (1978), 1980, Nelken (1982), and some forty more made her the most influential European choreographer of the century's last quarter; the Folkwang line (Reinhild Hoffmann, Susanne Linke) and Johann Kresnik's political "choreographic theatre" surrounded her.

    Belgium produced the next wave: Anne Teresa De Keersmaeker (Rosas, 1983; Fase, 1982; Rosas danst Rosas, 1983 — the mathematical minimalist), Wim Vandekeybus (Ultima Vez, 1986 — risk and impact), Alain Platel (les ballets C de la B — outsider bodies, chaos), Jan Fabre, and later Sidi Larbi Cherkaoui. France's nouvelle danse of the 1980s, funded through the decentralized Centres Chorégraphiques Nationaux — Maguy Marin (May B, 1981), Dominique Bagouet, Jean-Claude Gallotta, Régine Chopinot, Angelin Preljocaj, Philippe Decouflé — made France the most densely subsidized dance culture in the world. Britain had Richard Alston and Siobhan Davies in the Cunningham line, Michael Clark's punk ballet, Lloyd Newson's DV8 (Dead Dreams of Monochrome Men, 1988) founding physical theatre, and Matthew Bourne's all-male Swan Lake (1995). And in Japan, a parallel revolt: butoh — Hijikata Tatsumi's Kinjiki (1959), Kazuo Ohno's Admiring La Argentina (1977), Sankai Juku (1975) — the white-painted, slow, grotesque, inward body that is the makeup chapter's limit case and the shelf's.

    The contemporary now: 2000–¶

    "Contemporary dance" names a field, not a style, and its geography has widened past the transatlantic axis. A map by region, each entry a door to a shelf page or a tradition:

    • Israel — Batsheva (Graham's 1964 foundation) under Ohad Naharin from 1990: Gaga, the anti-vocabulary, and Decadance's touring fragments; Sharon Eyal's pulsing, DJ-driven unison; Hofesh Shechter's percussion- scored mass choreography (Political Mother, 2010), now British.
    • Britain — Akram Khan's kathak-contemporary (Zero Degrees, 2005, with Cherkaoui; Desh, 2011; his Giselle, 2016); Wayne McGregor's hyper-articulate bodies and computational collaborations; Russell Maliphant; the hip-hop theatre of Boy Blue and Botis Seva.
    • Canada — Crystal Pite's Kidd Pivot (Betroffenheit, 2015; Revisor, 2019) and her ballets (Flight Pattern, 2017); Édouard Lock's La La La Human Steps; Marie Chouinard.
    • The conceptual turn in continental Europe — Jérôme Bel (The Show Must Go On, 2001; Véronique Doisneau, 2004), Xavier Le Roy (Self Unfinished, 1998; Retrospective, 2012), Boris Charmatz's Musée de la danse (2009), La Ribot, Tino Sehgal, Meg Stuart's Damaged Goods — dance about dance, in galleries as often as theatres.
    • Africa and its diaspora — Germaine Acogny's École des Sables in Senegal and the Acogny technique; Faustin Linyekula (Kisangani), Gregory Maqoma and Dada Masilo (Johannesburg — her Swan Lake, 2010), Qudus Onikeku, Nora Chipaumire; in America Kyle Abraham, Camille A. Brown, Ralph Lemon, Okwui Okpokwasili, and Trajal Harrell's fusion of voguing with Judson.
    • Asia — Lin Hwai-min's Cloud Gate (Taiwan, 1973; Moon Water, 1998; Songs of the Wanderers, 1994, danced on tons of rice), Tao Ye's numbered abstractions (Beijing), Shen Wei (New York), Padmini Chettur and Chandralekha's post-bharatanatyam minimalism (India), Eko Supriyanto (Indonesia).
    • Latin America — Grupo Corpo (Belo Horizonte, 1975), Deborah Colker (Rio), Tânia Pérez-Salas (Mexico).
    • The United States — Pam Tanowitz, Faye Driscoll, Justin Peck in ballet, Rennie Harris's Puremovement (1992) bringing the street to the concert stage.

    The blur¶

    Two boundaries have dissolved in the last twenty years, and both matter for this subject. Contemporary and ballet: McGregor, Pite, Forsythe, Khan, and Ratmansky move between companies freely; a ballet company's "contemporary" program and a contemporary company's are often the same choreographers; "contemporary ballet" is a settled category. Contemporary and commercial: television competition formats since 2005 made "contemporary" a named style — lyrical, emotive, acrobatic, a hybrid of jazz, modern, and ballet — taught in every suburban studio and danced in music videos, and Gaga classes "for people" made Naharin's method a fitness practice. The concert lineage this chapter traced is now one stream among several that share a name, and the street styles of the next chapter are a third — which is the present state of the territory, and the reason the shelf matters: the lineage is where the ideas are, and the ideas are what the rest of this library is about.
