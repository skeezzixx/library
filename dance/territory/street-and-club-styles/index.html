    Street and club styles¶

    The styles in this chapter were invented in the last sixty years by young people in American cities, with no school, no company, no notation, and no choreographer — and they are now among the most widely practiced dance forms on earth, with named vocabularies as precise as ballet's, international competitive circuits, and, since 2024, an Olympic event. That makes them the counter-case to ballet's four centuries of institutions and the live demonstration of the codified-vocabularies chapter's claim that a canon can form without an academy. They are also, for this library's generative project, the dance forms most completely shaped by their music's structure, which the commons happens to hold in detail.

    The usual division is hip-hop styles (breaking and its East Coast party dances), funk styles (popping and locking, West Coast and older than hip-hop), and club styles (house, waacking, voguing), with krump as a later, separate birth. The division is cultural as much as formal, and practitioners defend it.

    Breaking¶

    Breaking was born in the Bronx in the early-to-mid 1970s, where DJ Kool Herc isolated and extended the percussion break of funk records — the section where the band drops out and the drums run — and dancers invented a form to fill it. The name is the music's: b-boys and b-girls dance the break. The vocabulary divides into toprock (standing footwork and posture, the entry), downrock or footwork (the six-step and its family, hands and feet on the floor), power moves (windmills, headspins, flares, airflares — the rotational, gymnastic material that television loves and purists rank below footwork), and freezes (held poses on the hands or head, the punctuation). Crews formed — the Zulu Kings under Afrika Bambaataa's Zulu Nation (1973), the Rock Steady Crew (1977) — and the battle, crew against crew in a cypher (the circle of onlookers who are also participants), became the form's native institution. Three films (Wild Style, 1983; Flashdance, 1983; Beat Street, 1984) took it global; Europe and Asia developed scenes that soon outran New York's; Battle of the Year (from 1990) and Red Bull BC One (from 2004) became the world championships; and Paris 2024 put it in the Olympics, with a judging system (technique, vocabulary, originality, execution, musicality) that the culture debated as both recognition and capture.1

    Popping and locking: the funk styles¶

    Locking is the older: Don Campbell in Los Angeles around 1970, the "Campbellock," a vocabulary of locks (sudden freezes), points, wrist rolls, knee drops, and comic timing, danced to funk; his group The Lockers (1973, including Toni Basil and Fred "Rerun" Berry) took it to Soul Train and national television, in the striped socks and apple hats the dressing-the-genre chapter described. Popping came from Fresno in the mid-1970s — Boogaloo Sam and the Electric Boogaloos — and is built on the hit or pop, a sudden contraction of the muscles on the beat, combined with boogaloo's rolling isolations and a family of illusions: the robot, waving, tutting (right-angled arm and hand geometry), animation, strobing. Popping is the most "mechanical" of the styles and the most obviously mappable to a beat grid; it is also a precise vocabulary with named sub-styles and ancestral credit, and practitioners are unforgiving about conflating it with breaking (the 1980s media label "electric boogie" lumped them and is still resented).

    The club styles¶

    House dance grew in the Chicago and New York clubs of the late 1980s — the Warehouse, the Paradise Garage — to house music: fast, intricate footwork (the "jack" — the torso's pump — over loose, skating, lofting steps), done for hours, in a loose community of dancers (Ejoe Wilson, Brian "Footwork" Green, Marjory Smarth are the names the scene credits) rather than in battle. Waacking came from the gay Black and Latino clubs of 1970s Los Angeles — whipped, rotating arm movements, posing, and a glamour that quotes Hollywood's divas — danced to disco (Tyrone Proctor, Viktor Manoel); it too reached Soul Train. Voguing is Harlem's: the ballroom scene of Black and Latino gay and trans communities, organized into houses (House of LaBeija, 1972; Xtravaganza; Ninja) that compete in balls across categories, many of them realness categories judged on passing as the named thing. The dance — lines, poses, and arm-work quoting fashion magazines — divides into Old Way (1980s: lines, symmetry, precision), New Way (1990s: flexibility, contortion, the arms' geometric illusions), and Vogue Fem (the dramatic elements: catwalk, duckwalk, hands, floor performance, spins and dips). Willi Ninja and Paris Dupree are the canonical figures; Paris Is Burning (1990), Madonna's "Vogue" (1990), and the series Pose (2018) carried it out of the ballroom and into the mainstream, with the usual argument about what was lost.2

    Krump and the rest¶

    Krump was born in South Central Los Angeles in the early 2000s (Tight Eyez and Big Mijo), out of Tommy the Clown's hip-hop clowning: chest pops, stomps, arm swings and jabs, an intensity — buck — that reads as rage and is practiced as release; the documentary Rize (2005) introduced it. Around and after these, the map keeps filling: Memphis jookin (the sliding, gliding footwork Lil Buck brought to concert stages), Chicago footwork to juke at 160 bpm, Detroit jit, Oakland turfing, Brooklyn flexing (the "bone-breaking" contortions of Storyboard P), Harlem litefeet; the long line of hip-hop party dances — the Running Man, the Cabbage Patch, the Harlem Shake, the Dougie, the Milly Rock, and the TikTok era's fifteen-second sequences; Jamaican dancehall's own lineage; and the African street styles (azonto, gwara gwara, the amapiano dances) that now travel the same viral routes. Each is a vocabulary with a place, a music, and a set of originators the culture can name.

    How a canon forms without an academy¶

    The codified-vocabularies chapter asked what codification costs and buys; this chapter's traditions show how it happens in the absence of every mechanism ballet used. The pieces:

    • Naming by practitioners. Moves are named by the people who invent them, often after the inventor, and the names spread through the cypher. A vocabulary exists when its terms are shared.
    • Foundation. Each style has a recognized core — breaking's toprock/footwork/freeze, popping's hit, locking's lock and point — that a dancer must demonstrate before originality counts. "Foundation" is the street's word for the closed vocabulary, and its guardians are the OGs, the originators and elders whose authority is historical rather than institutional.
    • The sin of biting. Copying another dancer's signature material without transformation is the culture's central offense. The rule enforces variation on the closed vocabulary — the operator logic of Part II as an ethic.
    • Transmission by circle and by tape. The cypher teaches by exposure; video (VHS in the 1980s, YouTube from 2005) made global transmission possible without a single school. A breaker in Seoul learned from tapes of New York, and then New York learned from Seoul.
    • Codification by competition. Battles need judges, judges need criteria, and criteria harden the vocabulary. The Olympic system made this explicit and institutional — which is exactly the moment a practitioner canon becomes an academy's, and exactly the moment the culture feared.

    The result is a set of vocabularies as precise and as teachable as ballet's, produced by peer legitimacy instead of by a king, in a fraction of the time. The machine-discovery chapter should be read with this in mind: an unsupervised system that clusters movement into units is doing mechanically what a cypher does socially, and the cypher's extra ingredient — the biting rule, which forces each unit to be used differently — is the part the clustering lacks.

    The music's grammar is the dance's¶

    Every style above is inseparable from a music, and the commons describes two of those musics in terms that explain the dance.

    Hip-hop runs two temporal ontologies at once: the instrumental is a cyclic loop — a two-to-four-bar drum-and-sample pattern with little internal change — and the MC's flow over it is linear and narrative; form is bar-counted (sixteen-bar verse, eight-bar hook) and punctuated by the sample-flip (variation by recontextualizing a fixed source) and the beat-switch, a hard, wholesale replacement of the instrumental at a structural boundary.3 Read the dance against that. The b-boy's break is the loop laid bare — the section that is only the cyclic drums — and breaking's structure (toprock to footwork to power to freeze, then the next dancer) is a set of short, self-contained sequences sized to the loop's cycle, with the freeze as a beat-switch in the body. The biting rule is the sample-flip as an ethic: take the foundation, flip it.

    House and techno are loop-grid music — a one-or-two-bar four-on-the-floor pattern as the state — whose sections are not new material but which layers are active, carved by muting and filtering, with phrase changes landing on eight-, sixteen-, and thirty-two-bar boundaries almost as a hard rule, because dancers and mixing DJs both need the boundary to be predictable.4 House dance is shaped by that precisely: continuous, non-sectional footwork that rides the loop for hours, with the dancer's own variation (the jack's amplitude, the footwork pattern, the lofting) doing the work that layer changes do in the music, and the phrase boundary as the place to change. It is participatory dance to music designed for participation, and it is the closest the contemporary world comes to the social function in its original form: the point is that everyone is dancing, and the music is built so that they can.

    For the generative project the lesson is blunt. These dances are grid-native in a way concert dance never was — their units are sized to the loop, their changes land on the phrase line, their variation is governed by an explicit rule against repetition — and a system that modeled the music's loop-and-bar grammar first would find the dance's structure already given. The music-to-dance generators trained on AIST++ (ten genres, most of them street) are closer to this than they know.
