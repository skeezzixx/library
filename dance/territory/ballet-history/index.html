    Ballet: the long history¶

    Part I · The territory — chapter 2 of 4 · ← Ballroom · Deep dive: the Ballets Russes →

    Ballet is the only dance tradition with four centuries of continuous institutional history — a lexicon, schools, companies, a repertory, and a paper trail — which makes it both the best- documented dance in the world and the one most often mistaken for dance in general. This chapter tells the history at the depth the syllabus assigns (detailed, not exhaustive), with two deep dives where the material earns them: the Ballets Russes, the twenty years that made ballet modern, and the pointe shoe, the one piece of dance equipment that is unambiguously a technology. The codified-vocabularies chapter covers the lexicon itself; the costume history covers the tutu; the composing chapter covers Tchaikovsky and Stravinsky. Here is the spine they hang on.

    The court: 1580–1760¶

    Ballet is born in the Italian Renaissance courts — Domenico da Piacenza's treatise (c. 1455) is the first dance manual in Europe — and grows up in France, where Catherine de' Medici's Ballet Comique de la Reine (1581) is conventionally the first ballet: a court spectacle of dance, verse, music, and machinery, performed by nobles for nobles. Under Louis XIV it became a state art. The king danced (the Ballet de la nuit, 1653), founded the Académie Royale de Danse (1661) to codify the technique, and his dancing master Pierre Beauchamp fixed the five positions of the feet that every ballet class still begins with. When the Académie Royale de Musique — the Paris Opéra — opened in 1669, dance moved from the court to the professional stage; women professionals appeared in 1681; and the Beauchamp-Feuillet notation (the notation chapter) recorded the repertory of a technique that was, at this point, turned out, upright, precise, and danced in heeled shoes and heavy costume.

    The eighteenth century's reforms were the costume history's removals and one idea: the ballet d'action. Jean-Georges Noverre's Lettres sur la danse (1760) argued that ballet should tell stories through expressive movement rather than decorate operas with divertissements; Gasparo Angiolini made the same argument in Vienna; Jean Dauberval's La Fille mal gardée (1789) is the oldest ballet still in the repertory in any form. The virtuoso male dancer (Auguste Vestris, the dieu de la danse) reigned; Charles Didelot took French technique to St Petersburg and hung dancers on wires.

    Romantic ballet: 1830–1850¶

    Within two decades the art changed sex and subject. Marie Taglioni's La Sylphide (Paris, 1832; choreography by her father Filippo) made the ballerina the center, the supernatural the subject, and rising onto the toes — pointe — the signature: a trick became the image of weightlessness. Giselle (1841; Jean Coralli and Jules Perrot, for Carlotta Grisi) is the Romantic ballet that survives whole: a peasant girl, a betrayal, madness, death, and a second act of vengeful spirits in white — the ballet blanc — whose corps de ballet in moonlit unison is still the test of a company. Perrot's Pas de Quatre (1845) put four rival ballerinas on one stage. In Copenhagen, August Bournonville built a parallel tradition — his own La Sylphide (1836), Napoli (1842) — whose light, bounding, buoyant style and strong male roles the Royal Danish Ballet has preserved almost intact, a living dialect from the 1840s. Everywhere else the male dancer faded; by mid-century in Paris men's roles were sometimes danced by women en travesti, and ballet's image as a feminine art dates from here.

    Petipa and Imperial Russia: 1850–1910¶

    The center moved east. Marius Petipa, a Marseillais, arrived in St Petersburg in 1847 and ran the Imperial Ballet for half a century, creating or revising the works that define classical ballet: Don Quixote (1869), La Bayadère (1877 — the Kingdom of the Shades, thirty-two ballerinas descending a ramp in arabesque, the purest abstraction in the nineteenth-century repertory), The Sleeping Beauty (1890), The Nutcracker (1892, staged mostly by his assistant Lev Ivanov), Swan Lake (the 1895 revival with Ivanov that rescued Tchaikovsky's score from its failed 1877 premiere), Raymonda (1898). Petipa's contribution was structure: the full-evening ballet as an architecture of mime scenes, character dances, ensembles, and the grand pas — entrée, adagio, variations, coda — whose grammar the composing chapter describes from the composer's side. The Italian virtuosas of the 1880s and 90s (Virginia Zucchi, Pierina Legnani and her thirty-two fouettés) brought a technique the Russians absorbed; Enrico Cecchetti taught them; and the Imperial school's training, systematized in the Soviet era by Agrippina Vaganova (Basic Principles of Classical Ballet, 1934), became the Vaganova method — the most influential of the schools.

    The schools matter, because ballet's lexicon is one language with dialects: Vaganova (Russian: expansive back, high extensions, plastique arms), Cecchetti (Italian: anatomical rigor, set daily exercises), Bournonville (Danish: light, quick, the arms low), the French school (Paris Opéra: elegance, speed, petite batterie), the Royal Academy of Dance syllabus (English, 1920), and Balanchine's (American: speed, attack, off-balance, the "neoclassical" line). A dancer's schooling is audible in the body the way an accent is in a voice.

    The Ballets Russes: 1909–1929¶

    Then Serge Diaghilev. The Ballets Russes — a touring company of Imperial-trained dancers, based in Paris and Monte Carlo, that never performed in Russia — made ballet a modern art in twenty seasons, and its story is told at length in the deep dive. In brief: Michel Fokine's reforms (Les Sylphides, The Firebird, Petrushka, expressive one-act ballets with every element unified); Nijinsky's three shocks (L'Après-midi d'un faune, 1912; Jeux and The Rite of Spring, 1913); Massine, Nijinska (Les Noces, 1923), and the young Balanchine (Apollo, 1928; Prodigal Son, 1929); scores by Stravinsky, Debussy, Ravel, Satie, Prokofiev, Poulenc; designs by Bakst, Benois, Picasso, Matisse, Chanel. When Diaghilev died in 1929 the company dissolved and its people founded the twentieth century: Ninette de Valois's Vic-Wells Ballet (1931, later the Royal Ballet), Marie Rambert's company (1926), Balanchine and Lincoln Kirstein's School of American Ballet (1934) and New York City Ballet (1948), Serge Lifar at the Paris Opéra (1930–58), the various Ballets Russes de Monte Carlo touring America. Ballet's worldwide company system is the Diaghilev diaspora.

    Neoclassicism and the twentieth century: 1930–1990¶

    Balanchine in America made plotless ballet the century's central form: Serenade (1934, the first work for his American students), Concerto Barocco (1941), The Four Temperaments (1946), Agon (1957), Jewels (1967) — the music visualized, the vocabulary sped up and pulled off its axis, the tutu replaced by practice clothes (the costume history's leotard ballets). Ashton in England made the lyrical, épaulement-rich English style — Symphonic Variations (1946), La Fille mal gardée (1960), The Dream (1964) — and Kenneth MacMillan the dramatic, psychologically raw full-evening ballet: Romeo and Juliet (1965), Manon (1974), Mayerling (1978). Jerome Robbins moved between Broadway and NYCB (Fancy Free, 1944; Afternoon of a Faun, 1953; Dances at a Gathering, 1969). The Soviet ballet built its own canon — the drama-ballet, Yuri Grigorovich's Spartacus (1968) at the Bolshoi, Vaganova's school at the Kirov — and exported it through defections (Nureyev 1961, Makarova 1970, Baryshnikov 1974) that transformed Western companies. In Europe: John Cranko's Stuttgart (Onegin, 1965) and his heirs John Neumeier (Hamburg) and Jiří Kylián (Nederlands Dans Theater); Maurice Béjart's populist spectacles (Boléro, 1961); Mats Ek's reimagined classics (Giselle, 1982, set in an asylum); and from 1984 William Forsythe at Ballett Frankfurt, taking the Balanchine line to its deconstructive limit (Artifact, 1984; In the Middle, Somewhat Elevated, 1987) — the hinge to the technique chapter and to Part V. Arthur Mitchell's Dance Theatre of Harlem (1969) answered the art form's whiteness with a classical company of Black dancers.

    Now¶

    Ballet in the 2020s is a repertory art in a company system: the great houses — Paris Opéra, the Royal Ballet, the Bolshoi and Mariinsky, New York City Ballet, American Ballet Theatre, the Royal Danish, La Scala, Stuttgart, Hamburg, San Francisco, the National Ballet of Canada, the Australian Ballet, Dutch National, and dozens more — each mixing the classics, Balanchine, Forsythe, and new work. Its choreographers are a recognizable set: Christopher Wheeldon and Alexei Ratmansky (whose reconstructions from Stepanov notation are the notation chapter's vindication); Wayne McGregor as the Royal Ballet's resident since 2006; Crystal Pite; Justin Peck at NYCB; Akram Khan's Giselle (2016) for English National Ballet; Cathy Marston, Pam Tanowitz, Alonzo King. Its arguments are about its inheritance: the racial and gender assumptions of the canon (pointe shoes in skin tones arrived only in 2018), the body standards, the nineteenth-century ballets' orientalism and their revision, the Russian companies' isolation after 2022, and the blur between contemporary ballet and contemporary dance — whether "ballet" names a technique, a repertory, or an institution. The best short answer is that it names all three, and that the technique is the durable part: a lexicon four centuries deep, taught in every city on earth, that every other form in this subject positions itself against.

    What ballet is, structurally¶

    For the rest of the library, the facts to carry:

    • The lexicon — several hundred named French terms for positions, movements, and qualities; the oldest codified movement vocabulary in continuous use, and the codified-vocabularies chapter's central case.
    • Turnout, the outward rotation of the legs from the hip, the technique's defining physical premise and the source of its line and its injuries; the plausible-body chapter treats the anatomy.
    • The schools as dialects of one language, audible in the body.
    • Pointe, the technology that made Romantic ballet's image — the deep dive.
    • The grand pas and the full-evening architecture, Petipa's form, which the music was written to fit.
    • The company-and-school system, Diaghilev's diaspora, which makes ballet the most institutionally reproduced dance in the world.
