    The Ballets Russes¶

    Part I · The territory — deep dive · ← Ballet: the long history · Deep dive: the pointe shoe as technology →

    Deep dive off Ballet: the long history.

    Between May 1909 and August 1929 a touring company of Russian-trained dancers, based in Paris and Monte Carlo and run by an impresario who neither danced nor choreographed nor composed, remade ballet as a modern art. It premiered most of the twentieth century's first great scores, brought the School of Paris into the theatre, produced four major choreographers in succession, and, when it dissolved on its founder's death, scattered the people who founded the company system the world still uses. No twenty-year span in dance history did more, and the reason is worth understanding precisely: the Ballets Russes was an institution for collaboration — a machine for putting the best choreographer, composer, and painter in one room and obliging them to make one thing.

    Diaghilev¶

    Serge Diaghilev (1872–1929) came to ballet sideways — through the Mir iskusstva (World of Art) circle in St Petersburg, art exhibitions in Paris, and a 1908 season of Russian opera there. The 1909 ballet season at the Châtelet, with Imperial dancers on leave — Anna Pavlova, Tamara Karsavina, Vaslav Nijinsky, Ida Rubinstein — and Fokine's repertory, was a sensation; from 1911 the company was permanent and independent of Russia; after 1917 it could not go back. Diaghilev's talent was taste, persuasion, and the will to unify: he chose the collaborators, paired them, overruled them, and paid for it all by charm and debt. He was also the first producer to make the total work — music, décor, movement conceived together — a practical rather than a Wagnerian ideal.

    Fokine: the reform, 1909–1912¶

    Michel Fokine arrived with a program, later set down as his "Five Principles": movement should suit the period and subject rather than the ready-made lexicon; mime should be expressive through the whole body, not a sign language; the corps should dance, not decorate; and music, design, and dance should be equal partners. The early repertory is the program enacted. Les Sylphides (1909, to Chopin) is the first plotless ballet — a Romantic ballet blanc with the plot removed, leaving mood. The Firebird (1910), Stravinsky's first ballet, and Schéhérazade (1910), Bakst's orientalist riot, made the company's name. Petrushka (1911; Stravinsky, Benois, Nijinsky as the puppet) is Fokine's masterpiece — a fairground, a love triangle of puppets, a death — where every dance is character and every character is movement. Le Spectre de la rose (1911) gave Nijinsky the leap through the window that became legend. Fokine's reforms are now simply what ballet is; in 1909 they were a revolution.

    Nijinsky: the three shocks, 1912–1913¶

    Vaslav Nijinsky was the greatest male dancer of his era and, for two years, a choreographer of a radicalism no one had anticipated. L'Après-midi d'un faune (1912; Debussy, Bakst) is twelve minutes of profile movement — heads and feet turned sideways as on a Greek vase, the body flattened, the faun's final erotic gesture over the nymph's scarf — with no ballet steps at all, made in over a hundred rehearsals. Jeux (1913; Debussy) put three tennis players in modern dress in a flirtation that critics found baffling. And The Rite of Spring (29 May 1913; Stravinsky, Roerich) — turned-in feet, stamping, hunched bodies, the Chosen One dancing herself to death to music of unprecedented rhythmic violence — provoked the most famous riot in the history of the performing arts. The counting crisis (the dancers counting aloud, Marie Rambert drilling the rhythm) is told in the composing chapter. Nijinsky's choreography was lost within a few years — the Rite survived only eight performances, and was reconstructed in 1987 by Millicent Hodson and Kenneth Archer — but the idea that ballet's vocabulary could be discarded entirely and a new one invented for one work was in the air from then on. He was dismissed in 1913 after marrying; his last years were spent in mental illness.

    Massine, Nijinska, Balanchine: 1915–1929¶

    Léonide Massine was the war years' and the 1920s' choreographer: Parade (1917; Satie, Cocteau, Picasso's Cubist costumes — the costume history's limit case), La Boutique fantasque (1919), Le Tricorne (1919; de Falla, Picasso), character dance and wit; later, in the 1930s, the symphonic ballets. Bronislava Nijinska, Vaslav's sister, made the company's two greatest works of the 1920s: Les Noces (1923; Stravinsky; Goncharova's brown-and-white peasant designs), a Russian wedding as a massed, stamping, architectural ritual, and Les Biches (1924; Poulenc; Marie Laurencin), the ironic, ambiguous house party that is the modern sensibility's first ballet. And George Balanchine, arriving from Petrograd in 1924, made Apollon musagète (1928; Stravinsky) — the birth of the god, the three muses, a clarity that taught him, he said, he could eliminate — and The Prodigal Son (1929; Prokofiev, Rouault), weeks before Diaghilev died. In Balanchine the company passed its inheritance to the second half of the century.

    The collaborators¶

    The list is the point. Composers: Stravinsky (Firebird, Petrushka, Rite, Pulcinella, Les Noces, Apollo), Debussy, Ravel (Daphnis et Chloé, 1912), Satie, Prokofiev, Poulenc, Milhaud, Auric, de Falla, Respighi. Painters and designers: Bakst, Benois, Roerich, Goncharova, Larionov, Picasso, Matisse, Derain, Braque, Miró, Ernst, de Chirico, Rouault, Laurencin, Chanel. Poets and librettists: Cocteau. Conductors: Monteux, Ansermet. Ballet had always used composers and designers; the Ballets Russes used the leading ones, and made the theatre a place where modern music and modern painting first met a mass public. The props chapter and the costume history follow the painters; the composing chapter the composers.

    Why the diaspora matters¶

    Diaghilev died in Venice in August 1929, insolvent; the company could not survive him. Its dispersal is the founding of the modern company system:

    • Ninette de Valois (a company dancer) founded the Vic-Wells Ballet in London (1931) — the Royal Ballet from 1956 — and Marie Rambert the Ballet Rambert (1926): British ballet.
    • Balanchine went to America with Lincoln Kirstein: the School of American Ballet (1934), Ballet Society, New York City Ballet (1948): American ballet.
    • Serge Lifar ran the Paris Opéra Ballet (1930–58) and restored its dignity.
    • The Ballets Russes de Monte Carlo (de Basil's and Denham's rival companies, 1932 onward) toured America for two decades and seeded regional ballet across the continent.
    • Dancers and teachers settled everywhere — Australia, Canada, South America — and taught.

    Ballet's worldwide network of national and civic companies, each with a school, a repertory mixing classics and new work, and a touring life, is this diaspora's shape. So is the expectation that a ballet is a collaboration among named artists — the thing Diaghilev institutionalized — and so, in a sense, is the shelf of this library: the idea that choreographers are authors whose works are studied is one the Ballets Russes made real.
