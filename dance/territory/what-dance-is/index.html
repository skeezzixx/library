    What dance is; what choreography is¶

    Every subject in this library opens by saying what its thing is, and dance resists the exercise more than most. The usual definition — rhythmic movement of the body, organized for its own sake or for expression — admits the wrong things (a marching band; a child skipping) and, more seriously, excludes the right ones: the Sufi whirler is not expressing, the ballroom competitor is not moving for its own sake, and a good deal of contemporary concert dance is neither rhythmic nor, in any obvious sense, organized. The working definition this subject uses is framing: dance is movement that a community treats as dance — watches, learns, names, evaluates, and transmits as dance. That sounds evasive and is in fact the most useful thing anyone has said about it, because it turns the question "what is dance?" into the question "what does this community's framing do?" — and the answer varies enormously, in ways that decide everything downstream.1

    This chapter lays out the basic ontology — the nouns every later chapter assumes — and then the lens Ryan asked for: the functions of dance, which the world atlas groups geographically and which this chapter treats as the primary way to understand any dance you meet.

    The nouns¶

    The dancer is the instrument and the performer at once — the one fact that makes dance unlike music, where a violin is not a violinist. Everything the dancer is (trained, tired, tall, injured, sixty) is in the dance, and every vocabulary in Part II is, at bottom, a way of making one dancer's movement repeatable by another.

    The choreographer is a recent invention. The word comes from Feuillet's Chorégraphie (1700), where it meant dance-writing; by the nineteenth century it meant the composing of dances, and the figure of the choreographer as author — a named person whose work is the dance — consolidated with the ballet masters of Romantic and Imperial ballet and hardened into a profession only in the twentieth century. Most of the world's dance has no choreographer in this sense. It has tradition, teachers, lead dancers, master drummers, ritual specialists, and communities who all know the steps; the authored work is a Western concert convention, and this library's generative project inherits the convention wholesale (a system that composes dances is, structurally, a choreographer). It is worth remembering how local that is.

    The work, the score, the performance. Is a dance the thing that happened on Tuesday night, or the thing that happens every time it is performed? Philosophers of art distinguish autographic works, identical with a physical object (a painting), from allographic ones, which exist as a type realized in many performances (a symphony) — and Nelson Goodman's observation was that dance is allographic only if it has a notation that fixes what counts as a correct performance.2 The notation chapter explains why it mostly doesn't; the practical consequence is that dance works are held in bodies and tradition — a répétiteur who knows Giselle, a company that knows its Bausch — and that a score, where one exists, may be a Labanotation manuscript, a video, a set of rules, or an instruction to improvise. The chance chapter is largely about scores that deliberately under-specify the performance, which is only possible once the distinction is clear.

    Improvisation versus set material is a spectrum, not a switch. At one end, Swan Lake's second act is set to the count; at the other, a contact-improvisation jam has no material at all. In between lies most of the world: structured improvisation (a score, a task, a frame within which the dancer chooses — a raga's grammar, a flamenco palo's compás, a cypher's conventions), and the universal studio reality that "set" material was improvised into existence last month. The distinction the chance chapter draws between a frame (checkable constraints) and a realization (what happens inside them) is the precise version, and it applies across the whole spectrum.

    The four functions¶

    Now the lens. Dance is done for something, and four functions recur across every culture — ritual, social, concert, and competitive. They are not genres; the same steps can serve different functions (and often migrate between them), and the function changes who watches, who may join, what counts as good, how the dance is taught, how it relates to its music, and — the point for this library — what a machine would be asked to do. The ethnomusicologist Thomas Turino's distinction between participatory performance (everyone present takes part; there is no audience; the goal is the maximum number of people dancing) and presentational performance (a prepared performance by some for others) cuts across the four and is the single most useful tool for reading them.3

    Ritual: dance as efficacy¶

    Ritual dance is done to accomplish something — to honor, to heal, to induct, to invoke, to mark a passage — and its criterion is efficacy, not beauty. The Mevlevi sema, the whirling of the Sufi dervishes, is a form of dhikr, remembrance of God; Haitian Vodou and Cuban Santería dance to summon specific spirits, each with a characteristic rhythm and movement; Balinese sanghyang is trance-dance in which the dancer is understood to be possessed; Japanese noh descends from shrine ritual; the powwow's grand entry is a ceremony before it is a contest. A few features follow. Ritual dance is usually participatory or semi-participatory: the congregation moves, or the dancer moves for them in a way that requires their presence. Its vocabulary is conserved — correctness matters because efficacy is believed to depend on it — and transmission is often restricted to initiates. And its relation to music is not aesthetic but functional: the drum calls the spirit, the repetition carries the dancer somewhere.

    Which raises the one claim about ritual dance that everyone repeats and that the commons has checked: that rhythm induces trance. The honest reading is split. That a steady beat entrains the nervous system is solid and replicated — EEG shows neural phase-locking to a musical beat and meter, enhanced at the beat frequency even when the sound doesn't emphasize it — and the finding that moderate syncopation is rated more pleasurable and more move-inducing than either none or maximum is equally solid. But the specific claim that drumming at particular tempi drives the brain into trance traces to a 1960s "auditory driving" hypothesis and a large anthropological literature, and the most direct recent test found entrainment correlating only weakly and inconsistently with altered-state reports — with a live competing hypothesis that predictable rhythm may cause the auditory cortex to suppress input rather than lock onto it. Real entrainment is not the same claim as induced altered consciousness; treat the second as unresolved. What the ritual traditions reliably achieve is sustained, entrained, communal engagement, and that is already a great deal.4

    Social: dance as participation¶

    Social dance is done with others, for its own sake and for the sake of the relationship — the circle and line dances of the Balkans, the Levant, and Greece (kolo, horo, dabke, hora); the partner dances of the European-derived world (waltz, polka, tango, swing, salsa); the club floor from Harlem to Chicago to Berlin; the wedding, the céilí, the quinceañera. It is participatory by definition: the point is that you dance, and a good social dance is one that the maximum number of people can join at the level they have. That shapes the movement profoundly. Social dances have low entry floors and high ceilings (anyone can waltz; few can waltz well); they run on shared, simple, cyclic structures (the eight-count, the compás, the four-on-the-floor) so that strangers can synchronize; and they are conserved by use, not by institutions — the steps survive because people keep doing them. Their relation to music is entrainment: the music exists so that bodies can share time. And they are the great reservoir from which every other function draws: ballet's court dances were social; the Lindy Hop was social before it was theatrical; breaking was social and competitive at once.

    Concert: dance as a work¶

    Concert dance is presentational: prepared by some, watched by others, and — the decisive feature — organized around the work, an authored object with a beginning, a shape, and an end, which can be good or bad, revived, interpreted, owned. This is ballet and modern and contemporary dance, the core of this library's subject and the tier it treats at full depth, and it is a small and recent slice of the world's dancing: a few centuries old, a few cultures' invention, exported everywhere through conservatories and touring. Its features invert social dance's. The floor is high (years of training), the vocabulary is elaborated for the eye rather than for participation, evaluation is aesthetic and critical, transmission runs through schools and companies, and the music relationship is composed — a designed choice, the choreomusicology chapter's whole subject. It is also the only function that produces choreographers in the authored sense, and therefore the only one whose logic a generative system straightforwardly reproduces. Everything in Part V and Part VII assumes the concert frame: a work, an author, an audience.

    Competitive: dance as contest¶

    Competitive dance is done to win, and the presence of judges changes the form as surely as the presence of a congregation does. The competitive world is large and old: the dance marathons and the Savoy Ballroom's contests of the 1920s and 30s, ballroom's DanceSport, the Irish feis, Highland games, powwow competitions with their categories and prize money, tap and jazz competitions, the television formats since the 2000s, and the street's battles — breaking's cyphers formalized into Battle of the Year (1990), Red Bull BC One (2004), and, in 2024, the Olympic Games. Three things happen to a dance when it is judged. Its vocabulary is codified so that it can be scored (the codified-vocabularies chapter on how street styles did this without an academy, and the anxiety when the Olympics imposed a rubric). Its movement is tuned for legibility at a distance under adverse conditions (the dressing-the-genre chapter on ballroom's floats and fringe). And its evaluation becomes an explicit selection pressure — which means that whatever the scoring rewards, the dance becomes, and whatever it cannot see, the dance loses. That is the oldest instance in the art form of the problem the evaluation thinking behind the synthetic dancer keeps meeting: a metric that is optimized stops measuring what it was meant to.

    Migration between functions¶

    Almost every dance worth knowing has moved. Bharatanatyam was a temple and court art (sadir, danced by hereditary devadasis) before Rukmini Devi Arundale and others reconstructed it as a concert form in the 1930s — ritual to concert, with the costume, the music, and the respectability transformed along the way. Hula went from sacred to tourist entertainment to the Hawaiian Renaissance's revival. The waltz went from village social dance to ballroom to the concert stage (and to competition). Breaking went from the Bronx block party to the battle to the Olympics in fifty years, and the culture's argument about what was lost at each step is the argument this section is about. The atlas pages track these migrations per tradition; the rule is that a dance's function is a fact about its present use, not its origin, and that a tradition can run several at once (a flamenco juerga, a tablao show, and a concert-hall ballet flamenco are three functions of one vocabulary).

    Why this matters for a system that makes dances¶

    A machine that generates movement is asked, implicitly, to serve one of these functions, and they want different things. A ritual or social generator would be judged by participation and entrainment — can people join, does the pulse hold — and has no use for novelty. A concert generator is judged by the work: shape, surprise, coherence, the things the gamut+rules architecture was built for. A competitive generator is judged by a rubric, and will find the rubric's holes. The literature on dance generation treats "dance" as one thing; this chapter's claim is that there is no such thing as good dance, only good for what, and that the first design decision — before the vocabulary, before the model — is which function the output serves. The rest of Part I walks the territory with that lens in hand.
